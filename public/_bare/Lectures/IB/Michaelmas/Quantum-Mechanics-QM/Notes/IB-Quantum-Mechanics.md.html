Schrodinger's equation open_in_new

Schordinger postulated that a particle can be described by a complex wavefunction $\psi(\v x,t):\R^3\times \R\to\C$ which satisfies

Schrodinger's equation

$$ \begin{align*} i\hbar \pdfd{\psi}{t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + U(\v x) \psi. \end{align*} $$

  • $\psi(\v x,t)$ is uniquely determined by the initial value $\psi(\v x,0)$.

  • The phase does not have physical meaning, i.e. $\psi(x)$ and $e^{i\phi} \psi(x)$ represents the same system.

  • Since the Schrodinger's equation is linear, if $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are solutions, then so is $\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2$. Also if $\psi_1$ and $\psi_2$ are normalisable, then so is $\alpha\psi_1 + \beta\psi_2$. (Derivation)

Conservation of probability in Schrodinger's equation open_in_new

Definition (probability density for a wavefunction) open_in_new

We interpret the probability density of finding a particle at a particular position $$ \begin{align*} \rho(x,t) = |\psi(\v x,t)|^2 \end{align*} $$ where $\psi$ is the wavefunction.

Therefore we require

Normalisation condition

$$ \begin{align*} \int_{\R^3} |\psi(\v x)|^2 \d V = 1. \end{align*} $$

Starting from Schrodinger's equation, we define the probability current $$ \begin{align*} \v j(\v x,t) = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}(\psi^\star \nabla\psi - \psi\nabla\psi^\star) \end{align*} $$ which satisfies the continuity equation $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{\rho}{t} + \nabla\cdot \v j = 0. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

By the divergence theorem, $$ \begin{align*} \dd{t}\int_{V} \rho(\v x,t) \d V = -\int_{\partial V} \v j\cdot \d \v S \implies \dd{t}\int_{\R^3} \rho(\v x,t) \d V = 0. \end{align*} $$ So if $\psi$ is normalised, all subsequent states are normalised.

Stationary states open_in_new

Time-independent Schrodinger's equation open_in_new

We perform a separation of variables $$ \begin{align*} \psi(\v x,t) = \chi(\v x) e^{-i\omega t}, \qquad E = \hbar \omega \end{align*} $$ to obtain the time-independent Schrodinger's equation $$ \begin{align*} -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \nabla^2 \chi(\v x) + U(\v x) \chi(\v x) = E\chi(\v x). \end{align*} $$

Free particle solution to the Schrodinger's equation open_in_new

Consider $U(\v x)=0$. Solving the time-independent Schrodinger's equation gives the plane wave solution $$ \begin{align*} \chi(\v x) = A\exp(i\v k\cdot \v x), \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2 |\v k|^2}{2m}. \end{align*} $$

However this is not normalisable.

Gaussian wave packet open_in_new

A superposition of the plane wave solution to the Schrodinger's equation can be normalisable: in 1D, take $$ \begin{align*} \psi(x,t) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathcal{A}(k) \exp i\big(kx - \frac{\hbar k^2}{2m} t\big) \d k, \qquad \mathcal{A}(k) = \exp\left(-\frac{\sigma}{2}(k-k_0)^2\right). \end{align*} $$

After normalising, we get $$ \begin{align*} \rho(x,t) = |\psi(x,t)|^2 = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma}{\pi}} \frac{1}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \hbar^2 t^2/m^2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\sigma(x - \hbar k_0 t/m)}{\sigma^2 + \hbar^2 t^2/m^2}\right). \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Beam interpretation of free particle wave function open_in_new

We can interpret the free particle wave function $$ \begin{align*} \psi_\v k(\v x,t) = A \exp i\big(\v k\cdot \v x - \frac{\hbar |\v k|^2}{2m}t\big) \end{align*} $$ as a beam of particles with

  • momenutm $\v p = \hbar \v k$;

  • energy $E = |\v p|^2/2m = \hbar\omega$;

  • average density of particles $\rho=|\psi|^2 = |A|^2$;

  • probability current $\v j=|A|^2 \times (\hbar \v k)/m =$ average density $\times$ velocity.

One dimensional bound state problems open_in_new

Consider a potential $U(x) \to U_\infty$ as $x\to \pm\infty$ and the energy $0<E<U_\infty$. A bound state of the system means that the particle is localised near the well.

This means the wavefunction needs to be normalisable, i.e. $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} |\chi(x)|^2 \d x < \infty$. So $|\chi(x)|^2$ must vanish faster than $1/x$ as $x\to \pm\infty$.

For large $|x|$, we can approximate $U(x) \approx U_\infty$. Solving the Schrodinger's equation yields normalisable solutions $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \chi(x) \sim A \exp(-\kappa x) &\text{as } x\to \infty, \\ \chi(x) \sim B \exp(\kappa x) &\text{as } x\to -\infty, \\ \end{cases} \qquad \text{where } \kappa &= \frac{\sqrt{2m(U_\infty - E)}}{\hbar} > 0. \end{align*} $$

Properties of stationary state solutions open_in_new

  • If $U(x)$ has a finite discontinuity, then $\chi$ and $\dfd{\chi}{x}$ is continuous. (Derivation)

  • If $U(x)$ has an infinite discontinuity, then $\chi(x)$ remains continuous but $\dfd{\chi}{x}$ becomes discontinuous.

  • If $U(x)$ is reflection invariant (i.e. $U(-x) = U(x)$) and the energy levels are non-degenerate (i.e. solutions of a particular energy are the same up to a phase $e^{i\theta}$), then $\chi$ is either even or odd. (Derivation)

Stationary state in 1D infinite potential well open_in_new

The potential $$ \begin{align*} U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, &0 \leq x \leq a \\ \infty, &\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ has stationary state solutions, for each $n=1,2,3,\ldots$, $$ \begin{align*} \chi(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin kx, \qquad k = \frac{n\pi}{a}, \qquad E = \frac{\hbar^2\pi^2}{2ma^2} n^2 \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

  • The ground state energy $E_1 = \hbar^2\pi^2/2ma^2$ is non-zero, which differs from classical mechanics. This is related to the uncertainty principle: if the particle is confined, it must have some momentum.

  • As $n\to \infty$, the probability density approaches a constant, which corresponds to the classical result. This is an example of the correspondence principle.

Stationary state in 1D finite potential well open_in_new

If $0\leq E< U_0$, the potential $$ \begin{align*} U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, &-a\leq x\leq a, \\ U_0, &\text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ gives rise to even and odd solutions $$ \begin{align*} \chi(x) &= \begin{cases} A\cos kx, &-a\leq x\leq a, \\ C\exp(-\kappa x), &x>a, \\ C\exp(\kappa x), &x<a, \end{cases} \\ \chi(x) &= \begin{cases} B\sin kx, &-a\leq x\leq a, \\ D\exp(-\kappa x), &x>a, \\ -D\exp(\kappa x), &x<a. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Scattering and tunneling open_in_new

Consider a potential barrier $U(x)$ that is localised near $x=0$ (i.e. $U(x)\to 0$ rapidly as $x\to \pm\infty$) with maximum barrier height $U_\text{max}$. A particle of mass $m$ and energy $E$ is incident on the barrier from the left.

In classical mechanics,

  • if $E>U_\text{max}$, the particle gets over the barrier and proceed to $x\to \infty$;

  • if $E<U_\text{max}$, the particle gets reflected back towards $x\to -\infty$.

In quantum mechanics, a wave packet centred at $x=x_0(t)\ll 0$ at initial time $t\ll 0$ with average momentum $\langle p\rangle>0$ evolves with the Schrodinger's equation to achieve a final state at $t\gg 0$. Define the reflection and transmission coefficients $$ \begin{align*} R = \lim_{t\to \infty} \int_{-\infty}^{0} |\psi(x,t)|^2 \d x, \qquad T = \lim_{t\to \infty} \int_{0}^{\infty} |\psi(x,t)|^2 \d x, \end{align*} $$ which should satisfy $R+T = 1$.

We will use non-normalisable stationary states and the beam interpretation to simplify calculations.

Scattering by a potential step open_in_new

Consider a plane wave with energy $E$ incident on a potential step $$ \begin{align*} U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, &x<0, \\ U_0, &x>0. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

  • For $E>U_0$, letting $k=\sqrt{2m E}/\hbar$ and $k' = \sqrt{2m(E-U_0)}/\hbar$, the waves are $$ \begin{align*} \text{incidence }\chi_i(x) &= A\exp(ikx), \\ \text{reflection }\chi_r(x) &= \frac{k-k'}{k+k'}A \exp(-ikx), \\ \text{transmission }\chi_t(x) &= \frac{2k}{k+k'}A \exp(ik'x). \end{align*} $$ We obtain the reflection and transmission coefficients $$ \begin{align*} R = \left(\frac{k-k'}{k+k'}\right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{4kk'}{(k+k')^2}. \end{align*} $$

  • For $E<U_0$, letting $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(U_0-E)}/\hbar$, the waves are $$ \begin{align*} \text{incidence } \chi_i(x) ={}& A\exp(ikx), \\ \text{reflection } \chi_r(x) ={}& \frac{k-i\kappa}{k+i\kappa} A\exp(-ikx), \\ \text{transmission } \chi_t(x) ={}& \frac{2k}{k+i\kappa} A\exp(-\kappa x). \end{align*} $$ And we get $$ \begin{align*} R = 1, \qquad T = 0. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Tunneling through a square barrier open_in_new

Consider a particle $E<U_0$ incident from $x\to -\infty$ on a square barrier $$ \begin{align*} U(x) = \begin{cases} 0, &x<0, \\ U_0, &0<x<a, \\ 0, &x>a. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ Letting $k=\sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$ and $\kappa = \sqrt{2m(U_0-E)}/\hbar$, the transmission coefficient is $$ \begin{align*} T = \frac{4k^2\kappa^2}{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2\sinh^2(\kappa a) + 4k^2\kappa^2}. \end{align*} $$

Generally we can approximate the transmission coefficient of a barrier of maximum height $U_0\gg E$ and width $a$ using $$ \begin{align*} T \approx \exp\left(-\frac{2a}{\hbar} \sqrt{2m \Delta U}\right). \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Stationary state in harmonic oscillator open_in_new

The potential $$ \begin{align*} U(x) = \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 \end{align*} $$ results in stationary states, for $n=0,1,2,\ldots$, $$ \begin{align*} \chi_n(x) \propto{}& H_n \left(\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}}x\right) \exp\left(-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2\right), \\ E_n ={}& \left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar \omega \end{align*} $$ where $H_n$ are the Hermite polynomials defined by the degree-$n$ polynomials whose coefficients satisfy $$ \begin{align*} a_{k+2} = \frac{2k - 2n}{(k+1)(k+2)}a_k. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Operators and observables open_in_new

An observable corresponds to an operator $\hat O$, which acts on a wavefunction $f: \R^3\to\C$ to give a new wavefunction $\hat O f : \R^3 \to \C$.

Here we only consider linear operators, i.e. those satisfying $\hat O(\alpha f + \beta g) = \alpha \hat O f + \beta \hat O g$.

Hermitian operators open_in_new

Definition (Hermitian operator in quantum mechanics) open_in_new

A linear operator $\hat O$ is said to be Hermitian (or self-adjoint) if for any normalisable functions $f, g: \R^3\to\C$, $$ \begin{align*} \int_{\R^3} f^\ast(\v x) \hat Og(\v x) \d V = \int_{\R^3} (\hat O f(\v x))^\ast g(\v x) \d V. \end{align*} $$

  • If $\hat O_1$ and $\hat O_2$ are Hermitian, so are $\alpha\hat O_1 + \beta\hat O_2$ and $\hat O_1^2$.

  • Eigenvalues of $\hat O$ are real. (Derivation)

  • Eigenfunctions of different eigenvalues are orthogonal, i.e. $\int_{\R^3} u_1^\ast(\v x) u_2(\v x) \d V = 0$. (Derivation)

  • The set of eigenfunctions $\set{u_n(\v x)}$ are complete, i.e. for any normalisable wavefunction $\psi(\v x)$, there exist complex constants $\set{a_n}$ such that $$ \begin{align*} \psi(\v x) = \sum_n a_n u_n(\v x). \end{align*} $$

  • If $u_n$ are normalised, then coefficients $a_n$ are given by $$ \begin{align*} a_n = \int_{\R^3} u_n^\ast(\v x) \psi(\v x) \d V. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

  • If furthermore $\psi$ is normalised, then $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{n} |a_n|^2 = 1. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Examples of operators open_in_new

Momentum operator open_in_new

The momentum operator is defined as the vector of operators $\vhat p = (\hat p_1, \hat p_2, \hat p_3)$, given by $$ \begin{align*} \hat p_j = -i\hbar \pdd{x_j}, \qquad \vhat p = -i\hbar \nabla. \end{align*} $$

  • $\vhat p$ is Hermitian, and so are $\hat p_i$. (Derivation)

The eigenfunction of definite momentum $\v p$ is $$ \begin{align*} \psi_\v p(\v x) = \exp\left(i \frac{\v p}{\hbar}\cdot \v x\right), \end{align*} $$

  • This is the plane-wave solution with wave vector $\v k = \v p/\hbar$ (de Broglie relation).

  • This is non-normalisable.

  • Completeness of $\set{\psi_\v p(\v x)}$ is equivalent to the existence of the Fourier transform of $\psi(\v x)$.

  • $\vhat p$ has a continuous spectrum, because the eigenvalues $\v p$ are continuous.

Position operator open_in_new

The position operator is a vector of operators $\vhat x = (\hat x_1, \hat x_2, \hat x_3)$ given by $$ \begin{align*} \hat x_i f(\v x) = x_i f(\v x), \qquad \vhat x f(\v x) = \v x f(\v x). \end{align*} $$

  • For any function $U$, the operator $U(\vhat x)$ behaves as $U(\vhat x) f(\v x) = U(\v x) f(\v x)$.

  • $\vhat x$ and $U(\vhat x)$ are Hermitian.

The eigenfunction in one dimension of definite position $x_0$ is $$ \begin{align*} \psi_{x_0}(x) = \delta(x - x_0). \end{align*} $$ And in three dimensions, $$ \begin{align*} \psi_\v X(\v x) = \delta^{(3)} (\v x - \v X) = \delta(x_1 - X_1) \delta(x_2 - X_2) \delta(x_3 - X_3). \end{align*} $$

  • These are non-normalisable, since $\int \delta^2(x-x_0)\d x$ does not exist.

Hamiltonian operator open_in_new

The Hamiltonian operator is defined as $$ \begin{align*} \hat H = - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2 + U(\v x). \end{align*} $$

  • $\hat H$ $= (\vhat p\cdot \vhat p)/2m + U(\v x)$, which is the same in classical mechanics.

  • $\hat H$ is Hermitian, because $\vhat p$ and $\vhat x$ are Hermitian.

Stationary states are eigenfunction of $\hat H$, i.e. $\hat H \psi = E\psi$.

Angular momentum operator open_in_new

The angular momentum operator $\vhat L = (\hat L_1, \hat L_2, \hat L_3)$ is given by $$ \begin{align*} \vhat L ={}& \vhat x\times \vhat p = -i\hbar \v x\times \nabla, \\ \hat L_i ={}& -i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} x_j \pdd{x_k}. \end{align*} $$ The total angular momentum operator is $$ \begin{align*} \hat L^2 = \hat L_1^2 + \hat L_2^2 + \hat L_3^2. \end{align*} $$

Postulates of quantum mechanics open_in_new

Description of the state of a system:

  1. Every state of the system at a given time is described by a normalisable wavefunction $\psi(\v x)$.

Measurement on a system:

  1. Each observable quantity $O$ corresponds to a Hermitian operator $\hat O$.

  2. Suppose $\hat O$ have a discrete and non-degenerate spectrum of eigenvalues $\set{\lambda_n}$ and corresponding normalised eigenfunction $\set{u_n(\v x)}$. For a system described by a wavefunction $\psi(\v x)$ with an eigenfunction expansion $$ \begin{align*} \psi(\v x) = \sum_{n} a_n u_n(\v x), \end{align*} $$ if a measurement of $O$ is carried out, then the outcome is $\lambda_n$ with probability $|a_n|^2$.

  3. Immediately after the measurement, the system is in the state with normalised wavefunction $u_n(\v x)$.

Time evolution of a system:

  1. Subsequent time evolution of the wavefunction is governed by the Schrodinger equation $$ \begin{align*} i\hbar \pdfd{\psi}{t} = \hat H(t) \psi \end{align*} $$ where $\hat H$ is the Hamiltonian.

Proposition (expectation value of measurement) open_in_new

Following the notation here, the expectation value of an observable $O$ in a state $\psi$ is $$ \begin{align*} \langle O\rangle_\psi := \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \lambda_n|a_n|^2 = \int_{\R^3} \psi^\ast(\v x) \,\hat O \psi(\v x) \d V. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (uncertainty of measurement) open_in_new

The uncertainty $\Delta_\psi O$ in the measurement of an observable $O$ in the state $\psi$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} \Delta_\psi O = \sqrt{\big\langle (O - \langle O\rangle_\psi)^2\big\rangle_{\psi}} = \sqrt{\langle O^2\rangle_\psi - \langle O\rangle_\psi^2}. \end{align*} $$

Commutation relations of operators open_in_new

Definition (commutator) open_in_new

Define the commutator of operators $\hat A$ and $\hat B$ as the operator $[\hat A, \hat B] = \hat A \hat B - \hat B \hat A$.

  • $[\hat A, \hat B] = -[\hat B, \hat A]$.

  • $[\hat A, \hat B^2] = [\hat A, \hat B] \hat B + \hat B[\hat A, \hat B]$.

  • Erhenfest theorem: $\dd{t}\langle \hat O\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar} \langle [\hat O, \hat H]\rangle + \langle \pdfd{\hat O}{t}\rangle$. (Proof)

Proposition (commutative operators share eigenfunctions) open_in_new

Let $\hat O_1$ and $\hat O_2$ be Hermitian operators.

If $\hat O_1$ and $\hat O_2$ have non-degenerate spectra, then they commute iff any eigenfunction of $\hat O_1$ is also an eigenfunction of $\hat O_2$.

Proof

Proposition (commutation relations) open_in_new

  • $[\hat x_i, \hat x_j] = 0$

  • $[\hat p_i, \hat p_j] = 0$

  • $[\hat x_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar \delta_{ij}$

  • $[\hat L_i, \hat L_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} \hat L_k$

  • $[\hat L_i, \hat L^2] = 0$

  • $[\hat L_i, \hat x_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} \hat x_k$

  • $[\hat L_i, \hat p_j] = i\hbar \epsilon_{ijk} \hat p_k$

Proof

Theorem (Heisenberg uncertainty relation) open_in_new

For any normalisable wavefunction $\psi$, $$ \begin{align*} \Delta_\psi x \cdot \Delta_\psi p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}. \end{align*} $$

In general, for any observables $A$ and $B$, $$ \begin{align*} \Delta_\psi A \cdot \Delta_\psi B \geq \frac{1}{2}\big|\langle [\hat A, \hat B]\rangle_\psi\big|. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • A Gaussian wavefunction $\psi(x) = (a/\pi)^{1/4} \exp(-ax^2/2)$ saturates the inequality i.e. $\Delta_\psi x \cdot \Delta_\psi p$ and therefore represents the state of minimum uncertainty.

Spherically symmetric potential open_in_new

Spherically symmetric stationary state open_in_new

In spherical polars, the time-independent Schrodinger's equation is $$ \begin{align*} E\psi = U(\v x) \psi - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\Big(\frac{1}{r} \pdd[2]{r}\big(r\psi\big) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta} \pdd{\theta}\big(\sin\theta \pdfd{\psi}{\theta}\big) + \frac{1}{r^2 \sin^2\theta} \pdfd[2]{\psi}{\phi}\Big). \end{align*} $$

For a spherically symmetric potential $U(\v x) = U(r)$, we look for a spherically symmetric stationary state $\psi(\v x) = \psi(r)$. Then $$ \begin{cases} Er\psi = U(r) r\psi - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\dd[2]{r}(r\psi), \\ \int_{\R^3} |\psi(\v x)|^2 \d V < \infty \iff \int_{0}^{\infty} |\psi(r)|^2 r^2 \d r < \infty. \end{cases} $$

Using $\sigma(r) = r\psi(r)$, this is equivalent to solving $$ \begin{cases} E\sigma = U(r) \sigma - \frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \dfd[2]{\sigma}{r}, \\ \sigma(0) = 0, \\ \int_{0}^{\infty} |\sigma(r)|^2 \d r <\infty, \end{cases} $$ which is the Schrodinger equation on the half line $r>0$. Equivalently, this is the odd-parity solution of the Schrodinger equation with the symmetric potential $U(-r) = U(r)$.

Maximal set of commuting observables open_in_new

If the potential is spherically symmetric ($U(\v x) = U(r)$), then $$ \begin{align*} \set{\hat H, \hat L^2, \hat L_3} \end{align*} $$ form a set of mutually commuting operators. (Derivation)

In fact this set is maximal, meaning we cannot construct another independent operator (other than the unit operator) that commutes with $\hat H$, $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_3$.

Spherical harmonics open_in_new

In spherical polars, $$ \begin{align*} \hat L^2 ={}& -\frac{\hbar^2}{\sin^2 \theta} \left(\sin\theta \pdd{\theta}\left(\sin\theta \pdd{\theta}\right) + \pdd[2]{\phi}\right), \\ \hat L_1 ={}& i\hbar\left(\sin\phi \pdd{\theta} + \cos\phi \cot\theta \pdd{\phi}\right), \\ \hat L_2 ={}& i\hbar\left(-\cos\phi \pdd{\theta} + \sin\phi \cot\theta \pdd{\phi}\right), \\ \hat L_3 ={}& -i\hbar \pdd{\phi}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

We look for a simultaneous eigenfunction of $\hat L^2$ and $\hat L_3$ of the form $\psi(\theta, \phi) = Y(\theta) Z(\phi)$. The solutions are $$ \begin{align*} Y_{l,m}(\theta, \phi) ={}& P_{l,m}(\cos\theta) \exp(im\phi), \\ ={}& \sin^{|m|}(\theta) \left(\dd[|m|]{(\cos\theta)} P_l(\cos\theta)\right) \exp(im\phi),& l,m\in \Z, \quad -l\leq m\leq l \end{align*} $$ where $P_{l,m}$ are the associated Legendre functions and $P_l$ is the Legendre polynomial, and the eigenvalues are $$ \begin{align*} \hat L^2 Y_{l,m} ={}& l(l+1) \hbar^2 Y_{l,m}, &&l=0,1,2,\ldots\\ \hat L_3 Y_{l,m} ={}& m\hbar Y_{l,m}, && m=-l,-l+1,\ldots,l. \end{align*} $$ $l$ is called the total angular momentum quantum number and $m$ is called the azimuthal quantum number. (Derivation)

Hydrogen atom open_in_new

For the hydrogen atom, we have the Coulomb potential $$ \begin{align*} U(\v x) = -\frac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}. \end{align*} $$ We seek for a simultaneous eigenstate of $\set{\hat H, \hat L^2, \hat L_3}$ in the form $\psi(\v x) = \chi(r)\, Y_{l,m}(\theta,\phi)$, The solutions are $$ \begin{align*} \psi_{N,l,m}(r,\theta,\phi) = \xi^l L_{N,l}(\xi) \exp(-\xi) \, Y_{l,m}(\theta, \phi) \end{align*} $$ where $L_{N,l}$ is the generalised Laguerre polynomial and $\xi = (e^2 m_e r)/(4N\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar^2)$, and the eigenvalues are $$ \begin{align*} E ={}& -\frac{e^4 m_e}{32\pi^2 \epsilon_0^2 \hbar^2} \frac{1}{N^2}, &&N=1,2,3,\ldots, \\ L^2 ={}& l(l+1)\hbar^2, &&l = 0,1,,\ldots,N-1, \\ L_3 ={}& m\hbar, &&m=-l, -l+1, \ldots, l. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

In particular, the ground state wavefunction $N=1, l=m=0$ are given by $$ \begin{align*} \psi(r) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{\pi r_B^3}} e^{-r/r_B}, \qquad \text{ where } r_B = \frac{4\pi\epsilon_0 \hbar^2}{e^2m_e}. \end{align*} $$ $r_B$ is the Bohr radius, the radius of the lowest orbit in a Bohr atom.
