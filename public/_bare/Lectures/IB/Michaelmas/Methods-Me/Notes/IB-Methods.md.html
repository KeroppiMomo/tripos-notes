Motivation for the Fourier series

Definition (complex Fourier series) open_in_new

For an $L$-periodic function $f: \R\to\C$, define the complex Fourier series $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{n\in\Z} \hat f_n e^{2\pi i\theta/L} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} \hat f_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) e^{-2\pi i\theta/L} \d \theta \end{align*} $$ and we call $\set{\hat f_n}$ the complex Fourier coefficients of $f$.

We write $$ \begin{align*} f(\theta) \sim \sum_{n\in\Z} \hat f_n e^{2\pi in\theta/L} \end{align*} $$ to mean the series on the right is the complex Fourier series of the $L$-periodic function on the left.

Definition (Fourier series) open_in_new

For an $L$-periodic function $f: \R\to\C$, define the Fourier series $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2} a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(a_n\cos \frac{2\pi n \theta}{L} + b_n \sin\frac{2\pi n\theta}{L}\right) \end{align*} $$ where, for each $n\in\N$, $$ \begin{align*} a_n &= \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) \cos \frac{2\pi n \theta}{L} \d \theta \\ b_n &= \frac{2}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) \sin \frac{2\pi n \theta}{L} \d \theta \end{align*} $$ and we call $\set{a_n, b_n}_{n=1}^\infty$ the Fourier coefficients of $f$.

Derivation (Fourier series from complex Fourier series)

  • If $f$ is even, then all $b_n = 0$.

  • If $f$ is odd, then all $a_n = 0$. (Derivation)

Convergence of Fourier series open_in_new

Definition (partial Fourier series) open_in_new

For an $L$-periodic function $f: \R\to\C$, we define the partial Fourier series $S_N f$ by $$ \begin{align*} (S_N f)(\theta) = \sum_{|n|\leq N} \hat f_n e^{2\pi i n\theta/L} = \frac{1}{2} a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{N} \left(a_n \cos \frac{2\pi n\theta}{L} + b_n \sin \frac{2\pi n \theta}{L}\right). \end{align*} $$

Proposition (convergence of Fourier series) open_in_new

If an $L$-periodic function $f: \R\to\C$ is of bounded variation, i.e.

  1. $f$ is continuous on $[0,L)$ away from a finite number of jump discontinuities, and

  2. $f$ has finitely many maxima/minima on $[0,L)$,

then for each $\theta\in[0,L)$, $$ \begin{align*} \frac{f(\theta_+) + f(\theta_-)}{2} = \lim_{N\to\infty} (S_N f)(\theta) = \sum_n \hat f_n e^{2\pi i n\theta/L} \end{align*} $$ where $f(\theta_{\pm}) = \lim_{\epsilon\searrow 0} f(\theta\pm\epsilon)$.

This means the Fourier series converges to the value of $f$ at points of continuity, and converges to the average value of $f$ at points of discontinuity.

Definition (cosine and sine series) open_in_new

Given a function $f: [0,L) \to \C$, define its cosine series as $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2} A_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} A_n \cos \frac{n\pi\theta}{L} \end{align*} $$ and sine series as $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} B_n \sin \frac{n\pi\theta}{L} \end{align*} $$ where the coefficients $\set{A_n}, \set{B_n}$ are given by $$ \begin{align*} A_n &= \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) \cos \frac{n\pi\theta}{L} \d \theta, \\ B_n &= \frac{2}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) \sin \frac{n\pi\theta}{L} \d \theta. \end{align*} $$

The cosine/sine series is the Fourier series of the $2L$-periodic even/odd extension of $f$. (Derivation)

Proposition (decay of Fourier coefficients) open_in_new

Let $f:\R\to\C$ be $L$-periodic.

  • If $f$ is integrable on $[0,L]$, then $\hat f_n$ $\to 0$ as $|n|\to\infty$.

  • If $f$ is $C^k$ (i.e. the $l$-derivative $f^{(l)}: \R\to\C$ are continuous for $l=0,1,\ldots,k$), then $|\hat f_n| =$ $o$$(1/n^k)$.

Proof

The more regular a function is, the more quickly the Fourier coefficients decay.

Proposition (Fourier coefficients of the derivative) open_in_new

Let $f\in \R\to\C$ be $L$-periodic. Suppose $f$ restricted to $(0,L)$ is continuously differentiable and the derivative is bounded.

Then the complex Fourier coefficients of $g:=f'$ are $$ \begin{align*} \hat g_n = \frac{f(L_-) - f(0_+)}{L} + \frac{2\pi i n}{L} \hat f_n. \end{align*} $$ In particular, if $f$ is continuous, then $\hat g_n = (2\pi i n/L) \hat f_n$, which is what you obtain from term-wise differentiation of the Fourier series.

Theorem (Parseval's theorem) open_in_new

Suppose $f,g: \R\to\C$ are $L$-periodic and square integrable with Fourier series $$ \begin{align*} f(\theta) = \sum_n \hat f_n e^{2\pi in\theta/L}, \qquad g(\theta) = \sum_n \hat g_m e^{2\pi im\theta/L}. \end{align*} $$

Then $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{L}\int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) \,\overline{g(\theta)} \d \theta = \sum_n \hat f_n \, \overline{\hat g_n}. \end{align*} $$ And in particular, $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{L} \int_{0}^{L} |f|^2 \d \theta = \sum_n |\hat f_n|^2. \end{align*} $$

Sturm-Liouville theory open_in_new

Definition (weighted inner product) open_in_new

In the vector space $V$ of continuous functions $f: [a,b]\to \C$, we can equip $V$ with an inner product $$ \begin{align*} \langle f,g\rangle_w = \int_{a}^{b} f(x)\overline{g(x)} w(x) \d x \end{align*} $$ where $w: (a,b)\to \R_{>0}$, the weight function, is such that $\int_{a}^{b} w(x) \d x$ exists.

We can also define the norm $$ \begin{align*} \|f\|_w = \sqrt{\langle f,f\rangle_w}. \end{align*} $$

Definition (self-adjoint differential operator) open_in_new

A differential operator $L$ is said to be self-adjoint on $(V, \langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle_w)$ if $$ \begin{align*} \langle Ly_1, y_2\rangle_w &= \langle y_1, Ly_2\rangle_w &&\text{for all }y_1,y_2\in V. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (properties of self-adjoint differential operator) open_in_new

Let $L$ be a self-adjoint differential operator in $(V, \langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_w)$.

  1. The eigenvalues of $L$ are real.

  2. The eigenfunctions with different eigenvalues are orthogonal ($f$ and $g$ are orthogonal iff $\langle f,g\rangle_w = 0$).

  3. The set of eigenfucntions is countable and complete, i.e. the eigenfunctions form a list $\set{y_n}_{n=1}^\infty$, and for each $f\in V$, we can write $$ \begin{align*} f(x) &= \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \hat f_n y_n(x) &&\text{where} & \hat f_n = \frac{\langle f, y_n\rangle_w}{\langle y_n, y_n\rangle_w}. \end{align*} $$ We call $\set{\hat f_n}$ the generalised Fourier coefficients.

Proof

This is analogous to a Hermitian matrix.

Definition (Sturm-Liouville operator) open_in_new

A Sturm-Liouville operator on $(a,b)$ has the form $$ \begin{align*} L = \frac{1}{w} \left(-\dd{x}\left(p \dd{x}\right) + q\right) \end{align*} $$ where $p,q,w$ are real-valued functions and $p,w > 0$ on $(a,b)$. $w$ is called the weight function.

Lemma (Sturm-Liouville operator and Wronskian) open_in_new

Let $L$ be a Sturm-Liouville operator on $(a,b)$ defined by functions $p,q,w$, and $y_1,y_2\in C^2[a,b]$. We have the identity $$ \begin{align*} y_2 Ly_1 - y_1 L y_2 = \frac{1}{w}\dd{x}\big(p W(y_1,y_2)\big) \end{align*} $$ where $W(y_1,y_2) = y_1 y_2' - y_1' y_2$ is the Wronskian.

Proof

Definition (singular endpoint of Sturm-Liouville operator) open_in_new

Let $L$ be a Sturm-Liouville operator on $(a,b)$ defined by functions $p,q,w$.

An endpoint $c\in\set{a,b}$ is said to be singular if $p(c) = 0$, or non-singular if otherwise.

Definition (real and homogenous boundary condition) open_in_new

A boundary condition at an endpoint $c\in\set{a,b}$ is said to be real and homogenous if it is of the form $$ \begin{align*} \alpha_c y(c) + \beta_cy'(c) = 0 \end{align*} $$ where $\alpha_c, \beta_c \in \R$ and $\alpha_c^2 + \beta_c^2 \neq 0$.

Proposition (condition where Sturm-Liouville operator is self-adjoint) open_in_new

Let $L$ be a Sturm-Liouville operator on $(a,b)$ defined by functions $p,q,w$, and $y_1,y_2\in C^2[a,b]$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \langle Ly_1, y_2\rangle_w - \langle y_1, Ly_2\rangle_w = \big[p(x) W(y_1, \overline{y_2})(x)\big]_{x=a}^{x=b} \end{align*} $$ where $W(y_1,\overline{y_2})$ is the Wronskian.

Let $V=\{ y\in C^2[a,b]\ |\ y$ satisfies real and homogenous boundary conditions at each non-singular endpoint$\}$. Then $L$ is self-adjoint with respect to $(V,$ $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_w$$)$.

Legendre equation open_in_new

The eigenvalue problem $$ \begin{align*} -\dd{x}\left((1-x^2) \dfd{y}{x}\right) = \lambda y, \qquad -1<x<1 \end{align*} $$ has eigenvalues and eigenfunctions and eigenfunctions ($n=0,1,2,\ldots$) $$ \begin{align*} \lambda_n &= n(n+1) \\ y_n &= \begin{cases} a_0 + a_2 x^2 + a_4 x^4 + \ldots + a_{2N} x^{2N}, &n = 2N \text{ is even},\\ a_1x + a_3 x^3 + a_5 x^5 + \ldots + a_{2N+1} x^{2N+1}, &n=2N + 1 \text{ is odd}, \end{cases} \\ \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} a_{k+2} = \frac{k(k+1) - \lambda}{(k+1)(k+2)} a_k. \end{align*} $$

The Legendre polynomials $P_n$ are the eigenfunctions normalised so that $y(1)=1$.

  • Rodrigue's formula: $$ \begin{align*} P_n(x) = \frac{1}{2^n n!} \dd[n]{x}(x^2 - 1)^n. \end{align*} $$

  • $P_n$ are orthogonal: $$ \begin{align*} \int_{-1}^{+1} P_n(x) P_m(x) \d x = \frac{2}{2n+1} \delta_{nm}. \end{align*} $$

  • $P_n$ are complete.

Bessel's equation open_in_new

The eigenvalue problem for some fixed $m = 0,1,2,\ldots$ $$ \begin{align*} -\dd{r}\left(r \dfd{y}{r}\right) + \frac{m^2}{r} y &= \lambda r y, &&0<r<1, \\ y(1) &= 0 \end{align*} $$ gives rise under the change of variable $z=r\sqrt{\lambda}$ to Bessel's equation of order $m$ $$ \begin{align*} z^2 R'' + zR' + (z^2 - m^2) R &= 0, &&0 < z < \sqrt{\lambda}, \\ R(\sqrt{\lambda}) &= 0. \end{align*} $$

The only solution which does not blow up as $z\to 0$ is the $m$-th order Bessel function $$ \begin{align*} J_m(z) = \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^m \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{(-1)^n}{n!(n+m)!} \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2n} \end{align*} $$ which has an asymptotic approximation $$ \begin{align*} J_m(z) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi z}} \cos\left(z - \frac{m\pi}{2} - \frac{\pi}{4}\right) + O\left(\frac{1}{z^{3/2}}\right) \qquad \text{as }z\to\infty. \end{align*} $$

$J_m(z)$ has infinitely many zeros for $z\in [0,\infty)$, say $j_{m1}, j_{m2}, j_{m3}, \ldots$.

The eigenfunction-eigenvalue pairs are $$ \begin{align*} y_k(r) = J_m(j_{mk} r), \quad \lambda_k = j_{mk}^2 \end{align*} $$ and they are orthogonal: $$ \begin{align*} \int_{0}^{1} J_m(j_{mk} r) J_m(j_{ml} r) r \d r = \frac{\big(J'_m(j_{mk})\big)^2}{2} \delta_{kl}. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Inhomogenous eigenvalue problem open_in_new

Consider an inhomogenous problem $$ \begin{align*} Ly = f, \qquad a<x<b \end{align*} $$ where $L$ is a Sturm-Liouville operator and $y,f\in V$ defined here. Since we can absorb the weight function into $f$, take $w=1$ WLOG.

Let $\set{Y_k}$ be the normalised eigenfunctions of $L$, with $LY_k = \lambda_k Y_k$ and $\|Y_k\| = 1$. Assume all $\lambda_k\neq 0$. Then by completeness, let $$ \begin{align*} f = \sum_k \hat f_k Y_k, \qquad y = \sum_k \hat y_k Y_k \end{align*} $$ and then $$ \begin{align*} Ly = f \implies \sum_k (\lambda_k \hat y_k - \hat f_k) Y_k = 0 \implies \hat y_k = \frac{\hat f_k}{\lambda_k} \end{align*} $$ so $$ \begin{align*} y(x) = \sum_{k} \frac{\langle f,Y_k\rangle}{\lambda_k} Y_k(x) = \int_{a}^{b} \left(\sum_{k} \frac{Y_k(x) Y_k(y)}{\lambda_k}\right) f(y) \d y := \int_{a}^{b} G(x,y) f(y) \d y \end{align*} $$ where $G(x,y)$ is called Green's function.

Linear PDEs and separation of variables open_in_new

We are interested in

Boundary value problem

$$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} P\psi(\v x) = 0, & \v x\in\Omega, \\ \text{boundary conditions}, &\v x\in \partial \Omega \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

and

Initial boundary value problem

$$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} Q\psi(\v x,t) = 0, & (\v x,t)\in\Omega\times (0,\infty), \\ \text{initial conditions}, &(\v x,t)\in \Omega\times\set{0}, \\ \text{boundary conditions}, &(\v x,t)\in \partial \Omega\times(0,\infty) \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

where $P$ and $Q$ are (partial) linear differential operators, and $\Omega$ is an open bounded subset of $\R^n$, $n=1,2,3$.

Using superposition to solve boundary value problems open_in_new

  1. For boundary value problems, we can assume all boundary conditions = 0 on all but one endpoint/edge/face, and superpose solutions together to satsify all boundary conditions.

  2. For initial boundary value problems, we can first assume all boundary conditions = 0, then assume instead all initial conditions = 0, and then superpose solutions.

Laplace's equation

Wave reflection and transmission open_in_new

For an infinite-long string with tension $\tau$ and densities $\mu_\pm$ on $\pm x>0$, the wave equations for both regions are $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} y_{tt} - c_-^2 y_{xx} = 0, &x<0, \\ y_{tt} - c_+^2 y_{xx} = 0, &x>0 \\ \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $c_\pm^2 = \tau/\mu_{\pm}$.

Assume

  • the string does not break, i.e. $y$ is continuous at $x=0$; and

  • no mass at $x=0$, i.e. $y_x$ is continuous at $x=0$.

For an incident wave from $x\to-\infty$ $$ \begin{align*} \cos\Big(\omega \big(t - \frac{x}{c_-}\big)\Big), \qquad x<0, \end{align*} $$ we obtain a reflected wave $$ \begin{align*} R \cos\Big(\omega \big(t + \frac{x}{c_-}\big)\Big) \quad \text{where } R= \frac{\mu_- - \mu_+}{\mu_- + \mu_+}, \qquad x<0 \end{align*} $$ and a transmitted wave $$ \begin{align*} T \cos\Big(\omega \big(t - \frac{x}{c_+}\big)\Big) \quad \text{where } T = \frac{2\mu_-}{\mu_- + \mu_+}, \qquad x>0. \end{align*} $$

Heat equation open_in_new

We want to solve $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{\phi}{t} = \kappa \Delta\phi \end{align*} $$ where $\kappa>0$ is a constant called the thermal diffusivity. This models the transfer of heat in conductive bodies.

Using separation of variables $\phi = T(t) \psi(\v x)$, we can turn the initial boundary value problem $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \phi_t = \kappa \Delta\phi, &\Omega \times (0,\infty), \\ \phi = f, &\Omega\times\set{0}, \\ \phi = 0, &\partial\Omega\times (0,\infty) \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ into $$ \begin{align*} T(t) = Ae^{-\mu \kappa t}, \quad \begin{cases} \Delta\psi + \mu\psi = 0, &\Omega, \\ \psi = 0, &\partial \Omega. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

Heat conduction in a square open_in_new

For a square $(0,L)\times(0,L)$, we have a separation of variables solution $$ \begin{align*} \phi(x,y,t) = \sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty} A_{m,n} e^{-\kappa \mu_{m,n}t} \sin \frac{n\pi x}{L} \sin \frac{m\pi y}{L} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} \mu_{m,n} ={}& \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{m\pi}{L}\right)^2, \\ A_{m,n} ={}& \frac{4}{L^2} \int_{0}^{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(x,y) \sin \frac{n\pi x}{L} \sin \frac{m\pi y}{L} \d x \d y. \end{align*} $$

Heat conduction on a cylindrical rod open_in_new

For a cylindrical rod of radius 1 and length $L$, the domain is $\Omega=[0,1) \times [0,2\pi) \times (0,L)$ in cylindrical polars $(\rho,\varphi,z)$. Assuming $f=f(\rho,z)$ is independent of $\varphi$, we can assume $\phi = \phi(\rho,z,t)$ is independent of $\varphi$ as well.

We have a separation of variables solution $$ \begin{align*} \phi(\rho, z, t) = \sum_{k,n=1}^{\infty} A_{k,n} e^{-\kappa\mu_{k,n} t} J_0(j_{0k}\, \rho) \sin \frac{n\pi z}{L} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} \mu_{kn} ={}& \left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2 + {j_{0k}}^2, \\ A_{kn} ={}& \frac{4}{L\,J_0'(j_{0k})^2} \int_{0}^{1} \left(\int_{0}^{L} f(\rho,z) \sin \frac{n\pi z}{L} \d z\right) J_0(j_{0k}\, \rho)\,\rho \d \rho. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Proposition (energy dissipation in heat equation) open_in_new

Let $\phi(\v x,t)$ be a solution to the initial value problem $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \phi_t = \kappa \Delta\phi, &\Omega \times (0,\infty), \\ \phi = f, &\Omega\times\set{0}, \\ \phi = 0, &\partial\Omega\times (0,\infty). \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

The energy, defined as $$ \begin{align*} Q(t) = \frac{1}{2} \int_{\Omega} \phi^2 \d V, \end{align*} $$ is decreasing with time, i.e. $Q'(t) \leq 0$. In particular $$ \begin{align*} Q(t) \leq Q(0) = \frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega} f^2 \d V. \end{align*} $$

Definition (Green's function) open_in_new

The Green's function $G(x;\xi)$ for a linear operator $L$ on $a<x<b$ is the solution, for each fixed $\xi\in (a,b)$, to $$ \begin{align*} L[G(x;\xi)] = \delta(x-\xi), \quad a<x<b \end{align*} $$ under some appropriate boundary conditions.

Using Green's functions to solve inhomogenous problems open_in_new

For some linear differential operator $L$, the problem $$ \begin{gather*} \begin{cases} Ly = f, &a<x<b, \\ y(a) = 0, \\ y(b) = 0, \end{cases} \end{gather*} $$ has a solution $$ \begin{align*} y(x) = \int_{a}^{b} G(x;\xi) f(\xi) \d \xi \end{align*} $$ where $G(x;\xi)$ is the Green's function for $L$ on $a<x<b$ with the Dirichlet boundary conditions.

(Derivation)

Jump conditions of Green's functions for second order linear operators open_in_new

Consider the Green's function $G(x;\xi)$ for a second order linear differential operator $$ \begin{align*} L = \alpha \dd[2]{x} + \beta \dd{x} + \gamma \end{align*} $$ where $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ are smooth and $\alpha(x)\neq 0$ on $a<x<b$.

$\dfd{G}{x}$ looks like the Heaviside function $H(x-\xi)$ and has a jump discontinuity $$ \begin{align*} \dfd{G}{x}(\xi^+ ; \xi) - \dfd{G}{x}(\xi^-; \xi) = \frac{1}{\alpha(\xi)}. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Proposition (Green's function from linearly independent solutions) open_in_new

Let $L = \alpha \dd[2]{x} + \beta \dd{x} + \gamma$ be a second-order linear differential operator with $\alpha(x)\neq 0$ on $(a,b)$. Suppose $y_1$ and $y_2$ be linearly independent solutions to $Ly=0$ such that $y_1(a) = 0$ and $y_2(b)=0$.

Then the Dirichlet Green's function for $L$ on $(a,b)$ is $$ \begin{align*} G(x;\xi) = \frac{1}{\alpha(\xi) W(y_1,y_2)(\xi)} \begin{cases} y_1(x)y_2(\xi), &a<x<\xi, \\ y_1(\xi) y_2(x), &\xi<x<b \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $W(y_1,y_2)=y_1y_2' - y_1'y_2$ is the Wronskain.

Proof

  • Therefore we can solve an inhomogenous problem $$ \begin{align*} y(x) = \int_{a}^{x} \frac{y_1(\xi) y_2(x) f(\xi)}{\alpha(\xi) W(y_1,y_2)(\xi)} \d \xi + \int_{x}^{b} \frac{y_1(x)y_2(\xi)f(\xi)}{\alpha(\xi) W(y_1,y_2)(\xi)} \d \xi. \end{align*} $$

  • For $L$ being Sturm-Liouville:

    • We can absorb $w$ into $f$ so WLOG assume $w=1$.

    • We use the identity $(pW(y_1,y_2))' = y_2 L y_1 - y_1 L y_2$ and $Ly_1 = 0 = Ly_2$ to see $$ \begin{align*} y(x) = -\frac{1}{p(c) W(y_1,y_2)(c)} \left(y_2(x) \int_{a}^{x} y_1(\xi)f(\xi) \d \xi + y_1(x) \int_{x}^{b} y_2(\xi) f(\xi) \d \xi\right) \end{align*} $$ for any $c\in (a,b)$.

Green's function for initial value problems open_in_new

For the initial value problem $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} Ly = f, &t>0, \\ y(0) = y'(0) = 0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $L = \alpha(t) \dd[2]{t} + \beta(t) \dd{t} + \gamma(t)$ and $\alpha(t) \neq 0$, the Green's function $G(t;\tau)$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} L[G(t;\tau)] = \delta(t-\tau),\\ G(0; \tau) = \dfd{G}{t}(0;\tau) = 0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ for each $\tau\in (0,\infty)$.

The Green's function is determined by

  1. $G(t;\tau) = 0$ for $t<\tau$;

  2. $L[G(t;\tau)] = 0$ on $t>\tau$; and

  3. jump condition $G(\tau^+; \tau) = 0$ and $\dfd{G}{t}(\tau^+, \tau) = 1/\alpha(\tau)$.

Therefore the solution $y$ is $$ \begin{align*} y(t) = \int_{0}^{t} G(t;\tau) f(\tau) \d \tau \end{align*} $$ which preserves causality: $y(t)$ only depends on the values $f(\tau)$ before $\tau<t$.

Fourier transform open_in_new

Throughout this section, write $\int \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$. Assume all functions are sufficiently nice.

Definition (Fourier transform) open_in_new

Let $f: \R\to\C$ be an integrable function.

Define its Fourier transform $\hat f: \R\to\C$ by $$ \begin{align*} \hat f(\lambda) = \int_{\infty}^{\infty} e^{-i\lambda x} f(x) \d x. \end{align*} $$ We write $\mathcal{F}[f] = \hat f$, or $\mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}[f(x)] = \hat f(\lambda)$.

Proposition (properties of Fourier transform) open_in_new

Let $f:\R\to\C$.

  1. Derivatives and powers:

    • $\mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}$$\left[\left(\dd{x}\right)^k f(x)\right] = (i\lambda)^k \hat f(\lambda)$;

    • $\mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}\left[x^k f(x)\right] = \left(i \dd{\lambda}\right)^k \hat f(\lambda)$.

  2. Translation and exponentials:

    • $\mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}\left[f(x-a)\right] = e^{-i\lambda a} \hat f(\lambda)$;

    • $\mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}\left[e^{-iax}f(x)\right] = \hat f(x+a)$.

Proof

Proposition (Fourier inversion formula) open_in_new

Let $f:\R\to\C$. We can recover $f$ from $\hat f$ via the inversion formula $$ \begin{align*} f(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int e^{i\lambda x}\hat f(\lambda) \d \lambda. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • Applying this to $f(x)=\delta(x-y)$ gives $$ \begin{align*} \delta(x-y) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int e^{i\lambda(x-y)} \d \lambda. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (Parseval's theorem for Fourier transform) open_in_new

Let $f,g:\R\to\C$. $$ \begin{align*} \int f(x) \,\overline{g(x)} \d x = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int \hat f(\lambda)\, \overline{\hat g(\lambda)} \d \lambda. \end{align*} $$

In particular, $$ \begin{align*} \int |f(x)|^2 \d x = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int |\hat f(\lambda)|^2 \d \lambda \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (convolution) open_in_new

Let $f,g:\R\to\C$. The convolution of $f$ and $g$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} (f*g)(x) = \int f(x-y)\,g(y) \d y. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (Fourier transform of convolution) open_in_new

For $f,g:\R\to\C$, $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F}_{x\mapsto \lambda}\left[f* g(x)\right] = \hat f(\lambda) \,\hat g(\lambda). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (higher dimensional Fourier transform) open_in_new

For a function $f: \R^n \to \C$, define its Fourier transform $\hat f: \R^n \to \C$ by $$ \begin{align*} \hat f(\v \lambda) = \int e^{-i\v \lambda \cdot \v x} f(\v x) \d ^n \v x. \end{align*} $$

We have analogous results from one dimensional Fourier transform:

  • Convolution theorem $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F}_{\v x\mapsto \v \lambda}\left[f*g(\v x)\right] = \hat f(\v \lambda) \hat g(\v \lambda), \qquad \text{where } f*g(\v x) = \int f(\v x-\v y) g(\v y) \d ^n \v y. \end{align*} $$

  • Inverse Fourier transform $$ \begin{align*} f(\v x) ={}& \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n} \int e^{i\v \lambda\cdot \v x} \hat f(\v \lambda) \d^n \v \lambda. \end{align*} $$

  • Delta functions $$ \begin{align*} \hat \delta(\v \lambda) = 1, \qquad \delta(\v x) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n} \int e^{i\v \lambda\cdot \v x} \d \v \lambda. \end{align*} $$

  • Laplacian $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F}_{\v x\mapsto \v \lambda}[\Delta f(\v x)] ={}& ((i\lambda_1)^2 + \ldots + (i\lambda_n)^2 ) \hat f(\v \lambda) \\ ={}& - |\v \lambda|^2 \hat f(\v \lambda). \end{align*} $$

Well-posedness open_in_new

Hadamard famously declared that for a problem to be well-posed, we require

  1. solution to exist;

  2. solution to be unique;

  3. solution to depend continuously on given data.

An example that fails 3. is the backwards heat equation $\phi_t + \kappa\phi_{x x} = 0$ on $\Omega = (0,\pi)$.

Method of characteristics open_in_new

Consider problems of the form $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} a(x,y) \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + b(x,y) \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = c(x,y,u), \\ u = f \qquad \text{on } C\subset \R^2. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

We will change variables $(x,y) \mapsto (t,s)$.

  1. Parametrise $C$ as $(x_0(s), y_0(s))$.

  2. Solve the characteristic curves $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{x}{t} ={}& a(x,y), \\ \pdfd{y}{t} ={}& b(x,y), \\ (x(0,s), y(0,s)) ={}& (x_0(s), y_0(s)). \end{align*} $$

  3. Let $z(t, s) = u(x(t, s), y(t, s))$. By the chain rule, $z$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{z}{t} ={}& c(x,y,z), \\ z(0, s) ={}& f(x_0(s), y_0(s)). \end{align*} $$

  4. Invert the map $(t,s) \mapsto (x,y)$ to get $u(x,y) = z(t(x,y), s(x,y))$.

The non-characteristic condition requires $(a, b)$ not to be tangent to $C$ at any point, i.e. if $\v n$ is the normal to $C$, we want $\v v\cdot \v n|_C \neq 0$.

Classification of second order PDEs open_in_new

A general second order linear PDE in two dimensions has the form $Lu = g$ where $$ \begin{align*} Lu ={}& a(x,y) \pdfd[2]{u}{x} + 2b(x,y) \pdfxd{u}{x}{y} + c(x,y) \pdfd[2]{u}{y} \\ & + \underbrace{d(x,y) \pdfd{u}{x} + e(x,y) \pdfd{u}{y} + f(x,y) u}_{\text{lower order terms}}. \end{align*} $$

Under a change of variables $(x,y) \mapsto (\xi, \eta)$, letting $U(\xi,\eta) = u(x,y)$, $L$ becomes $$ \begin{align*} Lu ={}& A(\xi, \eta) \pdfd[2]{U}{\xi} + 2B(\xi, \eta) \pdfxd{U}{\xi}{\eta} + C(\xi,\eta) \pdfd[2]{U}{\eta} + \text{lower order terms} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} A ={}& a\, \xi_x ^2 + 2b\,\xi_x \xi_y + c \, \xi_y^2, \\ B ={}& a\, \xi_x \eta_x + b\,(\xi_x \eta_y + \xi_y \eta_x) + c\, \xi_y \eta_y, \\ C ={}& a\, \eta_x ^2 + 2b\,\eta_x \eta_y + c \, \eta_y^2. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

We can make $A = C = 0$ if we can get $\xi_x/\xi_y$ and $\eta_x/\eta_y$ to solve the equation $az^2 + 2bz + c = 0$.

Consider a curve $\xi(x,y) = \text{const.}$. Taking derivative, we have $$ \begin{align*} 0 = \xi_x + \dfd{y}{x} \xi_y \implies \dfd{y}{x} = -\left(\frac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2 - ac}}{a}\right). \end{align*} $$ These are the characteristic curves.

We say $L$ is

  • elliptic if $b^2 < ac$ (no real characteristic curves);

  • parabolic if $b^2 = ac$ (one family of characteristic curve); or

  • hyperbolic if $b^2 > ac$ (two families of characteristic curves).

If we have a hyperbolic problem, we can introduce coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$ so that the problem is in canonical form $$ \begin{align*} \pdfxd{U}{\xi}{\eta} + \text{lower order terms} = G(\xi,\eta). \end{align*} $$

Proposition (free space Green's function for the Laplacian) open_in_new

Define the free space Green's function for the Laplacian on $\R^n$ as $$ \begin{align*} G(\v x; \v y) = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{1}{(n-2) |S^{n-1}|} \cdot \dfrac{1}{|\v x-\v y|^{n-2}}, &n>2, \\ \dfrac{1}{2\pi} \log |\v x-\v y|, &n=2 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $|S^{n-1}|$ is the area of a unit $(n-1)$-sphere.

Dirichlet Green's function for the Laplacian open_in_new

The Dirichlet Green's function $\mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y)$ for the Laplacian on $\Omega$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \Delta \mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y) = \delta(\v x - \v y), &\v x \in \Omega \\ \mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y) = 0, &\v x \in \partial \Omega. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

Consider the initial value problem $$ \begin{align*} (*)\begin{cases} v_{tt} - c^2 v_{x x} = P(x, t), &x>0,\, t>0 , \\ v(0, t) = p(t), &t>0, \\ v(x,0) = q(x), &x>0, \\ v_t(x,0) = r(x), &x>0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where the initial and boundary conditions are compatible at $(x,t)=(0,0)$, i.e. $p(0) = q(0)$ and $p'(0) = r(0)$.

This is equivalent to $$ \begin{align*} (\dagger)\begin{cases} u_{tt} - c^2 u_{x x} = F(x,t), &x>0, t>0, \\ u(0,t) = 0, &t>0, \\ u(x,0) = f(x), &x>0, \\ u_t(x,0) = g(x), &x>0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ with $F(0,t) = 0$ and $f(0) = g(0) = 0$.

Proposition (solution to wave equation on the half line) open_in_new

The solution to $(\dagger)$ here on the half line is the solution on the full line $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} w_{tt} - c^2 w_{x x} = F_\text{odd}(x,t), &\R\times(0,\infty), \\ w(x,0) = f_\text{odd}(x), &\R, \\ w_t(x,0) = g_\text{odd}(x), &\R \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ restricted to the half line, where $$ \begin{align*} f_\text{odd}(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), &x\geq 0, \\ -f(-x), &x<0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ is the odd extension of $f$ and similarly for $F_{\text{odd}}$ and $g_{\text{odd}}$.
