Well-posedness open_in_new

Hadamard famously declared that for a problem to be well-posed, we require

  1. solution to exist;

  2. solution to be unique;

  3. solution to depend continuously on given data.

An example that fails 3. is the backwards heat equation $\phi_t + \kappa\phi_{x x} = 0$ on $\Omega = (0,\pi)$.

Method of characteristics open_in_new

Consider problems of the form $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} a(x,y) \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x} + b(x,y) \dfrac{\partial u}{\partial y} = c(x,y,u), \\ u = f \qquad \text{on } C\subset \R^2. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

We will change variables $(x,y) \mapsto (t,s)$.

  1. Parametrise $C$ as $(x_0(s), y_0(s))$.

  2. Solve the characteristic curves $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{x}{t} ={}& a(x,y), \\ \pdfd{y}{t} ={}& b(x,y), \\ (x(0,s), y(0,s)) ={}& (x_0(s), y_0(s)). \end{align*} $$

  3. Let $z(t, s) = u(x(t, s), y(t, s))$. By the chain rule, $z$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{z}{t} ={}& c(x,y,z), \\ z(0, s) ={}& f(x_0(s), y_0(s)). \end{align*} $$

  4. Invert the map $(t,s) \mapsto (x,y)$ to get $u(x,y) = z(t(x,y), s(x,y))$.

The non-characteristic condition requires $(a, b)$ not to be tangent to $C$ at any point, i.e. if $\v n$ is the normal to $C$, we want $\v v\cdot \v n|_C \neq 0$.

Classification of second order PDEs open_in_new

A general second order linear PDE in two dimensions has the form $Lu = g$ where $$ \begin{align*} Lu ={}& a(x,y) \pdfd[2]{u}{x} + 2b(x,y) \pdfxd{u}{x}{y} + c(x,y) \pdfd[2]{u}{y} \\ & + \underbrace{d(x,y) \pdfd{u}{x} + e(x,y) \pdfd{u}{y} + f(x,y) u}_{\text{lower order terms}}. \end{align*} $$

Under a change of variables $(x,y) \mapsto (\xi, \eta)$, letting $U(\xi,\eta) = u(x,y)$, $L$ becomes $$ \begin{align*} Lu ={}& A(\xi, \eta) \pdfd[2]{U}{\xi} + 2B(\xi, \eta) \pdfxd{U}{\xi}{\eta} + C(\xi,\eta) \pdfd[2]{U}{\eta} + \text{lower order terms} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} A ={}& a\, \xi_x ^2 + 2b\,\xi_x \xi_y + c \, \xi_y^2, \\ B ={}& a\, \xi_x \eta_x + b\,(\xi_x \eta_y + \xi_y \eta_x) + c\, \xi_y \eta_y, \\ C ={}& a\, \eta_x ^2 + 2b\,\eta_x \eta_y + c \, \eta_y^2. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

We can make $A = C = 0$ if we can get $\xi_x/\xi_y$ and $\eta_x/\eta_y$ to solve the equation $az^2 + 2bz + c = 0$.

Consider a curve $\xi(x,y) = \text{const.}$. Taking derivative, we have $$ \begin{align*} 0 = \xi_x + \dfd{y}{x} \xi_y \implies \dfd{y}{x} = -\left(\frac{-b\pm \sqrt{b^2 - ac}}{a}\right). \end{align*} $$ These are the characteristic curves.

We say $L$ is

  • elliptic if $b^2 < ac$ (no real characteristic curves);

  • parabolic if $b^2 = ac$ (one family of characteristic curve); or

  • hyperbolic if $b^2 > ac$ (two families of characteristic curves).

If we have a hyperbolic problem, we can introduce coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$ so that the problem is in canonical form $$ \begin{align*} \pdfxd{U}{\xi}{\eta} + \text{lower order terms} = G(\xi,\eta). \end{align*} $$

Green's function for heat equation open_in_new

Definition (heat kernel) open_in_new

For the heat equation $u_t - \kappa\Delta u = F(\v x, t)$ in $\R^n$, define the heat kernel $$ \begin{align*} K_t(\v x) = \frac{1}{(4\pi \kappa t)^{n/2}} e^{-|\v x|^2 /4\kappa t}. \end{align*} $$

  • Its Fourier transform is $$ \begin{align*} \hat K_t(\v \lambda) = e^{-\kappa t|\v \lambda|^2}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Proposition (solution to heat equation) open_in_new

Consider the initial value problem of the heat equation $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} u_t - \kappa \Delta u = F(\v x, t), &\R^n \times (0,\infty), \\ u(\v x, 0) = f(\v x), &\R^n. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

  • For zero forcing $F=0$, the solution is $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x,t) = (K_t * f)(\v x) = \int K_t(\v x - \v y) \,f(\v y) \d ^n \v y. \end{align*} $$

  • For zero initial condition $f=0$, the solution is $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x,t) = \int_{0}^{t} \int K_{t-s}(\v x-\v y) \, F(\v y,s) \d ^n \v y \d s \\ \end{align*} $$ Equivalently, if we set $F(\v x,t) = 0$ for $t<0$, then $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x, t) ={}& \iint G(\v x,t; \v y, s) \, F(\v y, s) \d^n \v y \d s \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} G(\v x, t; \v y, s) = H(t-s) \, K_{t-s}(\v x-\v y) \end{align*} $$ is the Green's function for heat equation on $\R^n$ with zero initial conditions.

Proof

Proposition (Green's function solves heat equation with impulse forcing) open_in_new

The Green's function $G(\v x, t; \v y, s)$ for heat equation with zero initial conditions satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{G}{t} - \kappa \Delta G = \delta(\v x- \v y)\, \delta(t-s). \end{align*} $$

Green's function for Laplacian open_in_new

Proposition (Fourier transform of powers of radius) open_in_new

In $\R^n$, for $\alpha>0$, we have $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F}_{\v x\mapsto \v \lambda}\big[|\v x|^{-\alpha}\big] = C_{n,\alpha} |\v \lambda|^{\alpha-n} \end{align*} $$ where $C_{n,\alpha}$ is a constant.

Proof

Proposition (free space Green's function for the Laplacian) open_in_new

Define the free space Green's function for the Laplacian on $\R^n$ as $$ \begin{align*} G(\v x; \v y) = \begin{cases} -\dfrac{1}{(n-2) |S^{n-1}|} \cdot \dfrac{1}{|\v x-\v y|^{n-2}}, &n>2, \\ \dfrac{1}{2\pi} \log |\v x-\v y|, &n=2. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

Then $G$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \Delta G(\v x; \v y) = \delta(\v x-\v y). \end{align*} $$ Moreover for $n>2$, $G$ is the unique solution where $|G(\v x;\v y)| \to 0$ as $|\v x|\to \infty$.

Proof

Dirichlet Green's function for the Laplacian open_in_new

The Dirichlet Green's function $\mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y)$ for the Laplacian on $\Omega$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \Delta \mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y) = \delta(\v x - \v y), &\v x \in \Omega \\ \mathcal{G}(\v x; \v y) = 0, &\v x \in \partial \Omega. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

The solution to $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} \Delta u = F, & \v x\in \Omega, \\ u = 0,& \v x \in \partial \Omega \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ is then $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x) = \int_{\Omega} \mathcal{G}(\v x;\v y) F(\v y) \d^n \v y. \end{align*} $$

To construct $\mathcal{G}$, we need to add on suitable harmonic function to the free space Green's function $G(\v x; \v y)$ to get the correct boundary conditions.

Green's function for wave equation open_in_new

Definition (kernel for wave equation) open_in_new

For the wave equation $u_{tt} - c^2 \Delta u = F(\v x,t)$ in $\R^n$, define $\Phi_t(\v x)$ to have the Fourier transform $$ \begin{align*} \hat \Phi_t(\v \lambda) = \frac{\sin(ct|\v \lambda|)}{c|\v \lambda|}. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (kernel for wave equation in different dimensions) open_in_new

Consider $\Phi_t(\v x)$ in $\R^n$.

  • If $n=1$, then $$ \begin{align*} \Phi_t(x) = \frac{1}{2c}\big(H(x + ct) - h(x - ct)\big). \end{align*} $$

  • If $n=3$, then $$ \begin{align*} \Phi_t(x) = \frac{1}{4\pi c|\v x|}\big(\delta(|\v x|-ct) - \delta(|\v x| + ct)\big). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (solution to wave equation) open_in_new

Consider the initial value problem of the wave equation $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} u_{tt} - c^2 \Delta u = F(\v x, t), &\R^n \times (0,\infty), \\ u(\v x, 0) = f(\v x), &\R^n, \\ u_t(\v x, 0) = g(\v x), &\R^n. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$

  • For zero forcing $F=0$, the solution is $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x,t) = \pdd{t}(\Phi_t * f)(\v x) + (\Phi_t * g)(\v x). \end{align*} $$

  • For zero initial conditions $f=g=0$, the solution is $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x, t) = \int_{0}^{t} \int \Phi_{t-s}(\v x-\v y) \, F(\v y,s) \d^n \v y \d s. \end{align*} $$ Equivalently, if we set $F(\v x,t) = 0$ for $t<0$, then $$ \begin{align*} u(\v x,t) = \iint G(\v x, t; \v y, s) \, F(\v y, s) \d^n \v y \d s \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} G(\v x,t; \v y, s) = H(t-s)\, \Phi_{t-s}(\v x-\v y) \end{align*} $$ is the Green's function for the wave equation on $\R^n$.

Proof

Proposition (reduction of wave equation problem on the half line) open_in_new

Consider the initial value problem $$ \begin{align*} (*)\begin{cases} v_{tt} - c^2 v_{x x} = P(x, t), &x>0,\, t>0 , \\ v(0, t) = p(t), &t>0, \\ v(x,0) = q(x), &x>0, \\ v_t(x,0) = r(x), &x>0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where the initial and boundary conditions are compatible at $(x,t)=(0,0)$, i.e. $p(0) = q(0)$ and $p'(0) = r(0)$.

This is equivalent to $$ \begin{align*} (\dagger)\begin{cases} u_{tt} - c^2 u_{x x} = F(x,t), &x>0, t>0, \\ u(0,t) = 0, &t>0, \\ u(x,0) = f(x), &x>0, \\ u_t(x,0) = g(x), &x>0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ with $F(0,t) = 0$ and $f(0) = g(0) = 0$.

Proof

Proposition (solution to wave equation on the half line) open_in_new

The solution to $(\dagger)$ here on the half line is the solution on the full line $$ \begin{align*} \begin{cases} w_{tt} - c^2 w_{x x} = F_\text{odd}(x,t), &\R\times(0,\infty), \\ w(x,0) = f_\text{odd}(x), &\R, \\ w_t(x,0) = g_\text{odd}(x), &\R \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ restricted to the half line, where $$ \begin{align*} f_\text{odd}(x) = \begin{cases} f(x), &x\geq 0, \\ -f(-x), &x<0 \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ is the odd extension of $f$ and similarly for $F_{\text{odd}}$ and $g_{\text{odd}}$.
