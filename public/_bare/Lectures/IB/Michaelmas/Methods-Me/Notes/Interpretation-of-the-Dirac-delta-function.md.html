Define, for $n=1,2,3,\ldots$, $$ \begin{align*} \delta_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{n}{\alpha} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-n^2x^2}\right), &|x|<\frac{1}{n}, \\ 0, &|x| \geq \frac{1}{n}. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} \alpha = \int_{-1}^{1} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{1-y^2}\right) \d y. \end{align*} $$

The family of functions $\set{f_n}$ satisfies the following:

  1. $\delta_n(x)$ is smooth.

  2. $\lim_{n\to \infty} \delta_n(x) = 0$ for $x\neq 0$.

  3. $\lim_{n\to \infty} \int_{-\epsilon}^{\epsilon} \delta_n(x) \d x = 1$ for $\epsilon>0$.

We cannot say $\delta(x) = \lim_{n\to \infty} \delta_n(x)$ because the limit does not exist at $x=0$, but the limit does exist in a weaker sense.

When we see a Dirac delta function, we interpret it as sequences of statements involving $\delta_n(x)$, which we will eventually take the limit of. For example, $$ \begin{align*} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(x-a)f(x) \d x := \lim_{n\to \infty} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta_n(x-a) f(x)\d x \end{align*} $$ or $$ \begin{align*} \ddot y + \omega^2 y = \delta(t) \iff \ddot y_n + \omega^2 y_n = \delta_n(t), \quad y(t) = \lim_{n\to \infty} y_n(t). \end{align*} $$

We manipulate $\delta(x)$ as if it is an ordinary function, on the understanding that we are manipulating sequences of statements and then taking limits.
