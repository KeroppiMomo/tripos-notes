Simultaneously diagonalisable $\implies$ diagonalisable and commutes

Let $B$ be a basis for $V$ such that $[\alpha]^B_B$ and $[\beta]^B_B$. Clearly $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are then diagonalisable. Also since diagonal matrices commute, $$ \begin{align*} [\alpha\circ\beta]^B_B = [\alpha]^B_B [\beta]^B_B = [\beta]^B_B [\alpha]^B_B = [\beta\circ \alpha]^B_B \end{align*} $$ and so $\alpha\circ \beta = \beta\circ\alpha$.

Diagonalisable and commutes $\implies$ simultaneously diagonalisable

Suppose $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are diagonalisable and $\alpha\circ\beta=\beta\circ\alpha$.

Since $\alpha$ is diagonalisable, $V = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus \ldots \oplus V_{\lambda_k}$ where $\lambda_i$ are the eigenvalues of $\alpha$ and $V_{\lambda_i}$ is the corresponding $\lambda_i$-eigenspace of $\alpha$.

Since $\beta$ is diagonalisable, there exists $p\in \mathbb{F}[t]\setminus \set{0}$ such that $p(\beta) = 0$ and $\beta$ is a product of distinct linear factors.

Note that the restriction $\beta|_{V_{\lambda_i}} : V_{\lambda_i} \to V_{\lambda_i}$ is well-defined, since for each $v\in V_{\lambda_i}$, $$ \begin{align*} \alpha(\beta(v)) ={}& \beta(\alpha(v)) \\ ={}& \beta(\lambda_i v) \\ ={}& \lambda_i \beta(v) \end{align*} $$ so $\beta(v)\in V_{\lambda_{i}}$.

Then since $p(\beta|_{V_{\lambda_i}}) = p(\beta)|_{V_{\lambda_i}} = 0$ and $p\neq 0$ is a product of distinct linear factors, $\beta|_{V_{\lambda_i}}$ is diagonalisable.

Therefore we can let $B_i$ be a basis for each $V_{\lambda_i}$ consisting of eigenvectors of $B$. Then $B = \bigcup_{i=1}^{k} B_i$ is a basis for $V$, and each element of $B$ is an eigenvector in both $\alpha$ and $\beta$.

Hence $[\alpha]^B_B$ and $[\beta]^B_B$.
