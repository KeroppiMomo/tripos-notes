Polynomials open_in_new

Definition (polynomial, degree, monic, polynomial function, root) open_in_new

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a field.

  • A polynomial over $\mathbb{F}$ is a formal expression $a_nt^n + \ldots + a_1 t + a_0$ for some $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ and coefficients $a_i\in \mathbb{F}$.

  • The degree of $f$ (denoted $\deg(f)$) is the maximal integer $m$ such that $a_m\neq 0$. By convention, $\deg(0) = -\infty$.

  • $a_m$ is called the leading coefficient. $f$ is called monic if $a_m = 1$.

  • Let $\mathbb{F}[t]$ be the set of all polynomials of $\mathbb{F}$, equipped with addition and multiplication: for $f(t) = \sum_{i} ' a_i t^i$ and $g(t) = \sum_{j} ' b_j t^j$,

    • the coefficient of $t^k$ in $(f+g)$ is $a_k+b_k$;

    • the coefficient of $t^k$ in $(f\cdot g)$ is $\sum_{i=0}^{k} a_i b_{k-i}$.

  • The polynomial function $\mathbb{F}\to \mathbb{F}$ is defined by$f(\lambda) = \sum_{i} ' a_i \lambda^i$.

  • $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ is a root of $f\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ if $f(\lambda) = 0$.

  • We write $g\mid f$ if there exists $q\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ such that $f(t) = q(t) g(t)$.

Lemma (Euclidean algorithm for polynomials) open_in_new

For all $f,g\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$ with $g\neq 0$, there exists $q,r\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ such that $$ \begin{align*} f(t) = q(t) g(t) + r(t) \quad \text{and} \quad \deg(r) < \deg(g). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Corollary (Bézout identity for polynomials) open_in_new

If $f_1, \ldots, f_k\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$ have no common factor of degree at least 1, then there exists polynomials $p_1, \ldots, p_k\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ such that $$ \begin{align*} 1 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} p_i(t) f_i(t). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Lemma (factor theorem) open_in_new

Let $f\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$.

$\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ is a root of $f$ iff $(t-\lambda) \mid f(t)$.

Proof

Definition (multiplicity of root) open_in_new

For a root $\lambda$ of $f\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$, the multiplicity of $\lambda$ is the largest integer $e$ such that $(t-\lambda)^e\mid f(t)$.

Corollary (most number of roots of polynomials) open_in_new

Let $f\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$. If $\deg(f) = n\geq 0$, then $f$ has at most $n$ roots counted with multiplicity.

Proof

Corollary (uniqueness of polynomials agreeing on points) open_in_new

Let $f_1, f_2\in \mathbb{F}[t]$.

If $\deg(f_1), \deg(f_2) < n$ and $f_1(\lambda_i) = f_2(\lambda_i)$ for some distinct $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n\in \mathbb{F}$, then $f_1 = f_2$.

Proof

Consequently, if $\mathbb{F}$ is infinite, then $f\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ is uniquely determined by its polynomial function $\lambda\mapsto f(\lambda)$.

  • If $\mathbb{F}$ is finite, then this fails, e.g. if $\mathbb{F}=\set{\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n}$, then $f(t) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} (t-\lambda_i)$ has degree $n$ but $f(\lambda) = 0$ for all $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$.

Theorem (fundamental theorem of algebra) open_in_new

Any $f\in$ $\C[t]$ of degree $n\geq 1$ has exactly $n$ roots, accounting multiplicity, i.e. $f(t)$ is a product of linear factors.

Definition (eigenvector, eigenvalue, eigenspace) open_in_new

Let $V$ be any $\mathbb{F}$-vector space, and $\alpha\in$ $\mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

  • $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ is said to be an eigenvalue of $\alpha$ if there exists $v\in V\setminus \set{\v 0}$ such that $\alpha(v) = \lambda v$.

  • Such $v$ is called a $\lambda$-eigenvector of $\alpha$.

  • The $\lambda$-eigenspace of $\alpha$ is defined as $V_\lambda = \sb{v\in V}{\alpha(v) = \lambda v}$. Equivalently, $V_\lambda = \ker(\alpha - \lambda\,\id_V) \leq V$.

Definition (diagonalisable and triangularisable) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space on $\mathbb{F}$.

  • $\alpha\in$ $\mathcal{L}(V,V)$ is diagonalisable if there exists $B$ for $V$ such that $[\alpha]^B_B$ is diagonal.

  • $\alpha\in\mathcal{L}(V,V)$ is triangularisable if there exists $B$ for $V$ such that $[\alpha]^B_B$ is upper triangular.

  • $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ is diagonalisable if $A$ is similar to a diagonal matrix.

  • $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ is triangularisable if $A$ is similar to an upper triangular matrix.

Theorem (distinct eigenvalues imply linearly independent eigenvectors) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

If $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n \in \mathbb{F}$ are distinct, and $v_i \in$ $V_{\lambda_i}$ $\setminus \set{\v 0}$, then $\set{v_1,\ldots,v_n}$ is linearly independent.

Proof

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\alpha$ has at most finitely many eigenvalues.

Proposition (direct sum of eigenspaces) open_in_new

Let $V$ be finite-dimensional and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$. Let $V_{\lambda_1}, \ldots, V_{\lambda_k}$ be all the non-zero eigenspaces for $\alpha$.

  1. $\langle V_{\lambda_1} \cup \ldots \cup V_{\lambda_k}\rangle = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus \ldots \oplus V_{\lambda_k} \leq V$.

  2. $\alpha$ is diagonalisable iff $V = V_{\lambda_1} \oplus \ldots \oplus V_{\lambda_k}$.

Proof

Definition (characteristic polynomial) open_in_new

  • For $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$, the characteristic polynomial of $A$ is $\chi_A(t) = \det(t\, I_n - A) \in \mathbb{F}[t]$.

  • Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space. For $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$, the characteristic polynomial of $\alpha$ is $\chi_\alpha(t) = \det(t\,\id_V - \alpha)$.

Proposition (triangularisable map and roots of characteristic polynomial) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb{F}$, and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

$\alpha$ is triangularisable iff $\chi_\alpha(t)$ is a product of linear factors.

Proof

  • If $\chi_\alpha$ is degree $n$ but does not have $n$ roots, then $\chi_\alpha$ is not a product of linear factors and so $\alpha$ is not triangularisable.

  • If $\mathbb{F}$ is $\mathbb{C}$, then $\chi_\alpha(t)$ is a product of linear factors so all $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(\C, \C)$ are triangularisable.

Definition (linear map polynomial, matrix polynomial) open_in_new

Let $p(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i t^i \in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$.

  • Let $V$ be any $\mathbb{F}$-vector space, and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$. Define $p(\alpha) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i \alpha^i \in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

  • Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$. Define $p(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i A^i \in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$.

Proposition (applying linear map polynomial to eigenvector) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$. For any $v\in$ $V_\lambda$ and $p\in \mathbb{F}[t]$, $$ \begin{align*} p(\alpha)(v) = p(\lambda)\cdot v. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (minimal polynomial) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $\alpha\in$ $\mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

Let $I_\alpha = \sb{f\in \mathbb{F}[t]}{f(\alpha) = 0}$.

Define the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ as the monic non-zero polynomial $m_a\in I_\alpha\setminus \set{0}$ of minimal degree.

Lemma (minimal polynomial divides polynomials that send linear map to zero) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

For any $f\in$ $I_\alpha$, we have $m_\alpha$ $\mid f$.

Proof

Proposition (existence and uniqueness of minimal polynomials) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

  1. The minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ is unique.

  2. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then the minimal polynomial exists.

Proof

Theorem (Cayley-Hamilton) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space.

For any $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$, we have $\chi_\alpha$$(\alpha)$ $=0$.

Proof for $\C$

  • Therefore the minimal polynomial $m_\alpha$ divides $\chi_\alpha$.

Definition (algebraic and geometric multiplicity) open_in_new

Let $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ be an eigenvalue of $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$ where $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space. Define

  • the algebraic multiplicity $a_\lambda$ of $\lambda$ as the multipliicty of $\lambda$ as a root of $\chi_\alpha(t)$; and

  • the geometric multiplicity $g_\lambda$ of $\lambda$ as $g_\lambda = \dim($$V_\lambda$$)$.

It is common to define $a_\lambda = 0 = g_{\lambda}$ if $\lambda$ is not an eigenvalue of $\alpha$.

Proposition (geometric multiplicity at most algebraic multiplicity) open_in_new

Let $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ be an eigenvalue of $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$ where $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space.

Then the geometric multiplicity $g_\lambda \leq a_{\lambda}$ the algebraic multiplicity.

Proof

Lemma (sum of multiplicities, triangularisability and diagonalisability) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let the eigenvalues of $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$ be $\lambda_1, \lambda_2,\ldots,\lambda_k$.

  1. $\sum_{i=1}^{k}$ $g_{\lambda_i}$ $\leq V$, with equality iff $\alpha$ is diagonalisable.

  2. $\sum_{i=1}^{k}$ $a_{\lambda_i}$ $\leq V$, with equality iff $\alpha$ is triangularisable.

Proof

Proposition (diagonalisable in C iff algebraic multiplicity equals geometric multiplicity) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\C$, and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

$\alpha$ is diagonalisable iff $a_\lambda$ $=$ $g_\lambda$ for all $\lambda\in \C$.

Proof

Definition (Jordan normal form) open_in_new

A matrix $A \in M_{n\times n}(\C)$ is said to be in Jordan normal form if $$ \begin{align*} A = \pmat{J_{n_1}(\lambda_1) \\ & J_{n_2}(\lambda_2) \\ & & \ddots \\ &&& J_{n_{k}}(\lambda_k)} \end{align*} $$ for some $\lambda_i\in \C$ (not necessarily distinct) and $n_i\in \N$ with $n=\sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i$.

Theorem (Jordan normal form) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$, and fix an endomorphism $α$, giving an $\mathbb{F}[X]$-module $V_α$.

If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $$ \begin{align*} V_α \isom \C[X]/((X-λ_1)^{a_1}) \oplus \ldots \oplus \C[X]/((X - λ_r)^{a_r}) \end{align*} $$ where $λ_i\in \C$ may not be distinct.

This gives a basis for $V$ where the matrix of $α$ is in Jordan canonical form $$ \begin{align*} \pmat{J_{a_1}(λ_1) \\ &\ddots \\ &&J_{a_r}(λ_r)}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (multiplicity and Jordan blocks) open_in_new

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{C})$.

  • $a_\lambda(A)$ $=$ sum of sizes of $\lambda$-Jordan blocks for $A$;

  • $g_\lambda(A)$ $=$ number of $\lambda$-Jordan blocks for $A$;

  • $c_\lambda(A)$ $=$ maximum size of a $\lambda$-Jordan block for $A$.

Proof

Lemma (uniqueness of Jordan normal form) open_in_new

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\C)$ with a Jordan normal form $\mathrm{JNF}(A)$.

For $r\in \N$ and $\lambda\in \C$, define $$ \begin{align*} R_{\lambda,r}(A) := \op{rk}\big((A-\lambda I_n)^{r-1}\big) - \op{rk}\big((A - \lambda I_n)^{r}\big). \end{align*} $$ Then $R_{\lambda,r}(A)$ equals the number of $\lambda$-Jordan blocks $J_l(\lambda)$ of $\mathrm{JNF}(A)$ with $l\geq r$.

Therefore $\mathrm{JNF}(A)$ is unique up to reordering the Jordan blocks.

Proof

Definition (generalised eigenspace) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\C$ and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$.

For an eigenvalue $\lambda_i$, define the generalised $\lambda_i$-eigenspace of $\alpha$ as $$ \begin{align*} V_i = \ker\big((\alpha - \lambda_i\,\id_V)^{c_i}) \leq V \end{align*} $$ where $c_i$ is the multiplicity of $\lambda_i$ as a root of the minimal polynomial $m_\alpha$.
