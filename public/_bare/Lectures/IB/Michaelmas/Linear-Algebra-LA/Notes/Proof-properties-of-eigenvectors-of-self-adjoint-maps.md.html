Real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors

Let $v\neq \v 0$ be a $\lambda$-eigenvector and $w\neq \v 0$ be a $\mu$-eigenvector. Note that $$ \begin{align*} \lambda\langle v,w\rangle ={}& \langle \lambda v, w \rangle \\ ={}& \langle \alpha(v), w\rangle \\ ={}& \langle v, \alpha(w)\rangle &&\text{self-adjoint} \\ ={}& \langle v, \mu w\rangle \\ ={}& \overline{\mu} \langle v, w\rangle. \end{align*} $$

  1. Take $v=w$ and $\lambda=\mu$ to see that since $\langle v,v\rangle\neq 0$, $\lambda = \overline{\lambda}$ so $\lambda$ is real.

  2. Take $\lambda\neq \mu = \overline{\mu}$ to see that $\langle v,w\rangle = 0$.

Existence of an eigenvector

Let $B$ be an orthonormal basis for $V$, and $A = [\alpha]_B$. We treat $A$ as a complex matrix.

Consider the characteristic polynomial $\chi_A$ as a complex polynomial. Then by the fundamental theorem of algebra, it has a root $\lambda\in \C$, which is an eigenvalue of $A$.

$\alpha$ is self-adjoint, so $A$ is Hermitian. By 1, $\lambda$ is real.

Let $\v c\in \C^n$ be an $\lambda$-eigenvector of $A$. If $V$ is over $\C$, let $v\in V$ such that $[v]_B = \v c$. Then $v$ is an $\lambda$-eigenvector of $\alpha$, and we are done.

For $V$ being over $\R$, write $\v c = \v a + i \v b$ where $\v a,\v b\in \R^n$. Note that $$ \begin{align*} A\v a + iA \v b = A\v c = \lambda \v c = \lambda \v a + i\lambda \v b. \end{align*} $$ Since $A$ is real and $\lambda$ is real, we have $A\v a = \lambda \v a$ and $A\v b = \lambda\v b$. Moreover $\v c\neq \v 0$, so either $\v a$ or $\v b$ is non-zero. Take $v\in V$ such that $[v]_B = \v a$ or $\v b$ whichever is non-zero, and then $v$ is an $\lambda$-eigenvector of $\alpha$.
