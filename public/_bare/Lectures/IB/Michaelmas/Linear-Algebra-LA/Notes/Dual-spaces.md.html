Definition (dual vector space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space.

The dual space of $V$ is defined as $V^\star =$ $\mathcal{L}$$(V,\mathbb{F})$.

An element of $V^\star$ is called a linear functional of $V$.

Proposition (dual basis) open_in_new

Let $B$ be a basis for a vector space $V$.

Define the basis set of $B$ is defined as $B^\star = \sb{b^\star}{b\in B} \subseteq$ $V^\star$, where $b^\star: V\to \mathbb{F}$ is a linear map given by $$ \begin{align*} b^\star\left(\sum_{c\in B} ' \lambda_c c\right) = \lambda_b, \end{align*} $$ or equivalently, $b^\star$ is the unique linear map defined by $b^\star(c) = \delta_{b,c}$ for all $c\in B$.

  1. $B^\star$ is linearly independent.

  2. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $B^\star$ is a basis for $V^\star$, called the dual basis of $B$. As a result $V\isom V^\star$.

Proof

  • There exist (infinite-dimensional) vectors spaces $V$ where $V\not\isom V^\star$. (Example)

Definition (annihilator) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, and $S\subseteq V$.

The annihilator of $S$ in $V^\star$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} S^0 = \sb{\theta\in V^\star}{\theta(s) = 0 \text{ for all } s\in S}. \end{align*} $$

Lemma (properties of annihilators) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, and $S, T\subseteq V$.

  1. $S^0$ $\leq$ $V^\star$.

  2. If $S\subseteq T$, then $T^0 \leq S^0$.

  3. $S^0 =$ $\langle S\rangle$$^0$.

  4. $\set{\v 0_V}^0 = V^\star$ and $V^0 = \set{\v 0_{V^\star}}$.

Proof

Proposition (annilhilator and complement, dimension of annilhilator) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, and $U, W \leq V$.

If $V = U$ $\oplus$ $W$, then $U^0$ $\isom$ $W^\star$.

In particular, if $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $$ \begin{align*} \dim(V) = \dim(U) + \dim(U^0). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (sums and annilhilators) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, and $U,W\leq V$.

  1. $(U$ $+$ $W)$$^0$ $= U^0 \cap W^0$.

  2. $U^0 + W^0 \leq (U\cap W)^0$.

  3. If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then we have equality in 2., i.e. $U^0 + W^0 = (U\cap W)^0$.

Proof

  • Equality holds in 2. for any vector space if we assume the axiom of choice.

Definition (dual map) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be vector spaces, and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. The dual map of $\alpha$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} \alpha^\star: W^\star &\to V^\star \\ \theta &\mapsto \theta\circ\alpha. \end{align*} $$

Illustration

Lemma (properties of dual maps) open_in_new

Let $U,V,W$ be vector spaces, $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$ and $\beta\in \mathcal{L}(U,V)$.

  1. $\alpha^\star$ is linear.

  2. The function $\mathcal{L}(V,W) \to \mathcal{L}(W^\star, V^\star)$ given by $\gamma\mapsto \gamma^\star$ is linear.

  3. $(\alpha\circ\beta)^\star = \beta^\star\circ\alpha^\star$.

  4. If $\alpha$ is an isomorphism, then so is $\alpha^\star$, and $(\alpha^\star)\inv = (\alpha\inv)^\star$.

Proof

Proposition (matrix of dual maps) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces, and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Further let $B$ be a basis for $V$ and $C$ be a basis for $W$. Then $$ \begin{align*} [\alpha^\star]^{C^\star}_{B^\star} = \big([\alpha]^B_C\big)^T. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Corollary (rank of dual map) open_in_new

Let $\alpha$ as in here. Then $\op{rk}($$\alpha^\star$$) = \op{rk}(\alpha)$.

Proof

Proposition (kernel and image of dual maps) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces and $\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,W)$. Then

  1. $\ker(\alpha^\star) = \im(\alpha)^0$;

  2. $\im(\alpha^\star) \leq \ker(\alpha)^0$;

  3. If $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional, then we have equality in 2., i.e. $\im(\alpha^\star) = \ker(\alpha)^0$.

Proof

Double dual open_in_new

Definition (double dual) open_in_new

For a vector space $V$, the double dual of $V$ is the dual space of the dual space of $V$: $$ \begin{align*} V^{\star\star} = (V^\star)^\star. \end{align*} $$

For $V$ finite-dimensional, it is common to identify $V^{\star\star}=V$: see here.

Theorem (double dual and evaluation) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space.

The evaluation map $\mathcal{E}: V\to V^{\star\star}$ given by $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E}(v)(\theta) = \theta(v) \end{align*} $$ is a linear map.

If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then $\mathcal{E}$ is an isomorphism.

Proof

  • $\mathcal{E}$ is also called the natural isomorphism, because it does not require a basis.

  • It is common to identify $\mathcal{E}(V) = V$, so we write $V^{\star\star}=V$.

Proposition (double annihilator) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space and $U\leq V$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E}(U) = U^{00}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • Identifying $\mathcal{E}(U) = U$, we write $U = U^{00}$.

  • For $X\subseteq V^\star$, $X^0 \leq V^{\star\star}$ is identified with $$ \begin{align*} X_0 = \mathcal{E}\inv(X^0) = \sb{v\in V}{\theta(v) = 0 \text{ for all }\theta\in X} = \bigcap_{\theta\in X} \ker(\theta). \end{align*} $$ Then every $U\leq V$ can be written as $U = (U^0)_0$.
