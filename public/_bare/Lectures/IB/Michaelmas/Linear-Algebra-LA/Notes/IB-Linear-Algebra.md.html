Vector spaces open_in_new

Definition (vector space) open_in_new

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a field.

An $\mathbb{F}$-vector space (or a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$) is a abelian group $(V, +, \v 0)$, equipped with a function (scalar multiplication) $$ \begin{align*} \mathbb{F}\times V &\to V \\ (\lambda, v) & \mapsto \lambda v \end{align*} $$ satisfying, for all $v,w\in V$ and $\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{F}$,

  • Distributive law:

    1. $(\lambda+\mu)v = \lambda v + \mu v$;

    2. $\lambda(v + w) = \lambda v + \lambda w$;

  1. Associative law: $(\lambda\mu) v = \lambda(\mu v)$;

  2. $1 v = v$.


  • A vector space over $\mathbb{F}$ is a $\mathbb{F}$-module.

  • $0v = \v 0$ for all $v\in V$. (Derivation)

  • $(-1)v = -v$ for all $v\in V$. (Derivation)

Definition (vector subspace) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{F}$-vector space.

A subset $U\subseteq V$ is said to be a subspace of $V$ (denoted as $U\leq V$) if it is a $\mathbb{F}$-vector space under the same operations as $V$, i.e. $(U, +)$ is a subgroup of $(V, +)$, and $\lambda u\in U$ for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$ and $u\in U$.

A subset $U\subseteq V$ is a subspace of a vector space $V$ iff

  1. $U$ is non-empty;

  2. for all $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$ and $u,w\in U$, we have $u+\lambda w \in U$.

  • Intersection of subspaces is also a subspace: if $U,W\leq V$, then $U\cap W\leq V$. (Derivation)

  • Union of subspaces is almost never a subspace.

Definition (sum of vector subspaces) open_in_new

Let $U,W$ $\leq$ $V$ where $V$ is a vector space.

Define the sum of $U$ and $W$ in $V$ as $$ \begin{align*} U + W = \sb{u+w}{u\in U, w\in W} \end{align*} $$

  • Sums are also subspaces. (Derivation)

  • $U+W$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ containing both $U$ and $W$.

Linear maps open_in_new

Definition (linear map) open_in_new

Let $V, W$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces.

A function $\alpha: V\to W$ is said to be a linear map if it preserves vector addition and scalar multiplication, i.e. for all $u,v\in V$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$, $$ \begin{align*} \alpha(u+v) = \alpha(u) + \alpha(v) \quad \text{and} \quad \alpha(\lambda u) = \lambda \alpha(u). \end{align*} $$

Equivalently, if for all $u,v\in V$ and $\lambda\in\mathbb{F}$, $$ \begin{align*} \alpha(u+\lambda v) = \alpha(u) + \lambda \alpha(v). \end{align*} $$

Examples of linear maps

Composition of linear maps is also linear.

Definition (isomorphism of vector spaces) open_in_new

A bijective linear map $\alpha: V\to W$ is called an isomorphism.

We say $V$ are $W$ are isomorphic (denoted as $V\cong W$) if there exists an isomorphism from $V$ to $W$.

Proposition (inverse of isomorphism is an isomorphism) open_in_new

If $\alpha: V\to W$ is an isomorphism, then so is $\alpha\inv: W\to V$.

Proof

Definition (kernel and image of a linear map) open_in_new

Let $\alpha: V\to W$ be a linear map. Define

  • $\ker(\alpha) = \sb{v\in V}{\alpha(v) = \v 0_W}$ as the kernel of $\alpha$;

  • $\im(\alpha) = \sb{\alpha(v)}{v\in V}$ as the image of $\alpha$.

  • $\ker(\alpha) \leq V$ and $\im(\alpha) \leq W$. (Derivation)

  • $\alpha$ is surjective iff $\im(\alpha) = W$.

  • $\alpha$ is injective iff $\ker(\alpha) = \set{\v 0_V}$. (Derivation)

Definition (quotient space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $U\leq V$.

The quotient space of $V$ by $U$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} V/U := \sb{v + U}{v\in V}, \end{align*} $$ the set of left cosets of $(U,+)$ in $(V,+)$.

$V/U$ is an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space under the operations $$ \begin{align*} (v_1 + U) + (v_2 + U) &= (v_1+v_2) + U, \\ \lambda(u_1 + U) &= (\lambda u_1) + U. \end{align*} $$ Proof

Proposition (map to quotient space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space and $U\leq V$.

There exists a surjective linear map $$ \begin{align*} \pi_U : V &\to V/U \\ v &\mapsto v + U \end{align*} $$ with a kernel $\ker(\pi_U) = U$.

Proof

Bases open_in_new

Definition (linear combination, span) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $S\subseteq V$.

  • A linear combination of $S$ is an expression of the form $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{s\in S}' \lambda_s s. \end{align*} $$ We assume $\lambda_s = 0$ for all but finitely many $s\in S$ ($\sum'$ denotes finite sum).

  • Define the span of $S$ in $V$ be the set of all linear combinations of $S$ $$ \begin{align*} \langle S\rangle = \Big\{\sum_{s\in S}' \lambda_s s \ \Big|\ \lambda_s \in \mathbb{F}\Big\}. \end{align*} $$ We define $\langle \emptyset\rangle = \set{\v 0_V}$ by convention.

  • If $\langle S\rangle = V$, then we say $S$ spans $V$, or $S$ is a spanning set for $V$.

Definition (finite-dimensional vector space) open_in_new

A vector space $V$ is said to be finite-dimensional if $V$ has a finite spanning set.

  • $\langle S\rangle$ is the smallest subspace of $V$ that contains $S$. (Derivation)

  • $\langle S\cup T\rangle = \langle S\rangle + \langle T\rangle$.

Definition (linear independence) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $S\subseteq V$.

$S$ is said to be linearly independent if for all $\lambda_s \in \mathbb{F}$, $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{s\in S}' \lambda_s s = \v 0 \quad \implies \quad \lambda_s = 0 \text{ for all } s\in S. \end{align*} $$

$S$ is said to be linearly dependent if it is not linearly independent.

  • If $\v 0\in S$, then $S$ is linearly dependent.

  • If $S$ is linearly independent, then any subset of $S$ is linearly independent.

  • If $S$ is infinite, $S$ is linearly independent iff every finite subset of $S$ is linearly independent.

Definition (basis) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space and $S\subseteq V$.

$S$ is said to be a basis for $V$ if

  1. $S$ is linearly independent; and

  2. $S$ spans $V$.

Theorem (Steinitz exchange lemma) open_in_new

Let $S, T \subseteq V$ be finite subsets in a vector space $V$. Suppose $S$ is linearly independent and $T$ spans $V$. Then

  1. $|S| \leq |T|$; and

  2. there exists a subset $T'\subseteq T$ such that $|T'| = |T| - |S|$ and $S\cup T'$ spans $V$. (This means you can remove $|S|$ elements from $T$, replace them with $S$, and obtain a set that still spans $V$.)

Proof

The picture is $$ \begin{align*} \text{linearly independent set} \quad \subseteq \quad \text{basis} \quad \subseteq \quad \text{spanning set}. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (finite spanning set contains basis) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, $S\subseteq V$ is finite and $S$ spans $V$.

Then there exists a subset $S' \subseteq S$ such that $S'$ is a basis of $V$.

Proof

Definition (dimension of a finite-dimensional vector space) open_in_new

The dimension $\dim(V)$ of a finite-dimensional vector space $V$ is defined as the size of a basis for $V$.

For this to be well-defined, we rely on the fact that

Definition (external direct sum) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces.

The external direct sum of $V$ and $W$ (denoted as $V\oplus W$) is defined as the $\mathbb{F}$-vector space with the underlying set $V\times W$ with operations $$ \begin{align*} (v_1,w_1) + (v_2,w_2) &= (v_1 + v_2, w_1+w_2) \\ \lambda(v,w) &= (\lambda v, \lambda w). \end{align*} $$ We similarly define $V_1\oplus \ldots\oplus V_n$.

Lemma (dimension of external direct sum) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces.

If $V$ and $W$ are finite-dimensional, then so is $V \oplus W$, $$ \begin{align*} \dim(V\oplus W) = \dim(V) + \dim(W). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space, and let $U,W$ $\leq$ $V$.

Then $U+W$ is finite-dimensional, and $$ \begin{align*} \dim(U+W) = \dim(U) + \dim(W) - \dim(U\cap W). \end{align*} $$ Proof

Definition (internal direct sum) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space, and $U,W$ $\leq$ $V$.

We say $V$ is the (internal) direct sum of $U$ and $W$ (denoted as $U\oplus W = V$) if, equivalently,

  • $U$ $+$ $W = V$ and $U\cap W = \set{\v 0}$;

  • the linear map $\phi: U$ $\oplus$ $W \to V$ given by $\phi(u,w) = u+w$ is an isomorphism;

  • every element of $V$ can be uniquely expressed as $u+w$ where $u\in U$ and $w \in W$.

Definition (direct complement) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space and let $U,W\leq V$.

We say $W$ is a direct complement to $U$ in $V$ if $V = U$ $\oplus$ $W$.

Definition (direct sum of many subspaces) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a vector space and $U_1, U_2, \ldots, U_n \leq V$.

We say $V$ is the direct sum of $U_i$ (denoted as $V = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n U_i$) if, equivalently,

  • $\phi : U_1$ $\oplus$ $U_2 \oplus \ldots \oplus U_n \to V$ given by $(u_1, u_2, \ldots, u_n) \mapsto u_1 + u_2 + \ldots + u_n$ is an isomorphism;

  • every $v\in V$ can be uniquely expressed as $u_1 + u_2 + \ldots + u_n$ for $u_i\in U_i$.

Note that this is not equivalent to saying $V = U_1+\ldots+U_n$ and $U_i\cap U_j = \set{\v 0}$ for all $i\neq j$.

Proposition (every subspace of a finite-dimensional vector space has a direct complement) open_in_new

Then every subspace of $V$ has a direct complement.

Definition (equivalent matrices) open_in_new

Two $m\times n$ matrices $A, A'$ are said to be equivalent if there exists invertible $m\times m$ matrix $P$ and $n\times n$ matrix $Q$ such that $$ \begin{align*} A' = PAQ. \end{align*} $$

Elementary operations on matrices open_in_new

Definition (elementary matrices) open_in_new

There are three types of elementary matrices:

  1. identity with row $i$ and $j$ swapped $$ \begin{align*} T_{i,j} = \pmat{1 \\ &\ddots \\ && 0 && 1 \\ &&& \ddots \\ && 1 && 0 \\ &&&&&\ddots \\ &&&&&&1}, \end{align*} $$

  2. identity with the $i$-th diagonal entry replaced with $\lambda\neq 0$ $$ \begin{align*} E_{i,\lambda} = \pmat{1 \\ &\ddots \\ &&1 \\ &&&\lambda \\ &&&& 1 \\ &&&&& \ddots \\ &&&&&& 1}, \end{align*} $$

  3. identity with $\lambda$ in an off-diagonal entry $(i,j)$ $$ \begin{align*} C_{i,j,\lambda} = \pmat{1 \\ &\ddots \\ &&1 \\ &&& \ddots \\ &&\lambda &&1 \\ &&&&& \ddots \\ &&&&&& 1}. \end{align*} $$

Definition (elementary operations) open_in_new

Let $A\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F})$. Write $\v c_i$ and $\v r_i$ to denote the $i$-th column and row.

There are three types of elementary column operations on $A$:

  1. Right multiply $T_{i,j}$, which swaps $\v c_i$ and $\v c_j$;

  2. Right multiply $E_{i,\lambda}$, which replaces $\v c_i$ by $\lambda \v c_i$ (where $0\neq \lambda\in \mathbb{F}$);

  3. Right multiply $C_{i,j,\lambda}$, which replaces $\v c_j$ by $\v c_j + \lambda\v c_i$ (where $i\neq j$).

There are three types of elementary row operations on $A$:

  1. Left multiply $T_{i,j}$, which swaps $\v r_i$ and $\v r_j$;

  2. Left multiply $E_{i,\lambda}$, which replaces $\v r_i$ by $\lambda \v r_i$ (where $0\neq \lambda\in \mathbb{F}$);

  3. Left multiply $C_{i,j,\lambda}$, which replaces $\v r_i$ by $\v r_i + \lambda\v r_j$ (where $i\neq j$).

(Note the different indices when left and right multiplying $C_{i,j,\lambda}$.)

  • Elementary matrices are invertible with elementary inverses.

  • Elementary row/column operations preserve the row/column space, and therefore the row/column ranks.

Definition (row and column-reduced echelon form) open_in_new

A matrix $A\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ is said to be in row-reduced echelon form if

  1. every non-zero row of $A$ appears above every zero row;

  2. in a non-zero row, the leftmost non-zero entry (the pivot entry) is 1;

  3. for two non-zero rows $\v r_i$ and $\v r_j$ where $i<j$, the pivot entry in $\v r_j$ is strictly to the right of that of $\v r_i$; and

  4. each pivot entry is the only non-zero entry in its column.

$A$ is said to be in column-reduced echelon form if $A^T$ is in row-reduced echelon form.

Lemma (rank of matrices in row-reduced echelon form) open_in_new

If a matrix $A$ is in row-reduced echelon form, then the row rank of $A$ is the number of non-zero rows.

Proof

Proposition (unique row-reduced echelon form) open_in_new

Every matrix can be put uniquely into row-reduced echelon form via elementary row operations.

Gaussian elimination and elementary row operations open_in_new

For some $A\in M_{m\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ and $\v b\in \mathbb{F}^m$, to solve $$ \begin{align*} A\v x = \v b,\qquad \v x\in \mathbb{F}^n, \end{align*} $$

  1. form the augmented matrix $\big(\begin{array}{c|c}A & \v b\end{array}\big)$;

  2. put into row-reduced echelon form $\big(\begin{array}{c|c}A' & \v b'\end{array}\big)$ (and $A'$ is in row-reduced echelon form as well);

  3. then solve $A'\v x = \v b'$.

(Derivation)

Solution exists iff $$ \begin{align*} \v b \in \im(A) = \op{Col}(A) &\iff \op{rk}(A) = \op{rk}(\big(\begin{array}{c|c}A & \v b\end{array}\big)) \\ &\iff \op{rk}(A') = \op{rk}(\big(\begin{array}{c|c}A' & \v b'\end{array}\big)). \end{align*} $$

If $\v x_0$ is a solution, then the solution set is $\v x_0 + \ker(A)$. The solution is unique iff $$ \begin{align*} \ker(A) = \set{\v 0} &\iff \op{rk}(A) = n \\ &\iff A' = \pmat{I_n \\ 0}. \end{align*} $$

  1. multilinear in columns: for any $\v v_1, \ldots, \v v_n \in \mathbb{F}$, the function $$ \begin{align*} \mathbb{F}^n &\to \mathbb{F} \\ {\color{yellow}\v v} &\mapsto F\big(\pmat{\v v_1 & \ldots & \v v_{i-1} & {\color{yellow}\v v} & \v v_{i+1} & \ldots \v v_n}\big) \end{align*} $$ is a linear map for all $1\leq i\leq n$.

  2. alternating: if $A$ has two columns that are the same, then $F(A) = 0$.

Lemma (properties of volume forms) open_in_new

Let $A\in M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$ and $F$ be an $n$-dimensional volume form on $M_{n\times n}(\mathbb{F})$.

  1. If $A$ has a zero column, then $F(A) = 0$.

  2. $F(A\cdot$ $T_{ij}$$) = -F(A)$.

  3. $F(A\cdot$ $M_{i,\lambda}$$) = \lambda F(A)$.

  4. $F(A\cdot$ $C_{i,j,\lambda}$$) = F(A)$.

Proof

Theorem (determinant as unique volume form) open_in_new

For each $n\in \N$, there is a unique $n$-dimensional volume form $\det$ on $M_{n\times n(\mathbb{F})}$ satisfying $F(I_n) = 1$, given by $$ \begin{align*} \det(A) = \sum_{\sigma\in S_n} \sign(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^n a_{\sigma(i), i} \end{align*} $$ called the $n$-dimensional determinant. Here $S_n$ is the $n$-th symmetric group and $\sign: S_n \to (\set{\pm 1}, \cdot)$ is the sign homomorphism.

This is called Leibniz formula for determinants.

Definition (polynomial, degree, monic, polynomial function, root) open_in_new

Let $\mathbb{F}$ be a field.

  • A polynomial over $\mathbb{F}$ is a formal expression $a_nt^n + \ldots + a_1 t + a_0$ for some $n=0,1,2,\ldots$ and coefficients $a_i\in \mathbb{F}$.

  • The degree of $f$ (denoted $\deg(f)$) is the maximal integer $m$ such that $a_m\neq 0$. By convention, $\deg(0) = -\infty$.

  • $a_m$ is called the leading coefficient. $f$ is called monic if $a_m = 1$.

  • Let $\mathbb{F}[t]$ be the set of all polynomials of $\mathbb{F}$, equipped with addition and multiplication: for $f(t) = \sum_{i} ' a_i t^i$ and $g(t) = \sum_{j} ' b_j t^j$,

    • the coefficient of $t^k$ in $(f+g)$ is $a_k+b_k$;

    • the coefficient of $t^k$ in $(f\cdot g)$ is $\sum_{i=0}^{k} a_i b_{k-i}$.

  • The polynomial function $\mathbb{F}\to \mathbb{F}$ is defined by$f(\lambda) = \sum_{i} ' a_i \lambda^i$.

  • $\lambda\in \mathbb{F}$ is a root of $f\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ if $f(\lambda) = 0$.

  • We write $g\mid f$ if there exists $q\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ such that $f(t) = q(t) g(t)$.

Lemma (Euclidean algorithm for polynomials) open_in_new

For all $f,g\in$ $\mathbb{F}[t]$ with $g\neq 0$, there exists $q,r\in \mathbb{F}[t]$ such that $$ \begin{align*} f(t) = q(t) g(t) + r(t) \quad \text{and} \quad \deg(r) < \deg(g). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Corollary (Bézout identity for polynomials) open_in_new

Proof

Corollary (uniqueness of polynomials agreeing on points) open_in_new

If $\deg(f_1), \deg(f_2) < n$ and $f_1(\lambda_i) = f_2(\lambda_i)$ for some distinct $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n\in \mathbb{F}$, then $f_1 = f_2$.

Bilinear forms and sesquilinear forms open_in_new

Definition (bilinear form) open_in_new

Let $U$ and $V$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces.

A function $\phi: U\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ is called a bilinear form if for all $u_0\in U$ and $v_0\in V$, both $$ \begin{align*} V &\to \mathbb{F} &&& U &\to \mathbb{F} \\ v &\mapsto \phi(u_0, v) &&\text{and} & u &\mapsto \phi(u, v_0) \end{align*} $$ are linear maps.

Definition (sesquilinear form) open_in_new

Let $U$ and $V$ be $\mathbb{C}$-vector spaces.

A function $\phi: U\times V\to \mathbb{C}$ is called a sesquilinear form if it is

  • linear in the first argument: $\phi(\lambda u + u', v) = \lambda\phi(u, v) + \phi(u', v)$ for all $\lambda\in \C, u,u'\in U, v\in V$; and

  • conjugate-linear in the second argument: $\phi(u, \mu v + v') = \overline{\mu}\phi(u,v) + \phi(u,v')$ for all $\mu\in \C, u\in U, v,v'\in V$.

Definition (symmetric bilinear form) open_in_new

A bilinear form $\phi:V\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ is said to be symmetric if for all $u,v\in V$, $\phi(u,v) = \phi(v,u)$.

Definition (Hermitian sesquilinear form) open_in_new

A sesquilinear form $\phi: V\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ is said to be Hermitian if for all $u,v\in V$, $\phi(u,v) = \overline{\phi(v,u)}$.

Proposition (matrix of bilinear and sesquilinear forms) open_in_new

Let $U$ and $V$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces with bases $B = \set{b_1, \ldots, b_n} \subseteq U$ and $C = \set{c_1, \ldots, c_m} \subseteq V$.

  • For any bilinear form $\phi$, the matrix of $\phi$ with respect to $B$ and $C$ $$ \begin{align*} [\phi]_{B,C} = (\phi(b_i, c_j))_{i,j} \in M_{n\times m}(\mathbb{F}) \end{align*} $$ is the unique matrix satisfying $$ \begin{align*} ([u]_B)^T\, [\phi]_{B,C} \,[v]_C = \phi(u, v) \qquad \text{for all }u\in U, v\in V. \end{align*} $$

  • For any sesquilinear form $\phi$, $[\phi]_{B,C}$ defined the same way is the unique matrix satisfyiing $$ \begin{align*} ([u]_B)^T \, [\phi]_{B,C} \, \overline{[v]_C} = \phi(u,v) \qquad \text{for all } u\in U, v\in V. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • For $U=V$ and $B=C$, we write $[\phi]_B \equiv [\phi]_{B,B}$.

Proposition (change of bases of bilinear and sesquilinear form) open_in_new

Let $U$ and $V$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces. Suppose $B,B'$ are bases for $U$ and $C,C'$ are bases for $V$.

  • For a bilinear form $\phi$, $$ \begin{align*} [\phi]_{B',C'} = ([\id_U]^{B'}_B)^T \, [\phi]_{B,C} \, [\id_V]^{C'}_C. \end{align*} $$

  • For a sesquilinear form $\phi$, $$ \begin{align*} [\phi]_{B',C'} = ([\id_U]^{B'}_B)^T \, [\phi]_{B,C} \, \overline{[\id_V]^{C'}_C}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (matrix of symmetric bilinear and Hermitian sesquilinear form) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space.

  • A bilinear form $\phi$ is symmetric iff $[\phi]_{B}$ is symmetric (i.e. $[\phi]_{B} = ([\phi]_B)^T$) for some (equivalently all) bases $B$ for $V$.

  • A sesquilinear form $\phi$ is Hermitian iff $[\phi]_{B}$ is Hermitian (i.e. $[\phi]_{B} = \overline{([\phi]_B)^T}$) for some (equivalently all) bases $B$ for $V$.

  • Let $V$ be a $\C$-vector space and $\phi$ be a Hermitian sesquilinear form on $V$.

Proof

Definition (inner product space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a real (resp. complex) vector space.

An inner product on $V$ is a bilinear form (resp. sesquilinear form) $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on $V$ that is

  • symmetric (resp. Hermitian); and

  • positive definite.

$(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is called an inner product space.

Definition (norm in inner product space) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space.

Define the norm of a vector $v\in V$ as $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle} \geq 0$.

  • $\|v\| = 0$ iff $v=\v 0$.

  • $\|\cdot\|: V\to\R$ uniquely determines $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ by the polarisation identity.

Lemma (Cauchy-Schwarz) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} |\langle u,v\rangle| \leq \|u\| \,\|v\|. \end{align*} $$ Equality occurs iff $\set{u,v}$ spans a subspace of dimensions $\leq 1$.

Proof

This shows $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle: V\times V \to V$ is continuous with respect to the norm topology.

Corollary (triangle inequality) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} \|u+v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Therefore any inner product space $V$ is a normed space.

Definition (orthogonal, orthonormal set) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. A subset $E\subseteq V$ is said to be

  • orthogonal if $\langle d,e\rangle = 0$ for all $d,e\in E$ with $d\neq e$; and

  • orthonormal if $\langle d,e\rangle = \delta_{d,e}$ for all $d,e\in E$.

If a subset $E\subseteq V$ is orthogonal and $\v 0 \not\in E$, then

  1. $E$ is linearly independent; and

  2. for all $v\in \langle E\rangle$, $$ \begin{align*} v = \sum_{e\in E} ' \frac{\langle v,e\rangle}{\langle e,e\rangle} e. \end{align*} $$

Theorem (Gram-Schmidt process) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an inner product space.

For any linearly independent (finite or infinite) sequence $v_1, v_2, \ldots\in V$, there exists an orthonormal sequence $e_1, e_2, \ldots \in V$ such that $$ \begin{align*} \langle e_1, \ldots, e_k\rangle = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_k\rangle \qquad \text{for all } k. \end{align*} $$ Moreover if $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_k}$ for some $k$ is already orthonormal, then $e_i = v_i$ for $i\leq k$.

Proof

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then performing Gram-Schmidt on a basis produces an orthonormal basis.

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $E\subseteq V$ is an orthonormal set, then we can extend to a basis for $V$ and perform Gram-Schmidt to obtain an orthonormal basis for $V$ containing $E$.
