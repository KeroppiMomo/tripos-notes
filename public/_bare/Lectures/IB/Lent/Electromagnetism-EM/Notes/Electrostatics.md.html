Electrostatics studies the electric field generated by a stationary charge distribution.

Gauss's law open_in_new

For a volume $V$ with boundary $S = \partial V$, Gauss's law states that $$ \begin{align*} \oint_S \v E\cdot \d \v S = \frac{Q}{ε_0} \end{align*} $$ where $Q = \int_{V} ρ \d V$ is the total charge enclosed. This is obtained from (M1) and divergence theorem.

Spherically symmetric charge distribution open_in_new

For a spherically symmetric charge distribution $ρ(r)$ contained within $r\leq R$ (in spherical polars), $$ \begin{align*} \v E(r) = \frac{Q}{4πε_0 r^2}, \qquad r>R. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

In the limit $R\to 0$, we have a point charge $Q$, corresponding to $$ \begin{align*} ρ(\v x) = Q\, δ(\v x). \end{align*} $$

The Coulomb force is the corresponding Lorentz force. $$ \begin{align*} \v F =q\v E = \frac{Qq}{4πε_0 r^2} \v e_r, \end{align*} $$ similar to gravity, but it can be both repulsive and attractive, and is much stronger.

Cylindrically symmetric charge distribution open_in_new

For a cylindrically symmetric charge distribution $ρ(r)$ contained within $r\leq R$ (in cylindrical polars), $$ \begin{align*} \v E(r) = \frac{λ}{2πε_0 r}, \qquad r>R, \end{align*} $$ where $λ$ is the charge per unit length. (Derivation)

In the limit $R\to 0$, we have a line charge $λ$, corresponding to $$ \begin{align*} ρ(\v x) = λ\,δ(x)\,δ(y). \end{align*} $$

Planar symmetric charge distribution open_in_new

Consider a planar charge distribution $ρ(z)$ (in Cartesian coordinates), contained within $-d<z<d$. Further assume reflectional symmetry $ρ(z) = ρ(-z)$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \v E(z) = \sign(z) \frac{σ}{2ε_0} \v e_z, \end{align*} $$ where $σ$ is the charge per unit area. (Derivation)

In the limit $d\to 0$, we have a surface charge $σ$, corresponding to $$ \begin{align*} ρ = σ\, δ(z). \end{align*} $$

Surface charge open_in_new

At any point on a charged surface, let $\v n$ be the unit normal vector, pointing from region 1 to 2.

The normal and tangential components of the electric fields have discontinuities $$ \begin{align*} [\v n\cdot \v E] ={}& \frac{σ}{ε_0}, \\ [\v n \times \v E] ={}& 0, \end{align*} $$ where $[X] = X_2 - X_1$. (Derivation)

Electrostatic potential open_in_new

By $∇\times \v E = 0$, now the electrostatic potential $\v Φ$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \v E = -∇Φ \end{align*} $$

The potential difference or voltage between two points $\v x_1$ and $\v x_2$ is $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x_2)-Φ(\v x_1) = \int_{\v x_1}^{\v x_2} \d Φ = -\int_{\v x_1}^{\v x_2} \v E(\v x) \cdot \d \v x \end{align*} $$ independent of path.

Adding an arbitrary constant to $Φ$ has no physical effect. We usually choose $Φ\to 0$ as $|\v x|\to\infty$, but this is only possible if $ρ(\v x)$ decays sufficiently rapidly. E.g. for a line charge, $Φ(r)\propto \ln r$.

(M1) implies $Φ$ satisfies the Poisson equation $$ \begin{align*} -∇^2Φ = \frac{ρ}{ε_0}, \end{align*} $$ which has an integral solution (assuming $Φ\to 0$ as $|\v x|\to \infty$) $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0} \int_{\R^3} \frac{ρ(\v x')}{|\v x'-\v x|} \d ^3\v x'. \end{align*} $$

This is the convolution of $ρ(\v x)$ with potential of a unit point charge at the origin $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0 |\v x|} \qquad \text{solving } {-∇^2Φ} = \frac{δ(\v x)}{ε_0}. \end{align*} $$

Electric dipole open_in_new

Let $\v d$ to be the vector from a $-q$ charge to a $+q$ charge. Define the dipole moment as $$ \begin{align*} \v p = q\v d. \end{align*} $$

Force on electric dipole open_in_new

Torque $$ \begin{align*} \v G = \v d\times(q\v E) = \v p\times \v E. \end{align*} $$

Force $$ \begin{align*} \v F = q\v E(\v r + \v d) - q\v E(\v r) \approx q(\v d\cdot\nabla)\v E = (\v p\cdot\nabla)\v E. \end{align*} $$

Potential and field due to electric dipole open_in_new

For $|\v x|\gg|\v d|$, the electric potential and field due to the dipole is $$ \begin{align*} \Phi(\v x) &= \frac{\v p\cdot \v x}{4\pi\epsilon_0 |\v x|^3} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}, \\ \v E(\v r) &= \frac{3(\v p\cdot \v x)\v x - |\v x|^2 \v p}{4πε_0 |\v x|^5} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat r + \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat\theta \end{align*} $$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\v x$ and $\v p$. (Derivation) Note that $\Phi\propto 1/r^2, E\propto 1/r^3$.

Therefore on axis ($\theta = 0$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ parallel to axis} \end{align*} $$ and off axis ($\theta = \pi/2$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ anti-parallel to axis}. \end{align*} $$

A point dipole $\v p$ at the origin correspond to $$ \begin{align*} ρ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇δ(\v x) \end{align*} $$ so by linearity of Poisson's equation, $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇\left(\frac{1}{4πε_0 |\v x|}\right). \end{align*} $$

In atoms and molecules, external electric fields causes polarisation and induces $\v p\propto \v E_\text{ext}$. This gives long-range forces between them:

  • Dipole-dipole: dipoles line up

  • Dipole-induced dipole

  • Fluctuating dipoles: dipoles synchronise and fluctuate in the same direction.

Multipole expansion for electric potentials open_in_new

For a general charge distribution $ρ(\v x)$ contained within $V = \sb{\v x}{|\v x|<R}$, the potential is, at large $|\v x|\gg R$, $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0}\left(\frac{Q}{|\v x|} + \frac{\v p\cdot \v x}{|\v x|^3} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{Q_{ij} x_i x_j}{|\v x|^5} + \ldots\right) \end{align*} $$ where the first three multipole moments are

  • total charge (electric monopole moment, scalar) $$ \begin{align*} Q = \int_{V} ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x, \end{align*} $$

  • electric dipole moment (vector) $$ \begin{align*} \v p = \int_{V} \v x\,ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x, \end{align*} $$

  • electric quadruple moment (traceless second-order symmetric tensor) $$ \begin{align*} Q_{ij} = \int_{V} (3x_i x_j - |\v x|^2 δ_{ij})\,ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Electrostatic energy open_in_new

The work done against the electric force $\v F = q\v E$ in bringing a particle of charge $q$ from infinity (where $Φ = 0$) to $\v x$ is $$ \begin{align*} W = -\int_{\infty}^{\v x} \v F\cdot \d \v x = q \int_{\infty}^{\v x} ∇Φ\cdot \d \v x = q\, Φ(\v x). \end{align*} $$

The total work done to assemble a configuration of $N$ charges is $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\neq i}}^{N} \frac{q_i q_j}{4πε_0 |\v x_j - \v x_i|}. \end{align*} $$ For a continuous charge distribution, occupying a finite volume $V$, $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int_{V} ρ Φ \d V = \int_{\R^3} \frac{1}{2}ε_0 |\v E|^2\d^3 \v x. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

We can interpret this as the energy stored in the electric field.

Our expressions for $U$ suggest the self-energy of a point charge is infinite. Discard this as it is unchanging and causes no force.

Conductors open_in_new

Charges can move freely on conductors. Therefore in steady state, $\v E = \v 0$ inside conductor, $\v E\perp$ surface, and so $\Phi$ is constant everywhere on the conductor. Otherwise free charges would move in response to the electric force and a current would flow.

Charges redistribute to stay away from each other, so they go to the surface. Therefore external electric fields induce charges on the surface. If there is charges inside, taking a Gaussian surface around that charge gives $\v E\neq 0$, which is impossible.

Inside a hollow conductor, using a Gaussian surface containing the cavity, we get that the net charge is zero. If there is unbalanced charges, there will be field lines so there is potential difference. But the inside surface is equipotential, so there can't be any charges and $\v E = 0$.

A surface charge of $\sigma$ gives a electric field immediately outside the surface $$ \begin{align*} \v E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} \vhat n. \end{align*} $$

Capacitor open_in_new

For two parallel conducting plates with charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, the potential between them satisfies $$ \begin{align*} Q = CV \end{align*} $$ where $C$ is the capacitance, with unit farad $\mathrm{F} = \mathrm{C\ V\inv}$.

  • Parallel plate capacitor with area $A$ and separation $d$ $$ \begin{align*} C = \frac{A\epsilon_0}{d} \end{align*} $$

  • Spherical conductor with radius $a$ (the other charges are distributed infinitely far away) $$ \begin{align*} C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 a \end{align*} $$

  • Cylindrical capacitor with length $l$, inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$ $$ \begin{align*} \frac{C}{l} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}. \end{align*} $$

Energy of capacitor open_in_new

Energy stored in the capacitor = work done to charge up the capacitor $$ \begin{align*} W = \int_{0}^{Q} \frac{q}{C}\, dq = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{1}{2}CV^2. \end{align*} $$

This can also be thought of energy stored in the electric field $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int ρΦ \d V = \frac{1}{2}(+Q)Φ_+ + \frac{1}{2}(-Q)Φ_- = \frac{1}{2}QV. \end{align*} $$

This is the stress (pressure) on the plates caused by the field (e.g. from direct calculation or using virtual work argument by pulling apart the plates).
