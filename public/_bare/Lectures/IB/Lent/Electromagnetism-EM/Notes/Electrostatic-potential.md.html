By $∇\times \v E = 0$, now the electrostatic potential $\v Φ$ satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \v E = -∇Φ \end{align*} $$

The potential difference or voltage between two points $\v x_1$ and $\v x_2$ is $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x_2)-Φ(\v x_1) = \int_{\v x_1}^{\v x_2} \d Φ = -\int_{\v x_1}^{\v x_2} \v E(\v x) \cdot \d \v x \end{align*} $$ independent of path.

Adding an arbitrary constant to $Φ$ has no physical effect. We usually choose $Φ\to 0$ as $|\v x|\to\infty$, but this is only possible if $ρ(\v x)$ decays sufficiently rapidly. E.g. for a line charge, $Φ(r)\propto \ln r$.

(M1) implies $Φ$ satisfies the Poisson equation $$ \begin{align*} -∇^2Φ = \frac{ρ}{ε_0}, \end{align*} $$ which has an integral solution (assuming $Φ\to 0$ as $|\v x|\to \infty$) $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0} \int_{\R^3} \frac{ρ(\v x')}{|\v x'-\v x|} \d ^3\v x'. \end{align*} $$

This is the convolution of $ρ(\v x)$ with potential of a unit point charge at the origin $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0 |\v x|} \qquad \text{solving } {-∇^2Φ} = \frac{δ(\v x)}{ε_0}. \end{align*} $$

Electric dipole open_in_new

Let $\v d$ to be the vector from a $-q$ charge to a $+q$ charge. Define the dipole moment as $$ \begin{align*} \v p = q\v d. \end{align*} $$

Force on electric dipole open_in_new

Torque $$ \begin{align*} \v G = \v d\times(q\v E) = \v p\times \v E. \end{align*} $$

Force $$ \begin{align*} \v F = q\v E(\v r + \v d) - q\v E(\v r) \approx q(\v d\cdot\nabla)\v E = (\v p\cdot\nabla)\v E. \end{align*} $$

Potential and field due to electric dipole open_in_new

For $|\v x|\ll|\v d|$, the electric potential and field due to the dipole is $$ \begin{align*} \Phi(\v x) &= \frac{\v p\cdot \v x}{4\pi\epsilon_0 |\v x|^3} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}, \\ \v E(\v r) &= \frac{3(\v p\cdot \v x)\v x - |\v x|^2 \v p}{4πε_0 |\v x|^5} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat r + \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat\theta \end{align*} $$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\v x$ and $\v p$. (Derivation) Note that $\Phi\propto 1/r^2, E\propto 1/r^3$.

Therefore on axis ($\theta = 0$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ parallel to axis} \end{align*} $$ and off axis ($\theta = \pi/2$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ anti-parallel to axis}. \end{align*} $$

A point dipole $\v p$ at the origin correspond to $$ \begin{align*} ρ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇δ(\v x) \end{align*} $$ so by linearity of Poisson's equation, $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇\left(\frac{1}{4πε_0 |\v x|}\right). \end{align*} $$

In atoms and molecules, external electric fields causes polarisation and induces $\v p\propto \v E_\text{ext}$. This gives long-range forces between them:

  • Dipole-dipole: dipoles line up

  • Dipole-induced dipole

  • Fluctuating dipoles: dipoles synchronise and fluctuate in the same direction.

Multipole expansion for electric potentials open_in_new

For a general charge distribution $ρ(\v x)$ contained within $V = \sb{\v x}{|\v x|<R}$, the potential is, at large $|\v x|\gg R$, $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = \frac{1}{4πε_0}\left(\frac{Q}{|\v x|} + \frac{\v p\cdot \v x}{|\v x|^3} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{Q_{ij} x_i x_j}{|\v x|^5} + \ldots\right) \end{align*} $$ where the first three multipole moments are

  • total charge (electric monopole moment, scalar) $$ \begin{align*} Q = \int_{V} ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x, \end{align*} $$

  • electric dipole moment (vector) $$ \begin{align*} \v p = \int_{V} \v x\,ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x, \end{align*} $$

  • electric quadruple moment (traceless second-order symmetric tensor) $$ \begin{align*} Q_{ij} = \int_{V} (3x_i x_j - |\v x|^2 δ_{ij})\,ρ(\v x) \d^3 \v x. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Electrostatic energy open_in_new

The work done against the electric force $\v F = q\v E$ in bringing a particle of charge $q$ from infinity (where $Φ = 0$) to $\v x$ is $$ \begin{align*} W = -\int_{\infty}^{\v x} \v F\cdot \d \v x = q \int_{\infty}^{\v x} ∇Φ\cdot \d \v x = q\, Φ(\v x). \end{align*} $$

The total work done to assemble a configuration of $N$ charges is $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{\substack{j=1\\j\neq i}}^{N} \frac{q_i q_j}{4πε_0 |\v x_j - \v x_i|}. \end{align*} $$ For a continuous charge distribution, occupying a finite volume $V$, $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int_{V} ρ Φ \d V = \int_{\R^3} \frac{1}{2}ε_0 |\v E|^2\d^3 \v x. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

We can interpret this as the energy stored in the electric field.

Our expressions for $U$ suggest the self-energy of a point charge is infinite. Discard this as it is unchanging and causes no force.
