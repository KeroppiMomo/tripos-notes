A time-dependent magnetic field generates an electric field by (M3), causing currents to flow in a conductor.

Faraday's law open_in_new

For a time-dependent closed curve $C(t)$ that is the boundary of an open surface $S(t)$, Faraday's law states that $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = -\dfd{\mathcal{F}}{t} \end{align*} $$ where $\mathcal{E}$ is the electromotive force (emf) $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = \int_{C} (\v E + \v v\times \v B) \cdot \d \v x, \end{align*} $$ $\v v$ is the velocity of a point on $C$, and $\mathcal{F}$ is the magnetic flux through $S$ $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F} = \int_{S} \v B\cdot \d \v S = \int_{C} \v A\cdot \d \v x. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

The emf is not a force; it is the work done by the Lorentz force on a particle of unit charge confined to $C$: $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = \frac{1}{q} \int_{C} \v F\cdot \d \v x. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Lenz's law states that the direction of the induced current is always such as to produce a magnetic field that opposes the change in the flux that causes the emf; hence the minus sign in Faraday's law.

Inductance open_in_new

We define the mutual inductance as the magnetic flux $\Phi_{B1}$ through one circuit caused by the current $I_2$ through a second circuit $$ \begin{align*} M_{12} = \frac{\Phi_{B1}}{I_2}. \end{align*} $$

In the case of magnetic flux caused by the current through the same circuit, we have the self-inductance $$ \begin{align*} L = \frac{\Phi_B}{I}, \end{align*} $$ which has unit henry $\mathrm{H}=\mathrm{T\ m^2/A}$.

Emf across the circuit is then by Faraday's law $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = -\dfd{\Phi_B}{t} = -L\dot I. \end{align*} $$

Inductance of solenoids open_in_new

For a solenoid of length $l$, cross-section area $A$, with $N$ turns, $$ \begin{align*} L = \frac{NAB}{I} = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{l}. \end{align*} $$

Inductance of thin wires open_in_new

The mutual inductance between wire $i$ (along curve $C_i$ with current $I_i$) and wire $j$ (along curve $C_j$ with current $I_j$) is $$ \begin{align*} L_{ij} = \frac{μ_0}{4π} \int_{C_i} \int_{C_j} \frac{\d \v x_i\cdot \d \v x_j}{|\v x_i - \v x_j|} = L_{ji}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Magnetic field energy open_in_new

The energy required to reach a magnetostatic state $\v J(\v x)$ is $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int_{V} \v J\cdot \v A \d^3 \v x = \int_{\R^3} \frac{1}{2μ_0} |\v B|^2 \d^3 \v x, \end{align*} $$ which can be interpreted as the energy stored in the magnetic field. (Derivation)

Ohm's law open_in_new

A macroscopic property of a stationary conductor is the Ohm's law $$ \begin{align*} \v J = σ\v E \iff \v E = ρ \v J \end{align*} $$ where $σ$ is the electrical conductivity and $ρ = σ\inv$ is the electrical resistivity.

  • A perfect conductor corresponds to the limit $σ\to \infty$, so $\v E = 0$.

  • A perfect insulator corresponds to the limit $σ\to 0$, so $\v J = 0$.

For a straight wire of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$ and conductivity $σ$, the voltage along the wire and the current are related by $$ \begin{align*} V = IR \end{align*} $$ where the resistance is $$ \begin{align*} R = \frac{L}{σA}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Accompanying the resistance of a wire is Joule heating (or Ohmic heating): the conversion of electromagnetic energy into heat at the rate of $I^2 R$.
