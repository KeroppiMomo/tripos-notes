Pole of order $k$ $\implies$ $f(z) = (z-a)^{-k}g(z)$

If $f$ has a pole of order $k$, then its Laurent expansion is $$ \begin{align*} f(z) = \sum_{n=-k}^{\infty} c_n (z-a)^n = (z-a)^{-k} \underbrace{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_{n-k}(z-a)^n}_{g(z)} \end{align*} $$ where $g(z)$ is a power series with radius of convergence at least $r$ and so is holomorphic. Also $g(0) = c_{-n}\neq 0$ by definition.

$f(z) = (z-a)^{-k}g(z)$ $\implies$ pole of order $k$

Conversely, let $g$ have a power series expansion $g(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_n(z-a)^n$ valid on $B(a,r)$, with $b_0=g(a)\neq 0$. Then $f$ has a Laurent series $f(z) = \sum_{n=-k}^{\infty} b_{n+k}(z-a)^n$.

$f(z) = (z-a)^{-k} \,g(z)$ $\implies$ $f(z) = 1/h(z)$

By continuity, choose $ε>0$ such that $g(z)\neq 0$ on $B(a,ε)$. Then $z\mapsto 1/g(z)$ is a holomorphic function on $B(a,ε)$, and so $h(z):=(z-a)^k / g(z)$ is holomorphic with a zero of order $k$ at $z=a$.

$f(z) = 1/h(z)$ $\implies$ $f(z) = (z-a)^{-k} \,g(z)$

Conversely, write $h(z) = (z-a)^k \,\tilde h(z)$ where $\tilde h(z)\neq 0$ on $B(a,ε)$. Then $g(z) := 1/\tilde h(z)$ is holomorphic on $B(a,ε)$ and $f(z) = (z-a)^{-k} g(z)$ holds on $B(a,ε^\times)$. Extend $g$ to $B(a,r)$ by setting $g(z) = (z-a)^k f(z)$ on $B(a,r)^\times$.

Pole $\implies$ $\lim_{z\to a} |f(z)|=\infty$

Apply 2 or 3.

$\lim_{z\to a} |f(z)|=\infty$ $\implies$ pole

There exists $ε>0$ such that $f(z) \neq 0$ for all $z\in B(a,ε)^\times$, so $1/f(z)$ is holomorphic on $B(a,ε)^\times$. Moreover $|1/f(z)| \to 0$ as $z\to a$, so $z=a$ is a removable singularity for $1/f(z)$. Thus $1/f(z) = h(z)$ for some function $h$ holomorphic on $B(a,ε)$.

$h(a) = \lim_{z\to a} 1/f(z) = 0$ by continuity, and hence $h$ has a zero at $z=a$. This satisfies 3.
