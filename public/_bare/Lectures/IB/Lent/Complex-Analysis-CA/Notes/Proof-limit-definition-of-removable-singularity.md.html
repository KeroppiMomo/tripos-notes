Removable singularity $\implies$ $(z-a)\,f(z) \to 0$

If $f$ has a removable singularity at $z=a$, then $f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(z-a)^n$ is valid on $B(a,r)^\times$, so $f$ is the restriction of a power series with radius of convergence at least $r$, which is holomorphic on $B(a,r)$. Thus $(z-a)f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} c_n(z-a)^{n+1} \to 0$.

$(z-a)\,f(z)\to 0$ $\implies$ removable singularity

Define $$ \begin{align*} g(z) := \begin{cases} (z-a)^2 f(z), & z\neq a, \\ 0, &z=a. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ $g$ is continuous on $B(a,r)$ and holomorphic on $B(a,r)^\times$, so holomorphic on $B(a,r)$. Note that $g(a) = 0$ and $g'(a) = \lim_{z\to a} (z-a)^2f(z)/(z-a) = 0$ by hypothesis. Therefore the Taylor series expansion of $g$ has the form $$ \begin{align*} g(z) = \sum_{n=2}^{\infty} b_n(z-a)^n. \end{align*} $$ Hence $$ \begin{align*} f(z) = \frac{g(z)}{(z-a)^2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} b_{n+2}(z-a)^n &&\text{on }B(a,r)^\times. \end{align*} $$ By the identity principle (or uniqueness of Laurent expansion), the power series holds on $B(a,r)$ as well.
