Topological surfaces open_in_new

Definition (topological surface) open_in_new

A topological surface is a topological space $Σ$ such that

  1. for all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open neighbourhood $U\subseteq Σ$ of $p$ such that $U$ is homeomorphic to an open disc in $\R^2$; and

  2. $Σ$ is Hausdorff and second countable.


  • $\R^2$ is homeomorphic to $B(0,1)$ so we could also say $U$ is homeomorphic to $\R^2$.

Examples of topological surfaces:

  • The plane $\R^2$.

  • Any open subset of $\R^2$.

  • The graph $Γ_f = \sb{\bigl(x,y,f(x,y)\bigr)}{(x,y)\in \R^2} \subseteq \R^3$ of a continuous function $f: \R^2 \to \R$. $Γ_f$ is in fact homeomorphic to $\R^2$. (Proof)

The sphere as a topological surface open_in_new

Consider the sphere with the subspace topology $$ \begin{align*} S^2 = \sb{(x,y,z)\in \R^3}{x^2 + y^2 + z^2 = 1} \subseteq \R^3. \end{align*} $$

$S^2$ is Hausdorff and second countable, inherited from $\R^3$.

There are two stereographic projections, $π_+$ and $π_-$, given by $$ \begin{align*} π_{\pm} : \quad S^2 \setminus \set{(0,0,\pm 1)} &\to \R^2 \\ (x,y,z) &\mapsto \Bigl(\frac{x}{1\mp z}, \frac{y}{1\mp z}\Bigr). \end{align*} $$ It has an inverse $$ \begin{alignat}{1} π_\pm\inv :&& \quad \R^2 &\to S^2 \setminus \set{(0,0,\pm 1)} \\ &&\quad (u,v) &\mapsto \Bigl(\frac{2u}{u^2+v^2+1}, \frac{2v}{u^2+v^2+1}, \pm\frac{u^2+v^2-1}{u^2+v^2+1}\Bigr) \end{alignat} $$ They are continuous, and thus $π_{\pm}$ are homeomorphisms.

Every $p\in S^2$ lies in either $S^2\setminus \set{0,0,1}$ or $S^2\setminus \set{0,0,-1}$, which is an open neighbourhood homeomorphic to $\R^2$, so $S^2$ is a topological surface.

The real projective plane open_in_new

The real projective plane is $\mathbb{RP}^2 = S^2/{\sim} = S^2 / (\pm 1) = S^2 / (\Z/2)$, where the equivalence relation $\sim$ identifies $x$ with $-x$ for all $x\in S^2$.

  • On the level of sets, $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is the set of straight lines in $\R^3$ through the origin.

  • $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is Hausdorff. (Proof)

  • $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is second countable. (Proof)

  • $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is locally homeomorphic to an open disc. (Proof)

Thus $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is a topological surface.

  • $\mathbb{RP}^2$ can also be viewed as a polygon quotient.

The torus open_in_new

The torus is $S^1 \times S^1$ where $S^1 = \sb{z\in \C}{|z|=1}$.

  • $S^1\times S^1 \subseteq \C\times \C = \R^4$, so we can see it is compact and Hausdorff.

  • The map $$ \begin{alignat}{1} e:\quad && \R^2 &\to S^1 \times S^1 \\ && (s,t) &\mapsto (e^{2πis}, e^{2πit}) \end{alignat} $$ induces a homeomorphism $\R^2/\Z^2 \to S^1 \times S^1$. (Proof)

  • $\R^2/\Z^2$ is locally homeomorphic to an open disc. (Proof)

  • We can also view $\R^2/\Z^2$ as the quotient $[0,1]^2/{\sim}$ where $(x,0)\sim(x,1)$ and $(0,y)\sim(1,y)$ for all $0\leq x\leq 1$ and $0\leq y\leq 1$. (Proof)

Quotient of a polygon open_in_new

Let $P\subseteq \R^2$ be a planar polygon with all edges paired up. For simplicity assume paired sides have the same Euclidean length, and all sides are oriented.

For a pair of edges $\set{e, \hat e}$, there exists a unique isometry $f_{e\hat e}: e\to \hat e$ respecting the orientation. Define an equivalence relation $P$ by saying $x\sim f_{e\hat e}(x)$ whenever $x\in e$ an edge.

$P/{\sim}$ is a topological surface.

  • Clearly $P/{\sim}$ is Hausdorff.

  • $P$ is locally homeomorphic to a open disc by gluing together maps:

    For a point in the interior (red), choose a disc small enough to avoid all edges.

    For a point on an edge (pink) or at a vertex (green and orange), take sufficiently small sectors in $P$. We can rescale the angle and glue all of them to $B(0,δ)$.

    If the equivalence class of a vertex has only one element (blue), then it is at the same end of a pair of identified edges. Identifying the radii of the sector makes it homeomorphic to a cone, which is homeomorphic to a disc on $\R^2$.

    See this example for more details.

  • $P/{\sim}$ is second countable. In the interior of $P$, take $B(p, δ)$ for $p\in \Q^2 \cap P$ and $δ\in \Q_+$. At a vertex of $P$, take sectors of radius $\Q_+$.

Connect sums open_in_new

Let$Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ be topological surfaces, and choose embeddings (continuous injection) $i_1: \overline{B(0, δ)}\hookrightarrow Σ_1$ and $i_2: \overline{B(0,δ)} \hookrightarrow Σ_2$ of the closed disc $\overline{B(0,δ)}$ into $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$.

The connect sum $Σ_1\# Σ_2$ of $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ (with respect to the embeddings $i_1$ and $i_2$) is defined as $$ \begin{align*} \biggl(\Bigl(Σ_1 \setminus i_1(B(0,δ))\Bigr) \cup \Bigl(Σ_2 \setminus i_2(B(0,δ))\Bigr)\biggr) \bigg/ \sim \end{align*} $$ where $\sim$ identifies $i_1(δe^{iθ})$ and $i_2(δe^{iθ})$ for all $θ\in [0,2π]$.

  • It is a topological surface. We construct disc neighbourhoods of points on the image of $S^1(0,δ)$ in $Σ_1\#Σ_2$ by gluing half-discs together.

  • If $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ are connected, then up to homeomorphism, $Σ_1\#Σ_2$ depends only on $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ and not on the embeddings $i_1$ and $i_2$.

  • Connected sum of tori $T^2\# T^2$

Subdivisions open_in_new

Definition (subdivision) open_in_new

A subdivision of a compact topological surface $Σ$ comprises

  1. a finite set $V \subseteq Σ$ of vertices;

  2. a finite set $\set{e_i: \text{continuous }[0, 1] \to Σ}_{i\in E}$ of edges,

such that

  • each $e_i$ is injective on $(0,1)$;

  • $e_i\inv(V) = \set{0,1}$;

  • $e_i(0,1)$ and $e_j(0,1)$ are disjoint for $i\neq j$; and

  • each connected component of $Σ \setminus (\bigcup_{i} e_i[0,1] \cup V)$ is homeomorphic to an open disc, called an open face. Call a face the closure of such a component.

Definition (triangulation) open_in_new

A subdivision is a triangulation if each closed face contains exactly three edges, and two closed faces are either disjoint or meet at an edge or a vertex.

Definition (Euler characteristic) open_in_new

The Euler characteristic of subdivision of a compact topological surface $Σ$ is defined as the integer $$ \begin{align*} χ(Σ) = \# \text{ vertices} - \#\text{ edges} + \# \text{ faces}. \end{align*} $$

Theorem (invariant of Euler characteristic) open_in_new

  1. Every compact topological surface $Σ$ admits a subdivision (and in fact triangulation).

  2. The Euler characteristic $χ(Σ)$ of $Σ$ does not depend on the choice of subdivision, i.e. it defines a topological invariant of the surface $Σ$.

  • $χ(\R^2) = 1$: it admits a subdivision with no vertices, no edges, 1 face.

  • $χ(S^2) = 2$: it admits a subdivision with 1 vertex, no edges, 1 face.

  • $χ(T^2) = 0$: it admits a subdivision with 1 vertex, 2 edges, 1 face.

  • $χ(\mathbb{RP}^2) = 1$: it admits a subdivision with 2 vertices, 2 edges, 1 face.

Proposition (Euler characteristic of a connected sum) open_in_new

The Euler characteristic of a connect sum $Σ_1 \# Σ_2$ is $$ \begin{align*} χ(Σ_1 \# Σ_2) = χ(Σ_1) + χ(Σ_2) - 2. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • A $g$-torus is the connect sum of $g$ tori ($g$ is the called the genus of the surface). Its Euler characteristic is $χ = -2(g-1)$.

Definition (chart, atlas, local parametrisation) open_in_new

A chart for a topological surface $Σ$ is a pair $(U, ϕ)$ where $U\subseteq Σ$ is open and $ϕ: U\to V$ is a homeomorphism to an open set $V\subseteq \R^2$.

The inverse $σ = ϕ\inv: V \hookrightarrow Σ$ is called a local parametrisation of $Σ$.

An atlas for $Σ$ is a collection $\set{(U_i, ϕ_i)}_{i\in I}$ of charts such that $\bigcup_{i\in I} U_i = Σ$.

  • Every topological surface by definition admits an atlas: every point has an open neighbourhood homeomorphic to an open disc.

Definition (transition map) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a topological surface.

The transition map between two charts $(U_1,ϕ_1), (U_2, ϕ_2)$ is $$ \begin{align*} ϕ_2 \circ ϕ_1\inv : ϕ_1(U_1 \cap U_2) \to ϕ_2(U_1 \cap U_2). \end{align*} $$

  • Since $ϕ_1, ϕ_2$ are homeomorphisms, so the transition map.

Definition (smooth functions, diffeomorphism) open_in_new

Let $f: V\to V'$ be continuous.

  • For an open set $V\subseteq \R^n$ and $V'= \R^m$, $f$ is smooth if it is infinitely differentiable (i.e. all partial derivatives of all orders exist).

  • For a general subset $V\subseteq \R^n$ and $V' = \R^m$, $f$ is smooth at $p\in V$ if there exists an open neighbourhood $B\subseteq \R^n$ of $p$ and a smooth map $F: B \to \R^m$ such that $F|_{B\cap V} = f|_{B \cap V}$.

  • For $V = Σ$ an abstract smooth surface and $V' = \R^m$, $f$ is smooth at $p\in Σ$ if for a chart $(U, ϕ)$ at $p$ in our atlas, the map $f\circ ϕ\inv: ϕ(U) \to \R^m$ is smooth at $ϕ(p)$.

  • For $V=Σ_1$ and $V'=Σ_2$ abstract smooth surface, $f$ is smooth at $p$ if for charts $(U, ϕ)$ at $p$ and $(U', ϕ')$ at $f(p)$, the composition $ϕ' \circ f \circ ϕ\inv$ is smooth at $ϕ(p)$.

$f$ is called a diffeomorphism if it is a homeomorphism, and $f$ and $f\inv$ are smooth.

  • Smoothness on abstract smooth surfaces does not depend on the choice of chart $(U,ϕ)$ at $p$ in our atlas, because if we use a different chart $(U', ϕ')$ at $p$, $$ \begin{align*} f\circ ϕ'\inv = (f\circ ϕ\inv) \circ (ϕ \circ ϕ'\inv) \end{align*} $$ where the transition map $ϕ \circ ϕ'\inv$ is a diffeomorphism. So $f\circ ϕ'\inv$ is smooth iff $f\circ ϕ\inv$ is smooth.

Definition (abstract smooth surface) open_in_new

An abstract smooth surface $Σ$ is a topological surface with an atlas of charts such that all transition maps are diffeomorphisms (of open sets in $\R^2$).

Theorem (inverse function theorem) open_in_new

Let $f: U\to\R^n$ with $U\subseteq \R^n$ open, and $x_0\in U$.

If $f\in{}$$C^1(U)$ and $Df|_{x_0}$ is invertible, then there exists open neighbourhoods $V$ of $x_0$ and $W$ of $f(x_0)$ such that $f|_V: V\to W$ is a homeomorphism.

Moreover, $f\inv\in C^1(V)$ with derivative $$ \begin{align*} D(f\inv)(y) = \bigl(Df(f\inv(y))\bigr)\inv. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • This can be extended to say if $f$ is smooth, then so is $f\inv$.

Theorem (implicit function theorem) open_in_new

Let $f: U\to\R^m$ with $U\subseteq \R^{n+m}$ open, and $(x_0, y_0)\in U$.

If $f\in{}$$C^1(U)$ and $D_y f(x_0, y_0) \in \mathcal{L}(\R^m, \R^m)$ is invertible, then there exists an open neighbourhood $V$ of $x_0$ and a unique $g: V\to\R^m$ such that

  • $g(x_0) = y_0$; and

  • $f(x, g(x)) = 0$; and

  • there exists a neighbourhood $W\subseteq \R^n$ of $y_0$ such that if $(x,y)\in V\times W$, then $f(x,y)=0 \iff y=g(x)$.

Moreover, $g\in C^1(V)$ with derivative $$ \begin{align*} Dg(x) = -\bigl(D_y f(x,g(x))\bigr)\inv \bigl(D_x f(x,g(x))\bigr). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (smooth surface in R3) open_in_new

A smooth surface in $\R^3$ is a subspace $Σ\subseteq \R^3$ such that for all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open neighbourhood $U\subseteq Σ$ of $p$ which is diffeomorphic to an open set in $\R^2$.

  • Unpacking the definition: for all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open ball $B\subseteq \R^3$ containing $p$, an open set $V\subseteq \R^2$ and a smooth map $f: B \to V$, such that by writing $U = B \cap Σ$ (which is open in $Σ$), $f|_U: U \to V$ is a homeomorphism and its inverse $V\hookrightarrow \R^3$ is smooth.

Theorem (equivalent definitions of smooth surface in R3) open_in_new

Let $Σ\subseteq \R^3$ be a subspace. The following are equivalent:

  1. $Σ$ is a smooth surface in $\R^3$.

  2. $Σ$ is locally the graph of a smooth function over one of the coordinate planes:

    For all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open ball $B\subseteq \R^3$ containing $p$, an open set $V\subseteq \R^2$ and a smooth function $g: V\to \R$ such that $B \cap Σ = \sb{\bigl(x,y,g(x,y)\bigr)}{(x,y)\in V}$ or over the $xz$ or $yz$ plane.

  3. $Σ$ is locally the level set of a smooth function with non-zero derivative:

    For all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open ball $B\subseteq \R^3$ containing $p$ and a smooth function $f: B \to \R$, such that $B \cap Σ = f\inv(0)$ and $Df|_x$ $\neq 0$ for all $x\in B$.

  4. $Σ$ is locally the image of an allowable parametrization:

    For all $p\in Σ$, there exists an open neighbourhood $U\subseteq Σ$ of $p$, an open set $V\subseteq \R^2$ and a smooth function $σ: V\to U$, such that $σ$ is a homeomorphism and $Dσ|_x$ has rank 2 for all $x\in V$.

Proof

Surface of revolution open_in_new

Let $η(t) = (f(t), 0, g(t))$ for $t\in [a,b]$ be a smooth curve in the $xz$-plane. Assume $η$ is injective, $f(t)>0$ and $η'(t)\neq 0$.

The surface of revolution is obtained by spinning $η$ around the $z$-axis. Consider the parametrisation $$ \begin{align*} σ(u,v) = (f(u) \cos u, f(u) \sin v, g(u)) \end{align*} $$ where $u\in (a,b)$ and $v\in (θ, θ+δ)$ with $0<δ<2π$.

Useful computation: $$ \begin{align*} &σ_u \times σ_v = (-f\, g' \cos v, -f \, g' \sin v, f\, f') \\ \implies & \|σ_u \times σ_v\|^2 = f^2\bigl((f')^2 + (g')^2\bigr) \neq 0. \end{align*} $$ so $σ$ is an allowable parametrisation.

This is a smooth surface in $\R^3$, as we can vary $θ$ to cover all of the surface.

First fundamental form: $$ \begin{align*} \I = \bigl((f')^2 + (g')^2\bigr) \d u^2 + f^2 \d v^2. \end{align*} $$

Orientability open_in_new

Definition (orientation-preserving diffeomorphism on R2) open_in_new

Let $\GL^+(2, \R) \leq \GL(2, \R)$ be the subgroup of $2\times 2$ real matrices with positive determinants.

A diffeomorphism $f: V\to V'$ between open sets $V,V'\subseteq \R^2$ is said to be orientation-preserving if $Df|_x \in \GL^+(2,\R)$ for all $x\in V$.

Definition (orientable surface, orientation) open_in_new

An abstract smooth surface $Σ$ is orientable if it admits an atlas $\set{(U_i, ϕ_i)}_{i\in I}$ such that all transition maps are orientation-preserving.

The choice of such an atlas is called an orientation.

  • There is a definition of orientability for a topological surface, but it requires algebraic topology. We here simply define a topological surface to be orientable if it contains no subset homeomorphic to a Möbius band.

Lemma (diffeomorphisms preserve orientability) open_in_new

Let $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ be abstract smooth surfaces.

If $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ are diffeomorphic, then $Σ_1$ is orientable iff $Σ_2$ is orientable.

Proof

Definition (tangent plane of smooth surface in R3) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$. Fix an allowable parametrisation $σ: V\to U$ with $σ(0)=p$.

  • The tangent plane at $p$ is $$ \begin{align*} T_p Σ = \im Dσ|_0 \subseteq \R^3. \end{align*} $$

  • The affine tangent plane at $p$ is $p+T_pΣ \subseteq \R^3$.


  • $T_p Σ$ does not depend on the choice of $σ$. (Proof)

Lemma (tangent plane as derivative of all curves) open_in_new

The tangent plane of a smooth surface $Σ$ in $\R^3$ at $p$ is $$ \begin{align*} T_p Σ = \sb{γ'(0)}{\text{smooth }γ: (-ε, ε)\to Σ, \; γ(0)=p}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Lemma (derivative of map between surfaces preserves tangent spaces) open_in_new

Let $Σ_1$ and $Σ_2$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$.

If $A: \R^3 \to \R^3$ is smooth and $A: Σ_1 \to Σ_2$, then $DA|_p:$ $T_pΣ_1$ $\to T_{A(p)}Σ_2$.

Definition (normal direction, Gauss map) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$ and $p\in Σ$ be a point.

The normal direction to $Σ$ at $p$ is the Euclidean orthogonal complement $(T_p Σ)^\perp\subseteq \R^3$ of the tangent plane.

A unit vector in $(T_pΣ)^\perp$ is a unit normal vector.

The Gauss map $n: U\to S^2$ defined on a subset $U\subseteq Σ$ is a continuous map that sends a point to the unit normal vector.

Definition (two-sided surface) open_in_new

A smooth surface in $\R^3$ is two-sided if it admits a global continuous choice of unit normal vector, i.e. a globally well-defined Gauss map.

Lemma (Gauss map is smooth) open_in_new

The Gauss map $n: U \to S^2$ is smooth.

Proof

Definition (arc length of a curve) open_in_new

For some starting point $t_0$, the arc length $s(t)$ is the (signed) distance from $t_0$ to $t$, $$ s(t) = \int_{t_0}^t |\dv x(t')|\,dt'. $$

  • The arc length between two points on a curve is independent of the choice of parametrisation. (Proof)

  • Using $s$ to parametrise the curve i.e. $\v x(s)$, the tangent vector has unit norm: $$ \bigg|\dvd xs\bigg| = \frac{|d\v x/dt|}{ds/dt} = 1. $$

Definition (first fundamental form) open_in_new

Let $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation on a smooth surface $Σ$ in $\R^3$. Define $$ \begin{align*} E = \langle σ_u, σ_u\rangle, \quad F = \langle σ_u, σ_v\rangle, \quad G = \langle σ_v, σ_v\rangle \end{align*} $$ which are functions of $(u,v)\in V$.

The first fundamental form $\I$ of $Σ$ in the parametrisation $σ$ can be represented by

  • the formal expression $$ \begin{align*} E \d u^2 + 2F \d u \d v + G \d v^2, \end{align*} $$

  • the inner product on $\R^2$ $$ \begin{align*} I((u_1, v_1), (u_2, v_2)) = E u_1 u_2 + F(u_1 v_2 + v_1 u_2) + Gv_1 v_2, \end{align*} $$

  • or its matrix representation $$ \begin{align*} \pmat{E & F \\ F & G} = (Dσ)^T (Dσ). \end{align*} $$


  • The first fundamental form at $p = σ(0)$ represents the Euclidean inner product on $T_p Σ$: for $v = Dσ|_0(v_0)$ and $w = Dσ|_0(w_0)$, we have $$ \begin{align*} \langle v,w\rangle = I(v_0, w_0). \end{align*} $$

Lemma (length of curve on surface) open_in_new

Let $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation on a smooth surface $Σ$ in $\R^3$, and $γ(t) = σ(u(t), v(t)): [a,b] \to U$ be a smooth curve. Then $$ \begin{align*} \mathrm{length}(γ) = \int_{a}^{b} \sqrt{Eu'(t)^2 + 2F u'(t) v'(t) + G v'(t)^2} \d t = \int_{a}^{b} \sqrt{\I(u'(t), v'(t))} \d \end{align*} $$ where $\I(x,y) = x^T \pmat{E & F\\ F & G} y$ is the first fundamental form.

Proof

Definition (isometric, locally isometric) open_in_new

Let $Σ, Σ'$ be smooth surface in $\R^3$.

We say $Σ$ and $Σ'$ are isometric if there is a diffeomorphism $f: Σ\to Σ'$ such that for every smooth curve $γ: [a,b]\to Σ$, $$ \begin{align*} \mathrm{length}_Σ(γ) = \mathrm{length}_{Σ'}(f\circ γ). \end{align*} $$

We say $Σ$ and $Σ'$ are locally isometric near $p\in Σ$ and $p'\in Σ'$ if there are open neighbourhoods $U\subseteq Σ$ of $p$ and $U'\subseteq Σ'$ of $p'$ such that $U$ and $U'$ are isometric.

Lemma (locally isometric iff first fundamental form agrees) open_in_new

Let $Σ, Σ'$ be smooth surface in $\R^3$.

$Σ$ and $Σ'$ are locally isometric near $p\in Σ$ and $p'\in Σ'$ iff there are allowable parametrisations $σ: V\to U\subseteq Σ$ with $σ(0)=p$ and $σ': V\to U'\subseteq Σ$ with $σ'(0)=p'$ such that the first fundamental forms $\I_Σ = \I_{Σ'}$ as functions on $V$.

Proof

Lemma (first fundamental form in different parametrisations) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$, with two allowable parametrisations $\tilde σ: V\to U\subseteq Σ$ and $σ: \tilde V\to U\subseteq Σ$ near $p\in Σ$.

Let $ϕ = \tilde σ\inv\circ σ: V\to\tilde V$ be the transition map. The first fundamental forms in the two parametrisations are related by $$ \begin{align*} \pmat{E & F \\ F & G} = (Dϕ)^T \pmat{\tilde E & \tilde F \\ \tilde F & \tilde G} (Dϕ). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Lemma (angle on tangent plane) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$ and $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation at $σ(0) = p\in U\subseteq Σ$.

The angle $θ$ between $v = Dσ|_0(v_0)$ and $w=Dσ|_0(w_0)$ on the tangent plane is $$ \begin{align*} \cos θ = \frac{\I(v_0, w_0)}{\sqrt{\I(v_0, v_0)} \sqrt{\I(w_0, w_0)}} \end{align*} $$ where $\I(x,y) = x^T \pmat{E&F \\ F& G} y$ is the first fundamental form.

Proof

Lemma (conformal iff first fundamental form is isotropic) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$ and $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation at $σ(0) = p\in U$.

$E(0)=G(0)$ and $F(0)=0$ in the first fundamental form iff $σ$ is conformal at $0$, i.e. if smooth curves $γ$ and $\tilde γ$ on $V$ pass through $0$ at an angle $θ$, then $σ\circ γ$ and $σ\circ \tilde γ$ on $U$ pass through $p$ at angle $θ$ as well.

Proof

Definition (area on surface) open_in_new

Let $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation for an open set $U$ in a smooth surface in $\R^3$.

Define the area of $U$ as $$ \begin{align*} \mathrm{area}(U) = \int_{V} \sqrt{EG - F^2} \d u \d v = \int_{V} \sqrt{\det \I} \d u \d v \end{align*} $$ where $\I = \pmat{E & F \\ F & G}$ is the first fundamental form.

For any open sets $U\subseteq Σ$, define $\mathrm{area}(U)$ by covering it by images of allowable parametrisations.

  • Motivation: viewing $σ_u$ and $σ_v$ as an infinitesimal parallelogram on $Σ$, we integrate over the area $\sqrt{\langle σ_u\times σ_v, σ_u\times σ_v\rangle} = \sqrt{\langle σ_u, σ_u\rangle\langle σ_v, σ_v\rangle - \langle σ_u, σ_v\rangle^2}$.

  • $\mathrm{area}(U)$ does not depend on the parametrisation. (Proof)

Definition (second fundamental form) open_in_new

Let $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation on a smooth surface $Σ$ in $\R^3$. Define $$ \begin{align*} L ={}& \langle n, σ_{uu}\rangle = -\langle n_u, σ_u\rangle, \\ M ={}& \langle n, σ_{uv}\rangle = -\langle n_u, σ_v\rangle = -\langle n_v, σ_u\rangle, \\ N ={}& \langle n, σ_{vv}\rangle = -\langle n_v, σ_v\rangle, \\ n ={}& \frac{σ_u \times σ_v}{\|σ_u \times σ_v\|} \end{align*} $$ which are functions of $(u,v)\in V$. (Differentiate $\langle n, σ_u\rangle=0=\langle n, σ_v\rangle$ to see those equalities.)

The second fundamental form $\II$ is represented by

  • the formal expression $$ \begin{align*} L \d u^2 + 2M \d u \d v + N \d v^2, \end{align*} $$

  • the symmetric bilinear form $$ \begin{align*} \II((u_1, v_1), (u_2, v_2)) = L u_1 u_2 + M(u_1 v_2 + v_1 u_2) + N v_1 v_2, \end{align*} $$

  • or its matrix representation $$ \begin{align*} \pmat{L & M \\ M & N} = -(Dn)^T (Dσ). \end{align*} $$

Definition (shape operator) open_in_new

Definition (energy of curve) open_in_new

Definition (geodesic) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$.

A geodesic $γ:[a,b]\to Σ$ is a critical point for the energy functional in the space of smooth curves from $γ(a)$ to $γ(b)$.

Formulation: consider any one-parameter variation of $γ$ with fixed end-points, i.e. smooth map $Γ: (-ε, ε)\times [a,b]\to Σ$ such that writing $γ_s(t) = Γ(s,t)$, we have $γ_0 = γ$, and $γ_s(a)$ and $γ_s(b)$ are independent of $s$. Then $γ$ is a geodesic if $$ \begin{align*} \dd{s} E(γ_s)\Big|_{s=0} = 0. \end{align*} $$

Lemma (geodesic equations) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a smooth surface in $\R^3$ and $σ: V\to U$ be an allowable parametrisation.

A smooth curve $γ: [a,b]\to U$ is a geodesic iff it satisfies the geodesic equations $$ \begin{align*} \dd{t}(E\dot u + F\dot v) ={}& \frac{1}{2}(E_u \dot u^2 + 2F_u \dot u \dot v + G_u \dot v^2), \\ \dd{t}(F\dot u + G\dot v) ={}& \frac{1}{2}(E_v \dot u^2 + 2F_v \dot u \dot v + G_v \dot v^2). \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • These are the Euler-Lagrange equations.

A smooth curve $γ: [a,b]\to Σ$ is a geodesic iff $\ddot γ(t)$ is normal to the surface for all $t$.

Consequently, geodesics are always parametrised with constant speed.

Lemma (hyperbolic regular 4g-gon with angle summing up to 2pi) open_in_new

In hyperbolic space $\mathbb{H}$, say a flag comprises

  1. an oriented hyperbolic line;

  2. a point on that line;

  3. a choice of side to the line.

Given two flags, there exists a hyperbolic isometry taking one to another: use a Möbius map to match the point and the oriented line, and compose with an inverse if necessary to match the distinguished side.

Take a hyperbolic regular $n$-gon $G$ with internal angles $2π/4g$. Impose the edge identification: for any two edges $e,e'$ paired, there exists a hyperbolic isometry taking $e$ to $e'$ respecting the orientation of the line, and taking the inside of the polygon relative to $e$ to the outside relative to $e'$. (Illustration)

Now we give an atlas for $Σ_g = G/$side identification:

  1. If $p\in G$ lies in $\mathrm{int}(G)$, pick a small disc $B\subseteq \mathrm{int}(G)$. Then $q(B) \hookrightarrow D$ is an injection (where $q: G \to Σ_g$ is the quotient map).

  2. If $p\in G$ lies on an edge $e$ (excluding vertex), it has an identified $\tilde p$ on the paired edge $\tilde e$, and there exists an isometry $γ$ sending $e$ to $\tilde e$. Let $U = B(p,δ)\cap G$ and $\tilde U = B(p,δ)\setminus G$. Then the isometry $γ: U \hookrightarrow D$ and the inclusion $i: \tilde U \hookrightarrow D$ glue to give an injection $U\cup \tilde U\hookrightarrow D$, which descends to a map on the neighbourhood of $q(p) \in Σ_g$ into $D$.

  3. All vertices are identified. For a vertex $v\in G$, apply a isometry putting $v$ at $0$, and then rotate. The angles of all $4g$ sectors add up to $2π$, so they cover a neighbourhood of $0\in D$. Build a chart at $q(v)$ from this.

Discussion about the Gauss-Bonnet theorem open_in_new

Definition (geodesic polygon) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be an abstract smooth surface with an abstract Riemannian metric $g$.

A geodesic polygon on $(Σ, g)$ is an embedding (i.e. the image under an injective homeomorphism) of a closed disc such that the boundary can be decomposed into finitely many geodesic segments.

Theorem (local Gauss-Bonnet) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be an abstract smooth surface with an abstract Riemannian metric $g$.

If $R$ is a geodesic $n$-gon with internal angle $α_1, α_2, \ldots, α_n$, then $$ \begin{align*} \int_{R} κ_Σ \d A = \sum_{i=1}^{n} α_i - (n-2)π, \end{align*} $$ where $κ_Σ$ is the Gauss curvature of the surface.

Idea of the proof:

  1. Apply Green's theorem to the right function.

  2. Since the sides are geodesics, the line integral concentrates at corners and contributes quantities related to the internal angles.

Theorem (global Gauss-Bonnet) open_in_new

Let $Σ$ be a compact abstract smooth surface with an abstract Riemannian metric $g$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \int_{Σ} κ_Σ \d A = 2π\, χ(Σ) \end{align*} $$ where $κ_Σ$ is the Gauss curvature and $χ(Σ)$ is the Euler characteristic of the surface.

Proof sketch:

  1. Every Riemannian $Σ$ admits a decomposition into geodesic polygons.

  2. Then local Gauss-Bonnet implies global Gauss-Bonnet:

    Take a geodesic decomposition $\set{P_1, \ldots, P_F}$ of $Σ$, and suppose $P_i$ has $V_i$ internal angles $α_{i1}, \ldots, α_{iV_i}$. Then additivity of integration yields $$ \begin{align*} \int_{Σ} κ_Σ \d A ={}& \sum_{i=1}^{F} \int_{P_i} κ_Σ \d A \\ ={}& \sum_{i=1}^{F} \left(\sum_{j=1}^{V_i} α_{ij} - (V_i - 2)π \right) \\ ={}& \sum_{i=1}^{F} \sum_{j=1}^{V_i} α_{ij} - π\sum_{i=1}^{F} V_i + 2π \sum_{i=1}^{F} 1 \\ ={}& 2πV - 2πE + 2πF = 2π\,χ(Σ). \end{align*} $$

    The first term has a contribution $2π$ from every vertex. In the second term, each face contributes its number of edges to the sum, which double counts every edge.

Applications:

  1. Area of a spherical triangle ($κ\equiv 1$) is (sheet 2) $$ \begin{align*} \text{area}_{S^2}(T) = α_1 + α_2 + α_3 - π. \end{align*} $$

  2. Area of a hyperbolic triangle ($κ\equiv -1$) is $$ \begin{align*} \text{area}_{\mathbb{H}}(T) = π - α_1 - α_2 - α_3. \end{align*} $$

  3. Area of the compact hyperbolic surface $Σ_g$ with genus $g\geq 2$ is $$ \begin{align*} \text{area}(Σ_g) = -2π \,χ(Σ_g) = (4g-4)π. \end{align*} $$

    We can also see this from our constructions: using regular $4g$-gon $P$ with internal angles $2π/4g$, $\text{area}(Σ_g) = \text{area}_{\text{hyp}}(P) = (4g-2)π - \sum_{i=1}^{4g} 2π/4g = (4π - 4)π$. Using right-angled hexagons, a genus $g$ surface can be cut into $2g-2$ pairs of pants, each made of two right-angled hexagons, which has area $(6-2)π - \sum_{i=1}^{6} π/2 = π$.
