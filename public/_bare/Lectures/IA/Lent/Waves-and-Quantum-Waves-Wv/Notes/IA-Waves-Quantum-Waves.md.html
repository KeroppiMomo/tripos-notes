Waves open_in_new

The wave equation open_in_new

A wavefunction $\psi(x,t)$ should satisfy the wave equation $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd[2]{\psi}{x} = \frac{1}{c^2}\pdfd[2]{\psi}{t} \end{align*} $$ where $c$ is the wave speed.

  • Any solution is in the form $\psi(x,t) = f(x-ct) + g(x+ct)$. (Derivation)

  • This is linear in $\psi$, so principle of superposition applies i.e. $\psi_1+\psi_2$ is a solution.

  • It has complex solution $\psi = \exp(i(kx \pm \omega t))$, and taking the real part gives the physical quantity.

In higher dimensions, $$ \begin{align*} \nabla^2 \psi \equiv \pdfd[2]{\psi}{x} + \pdfd[2]{\psi}{y} + \pdfd[2]{\psi}{z} = \frac{1}{c^2}\pdfd[2]{\psi}{t}. \end{align*} $$

Wavenumber, wavevector and angular frequency open_in_new

The wavefunction $\cos(kx-\omega t)$ has wavelength $\lambda$ and frequency $f$, and so we define the wavenumber $k$ (unit $\mathrm{m\inv}$) and angular frequency $\omega$ (unit $\mathrm{rad\ s\inv}$) to be $$ \begin{align*} k = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}, \quad \omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f \implies c = \frac{\omega}{k}. \end{align*} $$

A plane wave in higher dimensions has a wavevector $\v k$, whose magnitude is $k=2\pi/\lambda$ pointing in the direction of propagation. It has wavefunction $$ \begin{align*} \psi(\v r, t) = A \cos(\v k\cdot\v r - \omega t). \end{align*} $$

Waves on a string open_in_new

A stretched string (with $\psi$ much less than string length) satisfies the wave equation, with $$ \begin{align*} c = \sqrt{\frac{T}{\rho}} \end{align*} $$ where $T$ is the tension and $\rho$ is the mass per length. (Derivation)

For a wavefunction $\psi(x,t)=A\cos(kx - \omega t)$, the kinetic and potential energy densities are $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{K}{x} = \pdfd{U}{x} = \frac{1}{2}\rho \omega^2 A^2 \sin^2(kx - \omega t) = \frac{1}{2}Tk^2A^2\sin^2(kx-\omega t). \end{align*} $$ (Derivation) Total energy density is $$ \begin{align*} \pdfd{E}{x} = \rho\omega^2 A^2\sin^2(kx-\omega t), \qquad \left\langle\pdfd{E}{x}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{2}\rho\omega^2 A^2. \end{align*} $$

The wave propagates along the string, so it delivers power to the newly oscillating length: $$ \begin{align*} \langle P\rangle = \left\langle\pdfd{E}{x}\right\rangle \dfd{x}{t} = \frac{1}{2}\rho\omega^2 A^2 c. \end{align*} $$

Standing waves open_in_new

A standing wave has wavefunction in separated form $$ \begin{align*} \psi(x,y,t) = X(x) Y(y) \cos\omega t. \end{align*} $$

Positions where $\psi=0$ are called nodes, and positions having maximum amplitude are called antinodes.

Upon reflection,

  • a fixed end ($\psi=0$) gives a phase change of $\pi$, but

  • a free end ($\partial\psi/\partial x = 0$) gives no phase change.

Solutions are quantised by an integer $n$. The $n=1$ solution is called fundamental, and higher values are overtones or harmonics.

Example (standing wave in pipes) open_in_new

The pressure difference from atmosphere satisfies $$ \begin{align*} \psi_p \propto \pdfd{\psi}{x}. \end{align*} $$

  • Open end has $\psi_p = 0 = \pdfd{\psi}{x}$;

  • Closed end has $\psi = 0$.

Huygen's principle open_in_new

  • Every point on a primary wavefront behaves as a source of spherical secondary wavelets;

  • The primary wavefront at a later time is the envelop of these wavelets.

Reflection and refraction open_in_new

A plane wave travels from a medium with refractive index $n_1 = c/v_1$ to one with $n_2 = c/v_2$.

(Usually larger $\lambda$ means smaller $n$.)

Law of reflection open_in_new

Angle of incidence $\theta_i = \theta_r$ angle of reflection. (Derivation)

If $n_2 > n_1$, there is a $\pi$ phase change upon reflection. If $n_2<n_1$, there is no phase change.

Snell's law open_in_new

Angle of incidence $\theta_1$ and angle of refraction $\theta_2$ are related by $$ \begin{align*} n_1 \sin \theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Total internal refraction occurs when $\sin\theta_2 > 1$.

Image-object relationship of refraction open_in_new

Refraction at a spherical surface of radius $R$: for small angles, $$ \begin{align*} \frac{n_1}{u} + \frac{n_2}{v} = \frac{n_2-n_1}{R} \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Take $R = \infty$ for planar surface. (Derivation)

Lens-maker's equation and lens equation open_in_new

A thin lens has focal length $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{u} + \frac{1}{v} = \left(\frac{n_2}{n_1}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\right) \equiv \frac{1}{f}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

The focal length $f$ is defined as the image distnace $v$ when $u=\infty$.

Sign convention:

Magnification of thin lens open_in_new

By similar triangles, $$ \begin{align*} M = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u} \end{align*} $$

Power of a lens open_in_new

Power of a lens $P$ (unit dioptres) is defined to be $$ \begin{align*} P = \frac{1}{f} \end{align*} $$

Astronomical telescope open_in_new

Angular magnification $$ \begin{align*} M \equiv \frac{\alpha_i}{\alpha_o} = \frac{f_o}{f_i}. \end{align*} $$

N-slit interference open_in_new

When the distance to screen $\gg$ slit separation $d$, we can assume outgoing light rays are parallel.

Angular position $\theta$ introduces a phase shift of $kd\sin\theta$.

  • For $N=2$ slits each with displacement $\psi=A_0\cos(\omega t)$, the intensity at screen is $$ \begin{align*} I \propto \cos^2\left(\frac{kd\sin\theta}{2}\right). \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

    • Maxima when $d\sin\theta = n\lambda \iff kd\sin\theta = 2n\pi$;

    • Minima when $d\sin\theta = (n+1/2)\lambda \iff kd\sin\theta = (2n+1)\pi$.

  • For a general $N$, add up the phasors.

    • Principal maxima when $d\sin\theta = n\lambda \iff kd\sin\theta = 2n\pi$.

    • Minima when $d\sin\theta = n\lambda/N \iff kd\sin\theta = 2n\pi/N$ where $n\in\Z$ is not a multiple of $N$.

    Non-examinable: $$ \begin{align*} A = \frac{\sin((Nkd\sin\theta)/2)}{\sin((kd\sin\theta)/2)}A_0. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Rayleigh criterion open_in_new

To resolve two lines in diffraction spectrum, the maximum of one line lies over the first minimum of the other.

For an $N$ slit grating to resolve $\lambda_1 < \lambda_2$ in the first-order spectrum, if angles are small, $$ \begin{align*} N = \frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Single slit diffraction open_in_new

For a single slit of finite width $a$, the amplitude at screen is $$ \begin{align*} A = \frac{\sin((ka\sin\theta)/2)}{(ka\sin\theta)/2} A_0. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Minima when $a\sin\theta = n\lambda$.

Quantum waves open_in_new

De Broglie wavelength and frequency open_in_new

Matter behaves as waves. De Broglie argues that the wavelength and frequency are $$ \begin{align*} E = hf = \hbar\omega, \\ p = \frac{h}{\lambda} = \hbar k. \end{align*} $$ where $h$ is Planck's constant and $\hbar = h/2\pi$ is reduced Planck's constant.

Wavefunction of matter wave open_in_new

Matter wave has a complex wavefunction $\hat\psi(x,t)$, where $|\hat\psi(x)|^2$ is the probability density of observing a particle at $x$.

Therefore we have to normalise $$ \begin{align*} \int_{\R} |\hat\psi|^2 dx = 1. \end{align*} $$

De Broglie wave has wavefunction $$ \begin{align*} \hat\psi\propto \exp i(kx-\omega t). \end{align*} $$

Schrodinger's equation open_in_new

The wavefunction $\hat\psi(x,t)$ satisfies the time-dependent Schrodinger's equation $$ \begin{align*} i\hbar \pdfd{\hat\psi}{t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pdfd[2]{\hat\psi}{x} + V(x,t)\hat\psi \end{align*} $$ where $V(x,t)$ is the potential energy.

If $V$ and total energy are constant, we have the time-independent Schrodinger's equation $$ \begin{align*} E\hat\psi = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\pdfd[2]{\hat\psi}{x} + V(x)\hat\psi \iff \pdfd[2]{\hat\psi}{x} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E-V(x))\hat\psi = 0. \end{align*} $$

In higher dimensions, $$ \begin{align*} \nabla^2\hat\psi + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\left(E-V(x)\right)\hat\psi = 0. \end{align*} $$

Multiply by $e^{-i\omega t}$ to get the time-dependent solution.

Boundary conditions:

  • $V=\infty\implies\hat\psi = 0$.

  • $\hat\psi$ is continuous, since otherwise $p\propto ik\hat\psi = \partial\hat\psi/\partial x = \pm\infty$.

  • $\partial\hat\psi/\partial x$ is continuous, since otherwise $E\propto \partial^2\hat\psi/\partial x^2 = \pm\infty$.

Solution to Schrodinger's equation for constant potentials open_in_new

If $V$ is constant in some region, then we have

  • for $E>V$, $$ \begin{align*} \hat\psi(x) = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx}, \quad k = \frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V)}}{\hbar}, \end{align*} $$

  • for $E<V$,$$ \begin{align*} \hat\psi(x) = Ce^{\kappa x} + De^{-\kappa x}, \quad \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V-E)}}{\hbar}. \end{align*} $$

(Derivation)

Infinite potential barrier open_in_new

In 1D, for $V(x) = \infty$ outside $[0,a]$, we impose that $\hat\psi(0) = \hat\psi(L) = 0$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \hat\psi(x) = A\sin kx, \qquad A = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}, \quad \frac{\sqrt{2m(E-V)}}{\hbar} = k = \frac{n\pi}{a}. \end{align*} $$ Therefore the energy is quantised: $$ \begin{align*} E_n - V = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}n^2. \end{align*} $$

In 3D, for $V(x,y,z) = \infty$ outside the box $[0,a]\times[0,b]\times[0,c]$, we can separate the equation $$ \begin{align*} \nabla^2\hat\psi + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E-V(x,y,z)) = 0 \implies \begin{cases} \pdfd[2]{\hat\psi_x}{x} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_x - V_x(x)) &= 0 \\ \pdfd[2]{\hat\psi_y}{y} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_y - V_y(y)) &= 0 \\ \pdfd[2]{\hat\psi_z}{z} + \frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E_z - V_z(z)) &= 0 \\ \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ so $$ \begin{align*} \hat\psi(x,y,z) &= A\sin (k_x x) \sin (k_y y) \sin(k_z z), \\ A &= \sqrt{\frac{2}{a} \frac{2}{b} \frac{2}{c}}, \\ E &= \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n_x^2}{a^2} + \frac{n_y^2}{b^2} + \frac{n_z^2}{c^2}\right). \end{align*} $$

(For finite potential barrier, we can assume the amplitude reflected from the further edge is small to simplify)

Hydrogen atom open_in_new

With the potential energy between an electron and a proton $$ \begin{align*} V(r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \frac{e^2}{r}, \end{align*} $$ solutions to the Schrodinger's equation have quantised energy levels $$ \begin{align*} E_n = -\frac{me^4}{8\epsilon_0^2h^2} \frac{1}{n^2} = -\frac{13.6 \mathrm{\ eV}}{n^2}. \end{align*} $$
