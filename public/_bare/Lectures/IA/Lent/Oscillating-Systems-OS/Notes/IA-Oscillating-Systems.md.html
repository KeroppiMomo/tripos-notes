Basic properties of oscillation open_in_new

For a system characterised by $x(t)$ which varies in time,

  • period $T$ is the shortest time-period for which $x(t+T) = x(t)$;

  • frequency $\nu$ is how many cycles the system go through per unit time: $$ \nu = \frac1T. $$

  • Angular frequency $\omega$ appears sinusoidal function $\cos \omega t$: $$ \omega = \frac\nu{2\pi}, \quad T = \frac{2\pi}\omega. $$

Simple harmonic motion open_in_new

A motion is harmonic when its frequency is independent of the amplitude.

A system is in simple harmonic motion if it satisfies $$ \ddot x = -\omega^2 x. $$

It has solutions in the form $$ x(t) = a_0 \cos(\omega t+\phi) \equiv A\cos\omega t + B\sin\omega t. $$

Derivatives of position in SHM open_in_new

The velocity $\dot x$ is $\pi/2$ phase ahead of $x$: $$ \dot x(t) = -\omega a_0\sin(\omega t + \phi) = \omega a_0\cos\big(\omega t+\phi+\frac\pi2\big) $$ and the acceleration is $\pi$ phase ahead: $$ \ddot x(t) = -\omega^2 a_0\cos(\omega t + \phi) = \omega^2 a_0\cos(\omega t+\phi+\pi) $$

Energy in SHM open_in_new

Energy in a spring system open_in_new

Consider a spring system $$ m\ddot x = -kx \implies x=a_0 \cos(\omega t+\phi), \quad \omega^2=\frac km. $$

Its potential energy is $$ U = \frac12 kx^2 = \frac12 ka_0^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi) \implies \langle U\rangle = \frac14 ka_0^2. $$ Its kinetic energy is $$ K = \frac12 m\dot x^2 = \frac12 ma_0^2\omega^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi) = \frac12 ka_0^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi) \implies \langle K\rangle = \frac14 ka_0^2. $$ The total energy is conserved: $$ E = U+K = \frac12 ka_0^2. $$

SHM from energy conservation open_in_new

When the total energy of the system is in the form $$ E = \frac12 \alpha \dot x^2 + \frac12 \beta x^2 $$ is conserved, the system is in simple harmonic motion with angular frequency $$ \omega = \sqrt{\frac\beta\alpha}. $$ (Derivation)

Phasor open_in_new

The physical value is obtained by projecting the phasor onto the axis.

Superposition of SHM open_in_new

For superposition of oscillations with equal frequency, can use phasors to figure out amplitude and phase.

Beats: suppose two SHM with similar frequency and equal amplitude interferes. $$ a_0\cos\omega_1 t + a_0\cos\omega_2 t = 2a_0\cos\Big(\frac{\omega_1+\omega_2}2 t\Big) \cos\Big(\frac{\omega_1-\omega_2}2 t\Big). $$ The amplitude oscillates with angular frequency $(\omega_1-\omega_2)/2$.

Complex representation of SHM open_in_new

For physical $x = a_0\cos(\omega t+\phi)$, write $$ z = a_0 e^{i(\omega t+\phi)} = A e^{i\omega t}, \quad A\in\C. $$

Physical values: $$\begin{align*} x &= \Re z \\ \dot x &= \Re\dot z = \Re i\omega z = -\omega\Im z \\ \ddot x &= \Re\ddot z = -\omega^2\Re z \\ U &= \frac12 k(\Re z)^2 \\ K &= \frac12 m(\Re \dot z)^2 = \frac12 k(\Im z)^2 \end{align*}$$

Damped harmonic motion open_in_new

When we introduce a force opposing and proportional to velocity, we get the equation of a damped harmonic motion: $$ \ddot x + 2\gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = 0.$$

Solutions to damped harmonic motion open_in_new

We have characteristic equation $$ \lambda^2 + 2\gamma\lambda + \omega_0^2 = 0 \implies \lambda = -\gamma \pm \sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}. $$

  • Heavy damping $\gamma>\omega_0$ is non-oscillatory. $$ x(t) = Ae^{-\big(\gamma+\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}\big)t} + Be^{-\big(\gamma-\sqrt{\gamma^2-\omega_0^2}\big)t}. $$

  • Light damping $\gamma<\omega_0$ oscillates with decaying amplitude. $$ x(t) = a_0 e^{-\gamma t}\cos(\omega_d t+\phi),\quad \omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \gamma^2} < \omega_0. $$ Note that the oscillation frequency is smaller with damping. When $\gamma\ll\omega_0$, $\omega_d \approx \omega_0$.

  • Critical damping $\gamma = \omega_0$ has the quickest decay without oscillation. $$ x(t) = Ae^{-\gamma t} + Bte^{-\gamma t}. $$

Decay in damped harmonic motion open_in_new

We can verify that the energy decay rate is exactly the power of the friction force: $$ \dot E = \dd t\Big(\frac12 m\dot x^2 + \frac12 kx^2\Big) = \dot x(m\ddot x + kx) = -b\dot x^2. $$

For light damping, the amplitude of the oscillator at time $t_n$ where the $n$-th maximum displacement occurs is $$ a_n = a_0 e^{-\gamma t_n} $$ so the characteristic time of amplitude decay is $1/\gamma$.

But for energy, $$ E_n = \frac12 ka_n^2 = \frac 12 ka_0^2 e^{-2\gamma t_n} $$ so the characteristic time of energy decay is $1/2\gamma$.

Comparing oscillators open_in_new

We want to combine the two characteristic times of decay time $\tau=1/2\gamma$ and oscillation period $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$.

Logarithmic decrement open_in_new

If the logarithmic decrement is $\Delta$, the _amplitude_ of successive oscillations decays by a factor of $e^{-\Delta}$. The formula is $$ \Delta = \frac{2\pi\gamma}{\omega_d}. $$ (Derivation)

The energy of successive oscillations decays by a factor of $e^{-2\Delta}$.

Quality factor open_in_new

The qualify factor $Q$ is defined as $$ Q = \frac{\omega_0}{2\gamma}. $$

For good oscillators, $Q$ represents the number of radians of oscillation required for the _energy_ to fall by a factor of $e$. (Derivation) Such number for the amplitude is $2Q$.

For good oscillators, $$ Q = \frac\pi\Delta. $$

Forced oscillations open_in_new

We investigate the equation $$ \ddot x + 2\gamma \dot x + \omega_0^2 x = f\cos\omega t. $$

Solutions to forced oscillations open_in_new

(Derivation) The solution is $x(t) = a_0 \cos(\omega t + \phi)$ where the complex amplitude is $$ A = \frac{f}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + 2i\gamma\omega} \implies \begin{cases} a_0 &= \dfrac{f}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2 \omega^2}}, \\ \tan\phi&= \dfrac{2\gamma\omega}{\omega^2 - \omega_0^2}. \end{cases}$$

Power lost in forced oscillations open_in_new

In steady state, power given by driving force = power dissipated by damping: $$ \langle P\rangle = b\langle \dot x^2\rangle = \frac b2\frac{f^2\omega^2}{(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)^2 + 4\gamma^2\omega^2} = \frac b2 \frac{f^2}{\big((\omega_0^2 - \omega^2)/\omega\big)^2 + 4\gamma^2} $$ which peaks when $\omega=\omega_0$.

Lorentzian distribution (non-examinable): for $\omega \approx \omega_0$, we have the approximation $$ \begin{align*} \langle P\rangle = \frac{b}{2} \frac{f^2}{\left((\omega_0 - \omega)(\omega_0+\omega)/\omega\right)^2 + 4\gamma^2} \approx \frac{b}{8}\frac{f^2}{(\omega_0-\omega)^2+\gamma^2 .} \end{align*} $$

Resonance width open_in_new

Quantify the width of the resonance peak by how far from resonant frequency $\omega$ can be before the power absorbed falls by half.

Resonance peak width $$ \delta\omega = 2\gamma \implies \frac{\delta\omega}{\omega_0} = \frac1Q. $$

Electric circuits open_in_new

Circuit basics open_in_new

  • Current: unit $\mathrm{A} = \mathrm{C\ s\inv}$. In a material, $$ \begin{align*} I = qnA\langle v\rangle \end{align*} $$ where $q$ is charge of each charge carrier, $n$ is number density (number of charges per unit volume), $A$ is cross-sectional area, $\langle v\rangle$ is the time-average drift velocity.

  • Voltage: unit $\mathrm{V} = \mathrm{J\ C\inv}$. $$ \begin{align*} qV = W = \int_{a}^{b} q\v E\cdot d\v x. \end{align*} $$

  • Emf: unit $\mathrm{V}$.

  • Resistance: unit $\mathrm{\Omega} = \mathrm{V\ A\inv}$. Experimentally we have Ohm's law $$ \begin{align*} V = IR. \end{align*} $$ Equivalently, $$ \begin{align*} R = \frac{l}{\sigma A} \end{align*} $$ where $\sigma$ is the conductivity of the material.

  • Power: $$ \begin{align*} W = QV \implies P = \dot W = IV = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}. \end{align*} $$

Kirchhoff's laws open_in_new

  • At each junction, $\sum I = 0$, with currents going in and out having opposite signs.

  • Around every loop, $\sum V = 0$.

Tricks: current loops, assigning voltages to wires.

Summary for sign conventions:

Capacitor open_in_new

For two parallel conducting plates with charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, the potential between them satisfies $$ \begin{align*} Q = CV \end{align*} $$ where $C$ is the capacitance, with unit farad $\mathrm{F} = \mathrm{C\ V\inv}$.

  • Parallel plate capacitor with area $A$ and separation $d$ $$ \begin{align*} C = \frac{A\epsilon_0}{d} \end{align*} $$

  • Spherical conductor with radius $a$ (the other charges are distributed infinitely far away) $$ \begin{align*} C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 a \end{align*} $$

  • Cylindrical capacitor with length $l$, inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$ $$ \begin{align*} \frac{C}{l} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}. \end{align*} $$

Energy of capacitor open_in_new

Energy stored in the capacitor = work done to charge up the capacitor $$ \begin{align*} W = \int_{0}^{Q} \frac{q}{C}\, dq = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{1}{2}CV^2. \end{align*} $$

This can also be thought of energy stored in the electric field $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int ρΦ \d V = \frac{1}{2}(+Q)Φ_+ + \frac{1}{2}(-Q)Φ_- = \frac{1}{2}QV. \end{align*} $$

This is the stress (pressure) on the plates caused by the field (e.g. from direct calculation or using virtual work argument by pulling apart the plates).

RC circuit open_in_new

Characteristic time for charge decay/gain $\tau = RC$, and so the characteristic time for energy stored is $RC/2$.

  • Discharge: $$ \begin{align*} \dot QR + \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \implies Q(t) = Q(0) e^{-t/RC}. \end{align*} $$

  • Charge with battery (emf $\mathcal{E})$ from $Q(0)=0$: $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} - \dot Q R - \frac{Q}{C} = 0 \implies Q(t) = C\mathcal{E}(1-e^{-t/RC}). \end{align*} $$

    • $\Delta E_{\text{battery}} = -Q\mathcal{E}$, $\Delta E_{\text{capacitor}} = Q\mathcal{E}/2$, $\Delta E_{\text{resistor}} = Q\mathcal{E}/2$.

Inductor open_in_new

Magnetic field $B$ has unit tesla $\mathrm{T} = \mathrm{N\ s\ C\inv\ m\inv} = \mathrm{kg\ s\inv\ C\inv}$.

Electromagnetic induction (Faraday's law) $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = -\dfd{\phi_B}{t} \end{align*} $$ where $\phi_B = \int_{S} \v B\cdot d\v A$ is the magnetic flux, with unit weber $\mathrm{Wb} = \mathrm{T\ m^2}$.

An inductor has self-inductance $L$, which has unit henry $\mathrm{H} = \mathrm{T\ m^2\ A\inv} = \mathrm{V\ s^2\ C\inv}$, defined as $$ \begin{align*} \phi_B = LI \implies \mathcal{E} = -L\dot I. \end{align*} $$

Energy stored in inductor open_in_new

Energy stored in inductor = work done to build up a current $$ \begin{align*} W = \int_{0}^{T} L\dfd{I}{t}\cdot I\, dt = \frac{1}{2}LI^2. \end{align*} $$

Simple circuit with inductor open_in_new

  • Connecting with a battery: $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{E} = L\dot I \implies I(t) = I(0) + \frac{\mathcal{E}}{L}t. \end{align*} $$

  • RL circuit: characteristic time for current decay $\tau = L/R$, and so characteristic time for energy decay $L/(2R)$ $$ \begin{align*} RI + L\dot I = 0 \implies I(t) = I(0) e^{-(R/L)t} \end{align*} $$

Free oscillations in circuits open_in_new

  • LC circuit: $$ \begin{align*} \frac{q}{C} + L\ddot q = 0 \iff \frac{1}{2}\frac{q^2}{C} + \frac{1}{2}L\dot q^2 = \text{const.} \\ \end{align*} $$ which has simple harmonic motion as the solution $$ \begin{align*} \omega_0^2 = \frac{1}{LC}. \end{align*} $$

  • RLC circuit: $$ \begin{align*} L\ddot q + R\dot q + \frac{q}{C} = 0 \end{align*} $$ which is a damped oscillation with $\gamma = R/2L$ and $\omega_0^2 = 1/LC$. Quality factor $Q = (1/R)\sqrt{L/C}$.

AC circuits open_in_new

We use complex voltage $V = V_0 e^{i\omega t}$ and complex current $I = I_0 e^{i\omega t}$.

Note that $P = \Re(V) \Re(I) \neq \Re(VI)$.

Impedence open_in_new

The complex impedence $Z$ of an electric element is defined using $$ \begin{align*} V = ZI. \end{align*} $$

  • Resistor: $Z_R = R$ because $V = IR$.

  • Capacitor: $Z_C = 1/i\omega C$ because $V = q/C = I/i\omega C$.

  • Inductor: $Z_L = i\omega L$ because $V = L\dot I = i\omega LI$.

Equivalence impedence:

  • In series $$ \begin{align*} Z = \sum_i Z_i \implies R = \sum_i R_i, \quad \frac{1}{C} = \sum_i \frac{1}{C_i}, \quad L = \sum_i L_i. \end{align*} $$

  • In parallel $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{Z} = \sum_i \frac{1}{Z_i} \implies \frac{1}{R} = \sum_i \frac{1}{R_i} , \quad C = \sum_i C_i, \quad \frac{1}{L} = \sum_i L_i. \end{align*} $$

RC filter open_in_new

Connect an AC source with voltage $V = V_0 e^{i\omega t}$ to a resistor $R$ and capacitor $C$, and measure the voltage across the capacitor.

Then using potential divider formula, $$ \begin{align*} V_C = V \frac{Z_C}{Z_C + Z_R} = \frac{1}{i\omega RC + 1}V \implies |V_C| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{(\omega RC)^2 + 1}} V_0. \end{align*} $$ So this is a low-pass filter, i.e. large $|V_C / V|$ for small $\omega$.

Driven LCR circuit open_in_new

$$ \begin{align*} \frac{q}{C} + R\dot q + L\ddot q = v_0\cos(\omega t) \end{align*} $$ For steady state response, $q$ oscillates at $\omega$, so we can use impendence $I = V/Z$ where $Z = 1/i\omega C + R + i\omega L$. $$ \begin{align*} |I| = \frac{v_0}{\sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L - 1/\omega C)^2}} \end{align*} $$ At resonance $\omega = \omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, the current is at a maximum with $Z = R$.

Power dissipated in the circuit is only by the resistor, so $$ \begin{align*} \langle P\rangle = \frac{1}{2}|I|^2 R = \frac{v_0^2 R}{2\left(R^2 + (\omega L-1/\omega C)^2\right)} \end{align*} $$ which has maximum power $\langle P\rangle = v_0^2/2R$ at resonance.

Width of resonant power peak (difference of $\omega$ at half maximum power) $$ \begin{align*} \delta\omega = \frac{R}{L} \implies \frac{\delta\omega}{\omega_0} = R\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} = \frac{1}{Q}. \end{align*} $$ This can be used as a selective amplifier/filter.
