Newton's law of universal gravitation open_in_new

For two point masses $m_1, m_2$ with separation $\v r$, the gravitational force is an attractive force: $$ \begin{align*} \v F = -G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}\vhat r \end{align*} $$ where $G$ is the universal gravitational constant.

The gravitational field is given by $$ \begin{align*} \v g = -\frac{Gm}{r^2} \vhat r. \end{align*} $$

This uses the gravitational mass, which differs conceptually from the inertial mass used in $\v F = m\v a$. But experimentally we found the two masses equal.

Gauss' law for gravitation open_in_new

For any surface $S$ (Gaussian surface), the gravitational field $\v g$ and the mass enclosed is related by $$ \begin{align*} \int_S \v g(\v r)\cdot d\v A = -4\pi G \sum_i M_i \end{align*} $$

Use this to compute the gravitational field due to a long rod ($g(r) = 2G\rho/r$) and a large plane ($g = 2\pi G\rho$).

Gravitational potential energy open_in_new

We can define a potential energy in a conservative force field $\v F$: $$ \begin{align*} \v F &= -\nabla U \\ dU &= -\v F\cdot d\v r \\ U(\v r) - U(\v r_0) &= -\int_{\v r_0}^{\v r} \v F(\v r')\cdot d\v r' \end{align*} $$ Physically, potential energy is the work done by an external force against the field in moving a body from [the point of zero potential energy] to the present location at constant velocity.

We define a gravitational potential $$ \begin{align*} \Phi = \frac{U}{m} \implies \v g = -\nabla\Phi. \end{align*} $$ We set $\Phi(\infty) = 0$. A point mass source gives $$ \begin{align*} \Phi(\v r) = -\frac{GM}{r} \end{align*} $$ To add a new point mass $m$ to a system at position $\v r$, the potential energy gained is $$ \begin{align*} U(\v r) = -\sum_i \frac{GmM_i}{|\v r - \v R_i|}. \end{align*} $$ The total gravitational potential energy of a system is the potential gained by bringing the masses together from infinity: $$ \begin{align*} U = -\sum_{i<j} \frac{GM_iM_j}{r_{ij}} = -\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\neq j} \frac{GM_iM_j}{r_{ij}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i M_i\Phi_i \end{align*} $$ where $\Phi_i$ is the potential at the position of $M_i$ due to all other masses.

For example, the gravitational potential energy of a uniform sphere with mass $M$ and radius $a$ is $$ \begin{align*} U = -\frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{a}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Kepler's laws open_in_new

Kepler's first law open_in_new

  1. Each planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus.

At one focus, an ellipse has polar equation $$ \begin{align*} r = \frac{r_0}{1+e\cos\theta} \end{align*} $$ where $r_0$ is the semi latus rectum and $e$ is the eccentricity.

At the centre, it has Cartesian equation $$ \begin{align*} \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \end{align*} $$ where $a,b$ are the semi major axis and semi minor axis.

Those quantities are related by $$ \begin{align*} r_0 &= a(1-e^2), && b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

In general, energy $E$ classifies orbits:

  • $E<0$: we have $0\leq e<1$, which is a (bound) elliptical orbit.

  • $E=0$: we have $e=1$, which is a parabolic orbit.

  • $E>0$: we have $e>1$, which is a hyperbolic orbit.

Non-examinable: using the Runge-Lenz vector $\v R = \v v\times \v L - GMm\vhat r$ (Derivation), we can show that the orbit is a conic section with parameter $$ \begin{align*} r_0 = \frac{L^2}{GMm^2}, \quad e = \sqrt{1+\frac{2L^2E}{G^2M^2m^3}}. \end{align*} $$

Kepler's second law open_in_new

  1. The line joining the Sun to a particular planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time.

Angular momentum is conserved since gravity is a central force. Area swept per unit time is $$ \begin{align*} \dfd{A}{t} = \frac{1}{2}|\v r\times \v v| = \frac{L}{2m} \end{align*} $$ so it is constant.

Kepler's third law open_in_new

  1. $(\text{Period of orbit})^2 \propto (\text{Semimajor axis})^3$.

$$ \begin{align*} T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{GM} \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Often using the apsides (singular apsis) i.e. perihelion (nearest point) and aphelion (furthest point) is useful.
