Electrostatic force open_in_new

For two point charges $q_1,q_2$ with separation $\v r$, the Coulomb force is $$ \begin{align*} \v F = \frac{q_1q_2}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\vhat r. \end{align*} $$

We define the electric field $\v E$ as $$ \begin{align*} \v E = \frac{\v F_{electric}}{q} \end{align*} $$ so due to a point charge, $$ \begin{align*} \v E = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}\vhat r. \end{align*} $$

The electric flux density $\v D$ is defined as $$ \begin{align*} \int_S \v D\cdot d\v A = Q_\text{enclosed}. \end{align*} $$

Using divergence theorem, we obtain Gauss' law for electric field $$ \begin{align*} \v D &= \epsilon_0\v E, &\int_S \v E\cdot d\v A &= \frac{Q_\text{enclosed}}{\epsilon_0}, &\nabla\cdot\v E = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}. \end{align*} $$

Electrostatic potential open_in_new

Electric potential is defined in a conservative electric field as $$ \begin{align*} \v E = -\nabla\Phi. \end{align*} $$ A point charge $Q$ gives an electrostatic potential $$ \begin{align*} \Phi = \frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r}. \end{align*} $$

Voltage is potential difference.

Conductors open_in_new

Charges can move freely on conductors. Therefore in steady state, $\v E = \v 0$ inside conductor, $\v E\perp$ surface, and so $\Phi$ is constant everywhere on the conductor. Otherwise free charges would move in response to the electric force and a current would flow.

Charges redistribute to stay away from each other, so they go to the surface. Therefore external electric fields induce charges on the surface. If there is charges inside, taking a Gaussian surface around that charge gives $\v E\neq 0$, which is impossible.

Inside a hollow conductor, using a Gaussian surface containing the cavity, we get that the net charge is zero. If there is unbalanced charges, there will be field lines so there is potential difference. But the inside surface is equipotential, so there can't be any charges and $\v E = 0$.

A surface charge of $\sigma$ gives a electric field immediately outside the surface $$ \begin{align*} \v E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} \vhat n. \end{align*} $$

Capacitor open_in_new

For two parallel conducting plates with charges $+Q$ and $-Q$, the potential between them satisfies $$ \begin{align*} Q = CV \end{align*} $$ where $C$ is the capacitance, with unit farad $\mathrm{F} = \mathrm{C\ V\inv}$.

  • Parallel plate capacitor with area $A$ and separation $d$ $$ \begin{align*} C = \frac{A\epsilon_0}{d} \end{align*} $$

  • Spherical conductor with radius $a$ (the other charges are distributed infinitely far away) $$ \begin{align*} C = 4\pi\epsilon_0 a \end{align*} $$

  • Cylindrical capacitor with length $l$, inner radius $a$, outer radius $b$ $$ \begin{align*} \frac{C}{l} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}. \end{align*} $$

Energy of capacitor open_in_new

Energy stored in the capacitor = work done to charge up the capacitor $$ \begin{align*} W = \int_{0}^{Q} \frac{q}{C}\, dq = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}QV = \frac{1}{2}CV^2. \end{align*} $$

This can also be thought of energy stored in the electric field $$ \begin{align*} U = \frac{1}{2}\int ρΦ \d V = \frac{1}{2}(+Q)Φ_+ + \frac{1}{2}(-Q)Φ_- = \frac{1}{2}QV. \end{align*} $$

This is the stress (pressure) on the plates caused by the field (e.g. from direct calculation or using virtual work argument by pulling apart the plates).

Electric dipole open_in_new

Let $\v d$ to be the vector from a $-q$ charge to a $+q$ charge. Define the dipole moment as $$ \begin{align*} \v p = q\v d. \end{align*} $$

Force on electric dipole open_in_new

Torque $$ \begin{align*} \v G = \v d\times(q\v E) = \v p\times \v E. \end{align*} $$

Force $$ \begin{align*} \v F = q\v E(\v r + \v d) - q\v E(\v r) \approx q(\v d\cdot\nabla)\v E = (\v p\cdot\nabla)\v E. \end{align*} $$

Potential and field due to electric dipole open_in_new

For $|\v x|\ll|\v d|$, the electric potential and field due to the dipole is $$ \begin{align*} \Phi(\v x) &= \frac{\v p\cdot \v x}{4\pi\epsilon_0 |\v x|^3} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}, \\ \v E(\v r) &= \frac{3(\v p\cdot \v x)\v x - |\v x|^2 \v p}{4πε_0 |\v x|^5} &&= \frac{p\cos\theta}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat r + \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\vhat\theta \end{align*} $$ where $\theta$ is the angle between $\v x$ and $\v p$. (Derivation) Note that $\Phi\propto 1/r^2, E\propto 1/r^3$.

Therefore on axis ($\theta = 0$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{2\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ parallel to axis} \end{align*} $$ and off axis ($\theta = \pi/2$), $$ \begin{align*} E(r) &= \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3} \text{ anti-parallel to axis}. \end{align*} $$

A point dipole $\v p$ at the origin correspond to $$ \begin{align*} ρ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇δ(\v x) \end{align*} $$ so by linearity of Poisson's equation, $$ \begin{align*} Φ(\v x) = -\v p\cdot ∇\left(\frac{1}{4πε_0 |\v x|}\right). \end{align*} $$

In atoms and molecules, external electric fields causes polarisation and induces $\v p\propto \v E_\text{ext}$. This gives long-range forces between them:

  • Dipole-dipole: dipoles line up

  • Dipole-induced dipole

  • Fluctuating dipoles: dipoles synchronise and fluctuate in the same direction.
