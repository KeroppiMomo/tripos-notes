Kepler's first law open_in_new

  1. Each planet moves in an elliptical orbit with the Sun at one focus.

At one focus, an ellipse has polar equation $$ \begin{align*} r = \frac{r_0}{1+e\cos\theta} \end{align*} $$ where $r_0$ is the semi latus rectum and $e$ is the eccentricity.

At the centre, it has Cartesian equation $$ \begin{align*} \frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1 \end{align*} $$ where $a,b$ are the semi major axis and semi minor axis.

Those quantities are related by $$ \begin{align*} r_0 &= a(1-e^2), && b = a\sqrt{1 - e^2}. \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

In general, energy $E$ classifies orbits:

  • $E<0$: we have $0\leq e<1$, which is a (bound) elliptical orbit.

  • $E=0$: we have $e=1$, which is a parabolic orbit.

  • $E>0$: we have $e>1$, which is a hyperbolic orbit.

Non-examinable: using the Runge-Lenz vector $\v R = \v v\times \v L - GMm\vhat r$ (Derivation), we can show that the orbit is a conic section with parameter $$ \begin{align*} r_0 = \frac{L^2}{GMm^2}, \quad e = \sqrt{1+\frac{2L^2E}{G^2M^2m^3}}. \end{align*} $$

Kepler's second law open_in_new

  1. The line joining the Sun to a particular planet sweeps out equal areas in equal time.

Angular momentum is conserved since gravity is a central force. Area swept per unit time is $$ \begin{align*} \dfd{A}{t} = \frac{1}{2}|\v r\times \v v| = \frac{L}{2m} \end{align*} $$ so it is constant.

Kepler's third law open_in_new

  1. $(\text{Period of orbit})^2 \propto (\text{Semimajor axis})^3$.

$$ \begin{align*} T^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{GM} \end{align*} $$ (Derivation)

Often using the apsides (singular apsis) i.e. perihelion (nearest point) and aphelion (furthest point) is useful.
