Proof in lecture

To see that $\sign$ is well-defined, suppose $\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_k$ and $\tau'_1,\ldots,\tau'_l$ are transpositions such that $$ \tau_1\tau_2\ldots\tau_k = \tau'_1\tau'_2\ldots\tau'_l. $$ Then by $\tau_i\inv = \tau_i$, $$ \tau_k\tau_{k-1}\ldots\tau_2\tau_1\ \tau'_1\tau'_2\ldots\tau'_l = e $$ so by the previous lemma, $$\begin{align*} k+l&\equiv 0\mod 2\\ k&\equiv l\mod 2 \\ (-1)^k&=(-1)^l \end{align*}$$ so $\sign(\tau_1\ldots\tau_k) = \sign(\tau'_1\ldots\tau'_l)$ as required.

To see that $\sign$ is a homomorphism, note that $$\begin{align*} \sign(\tau_1\ldots\tau_k \tau'_1\ldots\tau'_l) &= (-1)^{k+l} \\ &= (-1)^k (-1)^l \\ &= \sign(\tau_1\ldots\tau_k)\sign(\tau'_1\ldots\tau'_l). \end{align*}$$

(Shorter) proof in supervision

For $\sigma\in S_n$, let $\nu(\sigma)$ be the number of cycles (including singletons) in the disjoint cycle notation of $\sigma$.

We will show by induction that if $\sigma = \tau_1 \tau_2 \ldots \tau_k$ where $\tau_i$ are transpositions, then $$ \begin{align*} \nu(\sigma) + n \equiv k \mod 2 \end{align*} $$ For $k=0$, $\sigma=\mathrm{Id}$ so $\nu(\sigma) = n$ so $\nu(\sigma) + n \equiv 0$.

For a general $k$, let $\sigma = \tau \sigma'$ where $\tau = \tau_1 = \pmat{a & b}$ and $\sigma' = \tau_2 \ldots \tau_k$. We have two cases:

  • If $a,b$ are in the same cycle in $\sigma'$, then letting $$ \begin{align*} \sigma' = \pmat{a & x_1 & \ldots & x_p & b & y_1 & \ldots & y_q}\sigma'' \end{align*} $$ in disjoint cycle notation where $\sigma''$ is disjoint from the first cycle gives $$ \begin{align*} \sigma = \pmat{a & b}\sigma' = \pmat{a & x_1 & \ldots & x_p} \pmat{b & y_1 & \ldots & y_q} \sigma'' \end{align*} $$ so $\nu(\sigma) = \nu(\sigma') + 1$.

  • If $a,b$ are in different cycles in $\sigma'$, then letting $$ \begin{align*} \sigma' = \pmat{a & b}\sigma' = \pmat{a & x_1 & \ldots & x_p} \pmat{b & y_1 & \ldots & y_q} \sigma'' \end{align*} $$ in disjoint cycle notation gives $$ \begin{align*} \sigma = \pmat{a & b}\sigma' = \pmat{a & x_1 & \ldots & x_p & b & y_1 & \ldots & y_q}\sigma'' \end{align*} $$ so $\nu(\sigma) = \nu(\sigma') - 1$.
