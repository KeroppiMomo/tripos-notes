Definition (affine zero locus, affine algebraic set) open_in_new

Let $S \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ a subset of polynomials in $n$ variables.

The zero locus or vanishing set of $S$ in $\mathbb A^n$ is $$ \begin{align*} Z(S) = \sb{p\in \mathbb{A}^n}{f(p) = 0 \; \forall f\in S} \end{align*} $$ a subset of the affine $n$-space $\mathbb{A}^n$.

An affine algebraic set or affine variety is any set of the form $Z(S)$.

If $f\in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is non-constant, $Z(f)$ is called a hypersurface. If $f$ is linear, $Z(f)$ is called a hyperplane.

If $I\lhd k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the ideal generated by $S$, then $Z(S) = Z(I)$.

For any $S \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, there exists $f_1, \ldots, f_r\in S$ such that $Z(S) = Z(f_1, \ldots, f_r)$. (Proof)

$\emptyset = Z(1)$ and $\mathbb{A}^n = Z(0)$ are affine algebraic sets.

$Z$ is inclusion-reversing: if $S\subseteq T \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, then $Z(T) \subseteq Z(S)$.

$\bigcap_{i} Z(S_i) = Z(\bigcup_{i} S_i)$ for any collection $\set{S_i\subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]}_{i}$.

$Z(S) \cup Z(T) = Z(S\cdot T)$ for $S, T\subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, where $S\cdot T = \sb{f\cdot g}{f\in S, g\in T}$. (Proof)

Definition (projective space) open_in_new

Let $k = \bar k$ be an algebraically closed field of characteristic 0.

The projective $n$-space over $k$ is $$ \begin{align*} \mathbb{P}^n := \mathbb{P}_k^n := \mathbb{P}^n(k) &:= \set{\text{lines through 0 in }k^{n+1}} \\ &= \set{(a_0, \ldots, a_n)\in k^{n+1} \setminus \set{0}}/{\sim} \end{align*} $$ where $(a_0, \ldots, a_n)\sim (b_0, \ldots, b_n)$ if there exists $λ\in k^\times$ such that $a_i = λb_i$ for all $0\leq i\leq n$.

We write $(a_0: a_1: \ldots: a_n)$ (or $[a_0: a_1: \ldots : a_n]$) for the class of $(a_0, \ldots, a_n)$.

We call $x_0, \ldots, x_n$ the homogeneous coordinates on $\mathbb{P}^{n}$. (But they are no longer well-defined functions on $\mathbb{P}^n$.)

$\set{x_0 = 0} = \set{(0 : a_1 : \ldots : a_n)\in \mathbb{P}^n}$ can be identified with $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$, and $\set{x_0 \neq 0} = \set{(1: a_1: \ldots: a_n)\in \mathbb{P}^n}$ can be identified with $\mathbb{A}^n$.

Considering $U_j := \set{x_j\neq 0}\simeq \mathbb{A}^{n}$ for various $j$, we have $\mathbb{P}^n = U_0 \cup U_1 \cup \ldots \cup U_n$ called the standard affine patches.

Definition (homogeneous polynomial) open_in_new

A polynomial $F\in k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ is said to be homogeneous of degree $d$ if it is any sum of monomials of the form $c x_0^{r_0} \ldots x_n^{r_n}$ where $r_0 + \ldots + r_n = d$.

Given any polynomial $f\in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ of degree $d$, the homogenisation of $f$ is $$ \begin{align*} f^\ast = x_0^d \,f\Bigl(\frac{x_1}{x_0}, \ldots, \frac{x_n}{x_0}\Bigr) \in k[x_0, \ldots, x_n], \end{align*} $$ which is a homogeneous polynomial of degree $d$.

Given a homogeneous polynomial $F\in k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$, its dehomogenisation is $$ \begin{align*} F_\ast = F(1, x_1, \ldots, x_n) \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]. \end{align*} $$

Any polynomial $f\in k[x_1,\ldots, x_n]$ can be written as $f = \sum_{d\geq 0} f_d$ where $f_d$ is homogeneous of degree $d$.

$f = (f^\ast)_\ast$ and $F = x_0^r \, (F_\ast)^\ast$ where $r$ is the largest power such that $x_0^r \mid F$.

$(f\cdot g)^\ast = f^\ast\cdot g^\ast$ and $(F\cdot G)_\ast = F_\ast \cdot G_\ast$.

Euler's identity: if $F\in k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ is homogeneous of degree $d$, then $d\cdot F = \sum_{i=0}^{n} x_i \pdfd{F}{x_i}$.

Definition (homogeneous ideal) open_in_new

An ideal $J\lhd k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ is homogeneous if $J$ is generated by homogeneous polynomials.

Given an ideal $I\lhd k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$, the homogenisation of $I$, denoted $I^\ast$, is the ideal in $k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ generated by the homogenisation of $f$ for all $f\in I$.

$J\lhd k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$ is homogeneous iff for all $f\in J$, we have $f_d\in J$ where $f = \sum_{d\geq 0} f_d$ is the decomposition into degree $d$-homogeneous polynomials. (Proof)

The zero locus in $\mathbb{P}^n$ is $$ \begin{align*} Z(S) := \sb{(a_0 : a_1 : \ldots : a_n)}{F(a_0, \ldots, a_n) = 0 \quad \forall F\in S}. \end{align*} $$ (We write $Z_a$ and $Z_p$ to distinguish the affine and projective cases when there is confusion.)

Definition (rational map) open_in_new

Let $X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n$ be an irreducible quasiprojective variety.

For any polynomials $F_0, F_1, \ldots, F_m \in k[x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ all homogeneous and having the same degree, if not all $F_i$ is in $I(X)$, then $$ \begin{align*} F: X \setminus Z(F_0, \ldots, F_m) &\to \mathbb{P}^m \\ \v x &\mapsto (F_0(\v x) : \ldots : F_m(\v x)) \end{align*} $$ is a well-defined function on an open dense subset of $X$.

Such two $F, G$ are said to determine the same rational map if they agree on an open dense subset. A rational map is an equivalence class of maps of this form, denoted $ϕ: X \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^m$.

$ϕ$ is regular at a point $P\in X$ if there is a representative $(F_0 : \ldots : F_m)$ of $ϕ$ such that $F_0(P), \ldots, F_m(P)$ are not all zero. The domain of $ϕ$ is the set of regular points.

If $Y \subseteq \mathbb{P}^m$ is a quasiprojective variety, we write $F: X \dashrightarrow Y$ is a rational map if $F$ takes values in $Y$ on its domain.

$(F_0 : \ldots : F_m)$ and $(G_0 : \ldots : G_m)$ determine the same rational map iff $F_i G_j - G_j F_i \in I(X)$ for all $i,j$. > In the case $X = \mathbb{P}^n$, this happens iff $F_i = h\cdot G_i$ for some $h\in k[x_0, \ldots, x_n]$.

A rational function $f/g\in k[X]$ on $X$ is the same as a rational map $(g : f): X\dashrightarrow \mathbb{A}^1 \subseteq \mathbb{P}^1$.

We can identify $\mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ with $\set{x_n = 0}$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$.

This is a well-defined morphism on any subvariety $X \subseteq \mathbb{P}^n$ with $P \not\in X$. If $Z(F)$ is a hypersurface, then if $R = (r_0 : \ldots : r_{n-1})\in \mathbb{P}^{n-1}$ then the preimage of $R$ in $Z(F)$ is given by the locus where $F(r_0 : \ldots : r_{n-1} : x_n) = 0$, which is a union of at most $\deg F$ many points in $Z(F)$ (using $P \not\in Z(F)$).

However it can be possible that its restriction to a subvariety containing $P$ is a morphism: consider $π: C := Z(x_0 x_2 - x_1^2) \dashrightarrow \mathbb{P}^1, (x_0 : x_1 : x_2) \mapsto (x_0 : x_1)$, a projection from $P = (0,0,1)$. The other representation is $(x_1 : x_2)$.

Definition (algebraic element, minimal polynomial, transcendental element, algebraic extension) open_in_new

Definition (divisor, principal divisor, linear equivalence) open_in_new

A divisor on a curve $V$ is a finite formal sum $D = \sum_{P\in V} n_P P$ with integer coefficients. We also write $ν_P(D) = n_P$. The set of divisors is the divisor group $\op{Div}(V) = \bigoplus_{P\in V} \Z \cdot P$.

The degree of a divisor $D = \sum n_P P$ is $$ \begin{align*} \deg(D) := \sum n_P. \end{align*} $$ The divisors of degree 0 are denoted by $\op{Div}^0(V)$, a subgroup of $\op{Div}(V)$.

The support of a divisor $D = \sum n_P P$ is the set $$ \begin{align*} \op{supp}(D) := \sb{P\in V}{n_p\neq 0}. \end{align*} $$

A divisor $D$ is effective (denoted $D \geq 0$) if $ν_P(D) \geq 0$ for all $P\in V$.

Given a non-zero rational function $f\in k(V)^\ast$, the divisor of $f$ is $$ \begin{align*} \op{div}(f) := \sum_{P\in V} ν_P(f)\cdot P \qquad \text{(i.e. $ν_P(\op{div}(f)) = ν_P(f)$)}, \end{align*} $$ where $ν_P$ is the valuation at $P$. Any such divisor is called a principal divisor. This is a finite sum and has degree 0.

The set of principal divisors is a subgroup of $\op{Div}^0(V)$. The quotient of $\op{Div}(V)$ is the divisor class group $\op{Cl}(V)$, and the quotient of $\op{Div}^0(V)$ is the degree 0 divisor class group $\op{Cl}^0(V)$. We say that $D, D'\in \op{Div}(V)$ are linearly equivalent if they are in the same divisor class, i.e. there exists $f\in k(V)^\times$ such that $D' - D = \op{div}(f)$.

Note $\mathrm{Div}(V) \to \Z: D\to \deg(D)$ is a group homomorphism, which descends to $\mathrm{Cl}(V)\to \Z$.

$\op{div}: k(V)^\times \to \op{Div}(V)$ is a group homomorphism, because so is $ν_P: k(V)^\times\to \Z$.

For $f,g\in k(V)^\times$, $\op{div}(f) = \op{div}(g)$ iff $f = cg$ for some $c\in k^\times$. (Derivation)

$V$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{P}^1$ iff $\op{Cl}^0(V)$ is trivial, in which case $\op{Cl}(V)\isom \Z$. (Proof)

Definition (valuation of differential) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a curve and $P\in V$.

For any local parameter $π_P$ at $P$, define the valuation of the differential $h \d π_P$ at $P$ as $ν_P(h \d π_P) :={}$$ν_P$$(h)$.

We say $ω\in Ω_V$ has a pole at $P$ if $ν_P(ω) < 0$ and a zero at $P$ if $ν_P(ω) > 0$.

$ν_P(ω)$ is well-defined. (Derivation)

For $g\in \mathfrak{m}_P$, $$ \begin{align*} ν_P(\d g) = ν_P(g) - 1. \end{align*} $$ (Proof)

The ultrametric inequality holds: $ν_P(ω + ω') \geq \min\{ν_P(ω), ν_P(ω')\}$ and equality holds when $ν_P(ω) \neq ν_P(ω')$.

$ν_P(fω) = ν_P(f) + ν_{P}(ω)$ for $f\in k(V)^\times$ and $ω\in Ω_V$.

For any $ω\in Ω_V$, $\sb{P\in V}{ν_P(ω) \neq 0}$ is finite. (Proof)

$ν_P(ω) \geq 0$ iff $ω$ is regular at $P$, because $Ω_{V,P} = O_{V,P} \d π_P$ and $ν_P|_{O_{V,P}} \geq 0$.

Definition (canonical divisor, genus of curve) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a curve and $P\in V$.

For a rational differential $ω\in Ω_{V}$, the divisor of $ω$ is $$ \begin{align*} \op{div}(ω) = \sum_{P\in V} ν_P(ω) P \qquad \text{(i.e. $ν_P(\op{div}(ω)) = ν_P(ω)$)} \end{align*} $$ where $ν_P(ω)$ is the valuation of $ω$ at $P$.

Such a divisor is called a canonical divisor (denoted $K_V$), and its divisor class is called the canonical divisor class.

The genus $g(V)$ of $V$ is $l(K_V)$ for any canonical divisor $K_V$.

$\op{div}(ω)$ and $\op{div}(ω')$ are linearly equivalent for any non-zero rational differentials $ω, ω'$, because $Ω_V$ is one-dimensional so $ω' = f ω$ for some $f\in k(V)^\times$.

Theorem (Riemann-Roch) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a curve.

For any divisor $D$ and any canonical divisor $K_V$, $$ \begin{align*} l(D) - l(K_V - D) = 1 - g + \deg(D). \end{align*} $$ ($g$, $l$, $\deg$)

Definition (morphism associated to divisor, very ample) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a curve and $D$ be a divisor. Suppose $n+1 :={}$$l(D)$${}\geq 2$ (i.e. $L(D) \not \subseteq k$), and let $B = \set{f_0, f_1, \ldots, f_n}$ be a basis for $L(D)$.

The morphism associated to $D$ with respect to $B$ is $$ \begin{align*} ϕ_D := (f_0 : f_1 : \ldots : f_n) : V \to \mathbb{P}^n. \end{align*} $$

If $ϕ_D: V\to ϕ_D(V)$ is an isomorphism, we say $ϕ_D$ is an embedding and $D$ is very ample.

Changing $B$ corresponds to a linear change of coordinates in $\mathbb{P}^n$.

Theorem (condition for very ample divisor) open_in_new

Therefore if $D\geq 2g+1$, then $D$ is very ample.
