Since $S^1$ is abelian, all its irreducible representation are one-dimensional. Therefore we aim to find all continuous group homomorphisms $ρ: S^1 \to \C^\times$.

Consider $|ρ|: S^1 \to (\R_{>0}, \times )$, which is a continuous group homomorphism. Since $S^1$ is compact, the image of $|ρ|$ is compact as well. The only compact subgroup of $(\R_{>0}, \times )$ is the trivial subgroup $\set{1}$, so $ρ: S^1 \to S^1$.

Let $$ψ: (\R, +)\to S^1: t \mapsto e^{2πit},$$ a covering map of $S^1$ which is also a group homomorphism. Then $ρ\circ ψ: \R\to S^1$ is still a continuous group homomorphism. We can lift this to a continuous function $f: \R\to \R$ such that $$ψ \circ f = ρ \circ ψ$$ and $f(0) = 0$, and we claim this is a group homomorphism. Consider the map $Δ: \R\times \R\to \R: (t,s) \mapsto f(t) + f(s) - f(t+s)$, which is continuous. Since $ψ \circ f$ is a group homomorphism, $ψ\circ Δ$ is constant identity, so $Δ: \R \times \R \to \Z$. By connectedness, $Δ$ is constant, and plugging in $(0,0)$ shows $Δ \equiv 0$.

We claim any continuous group homomorphism $f: \R\to \R$ must be $x\mapsto cx$ for some $c\in \R$. Let $c = f(1)$. Then for $n\in \N$, $f(n) = f(1) + \ldots + f(1) = nc$ and $f(-n) = -f(n) = -nc$. Moreover for $a,b\in \Z$, $b\neq 0$, we have $f(a/b) + \ldots + f(a/b) = f(a) = na$, so $f(a/b) = na/b$. Thus $f(q) = cq$ for all $q\in \Q$, so by continuity and density of $\Q$, $f(x) = cx$ for all $x\in \R$.

This then imply $$ \begin{align*} ρ(e^{2πit}) = e^{2πict}, \qquad t\in \R. \end{align*} $$ But $e^{2πi(1)} = e^{2πi(0)}$, so we have $c\in \Z$.

The last statement follows from complete reducibility of representations.
