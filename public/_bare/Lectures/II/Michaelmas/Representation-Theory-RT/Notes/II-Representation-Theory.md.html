Lemma (idempotent endomorphism of finite-dimensional complex vector space is diagonalisable) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional complex vector space.

If $α:V\to V$ is idempotent, i.e. $α^m = \id$ for some $m\geq 1$, then $α$ is diagonalisable.

Proof

Definition (representation) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over a field $k$.

A representation of a group $G$ on $V$ is a group homomorphism $ρ: G \to{}$$\GL$$(V)$, the group of automorphisms (invertible endomorphisms) on $V$.

The dimension or degree of $ρ$ is $\dim_k V$.

$ρ$ is faithful if the kernel is trivial, i.e. $ρ$ is injective.

Equivalently, a representation is an action $G\actson V$ which is $k$-linear, i.e. $g(λu + μv) = λgu + μgv$ for all $λ,μ\in k$, $u,v\in V$. (So we write $g$ for $ρ(g)$.)

Choosing a basis for $V$, a representation is equivalent to a matrix representation $G\to \GL_n(k)$.

We say $V$ is a $G$-module (actually it is a module over $k[G]$, the ring of formal linear combinations of $G$).

The trivial representation is the linear representation $\mathbb{1}: G\to \GL(k) \isom k^\times: g\mapsto 1$.

If $G$ is finite, then $ρ(g)$ has finite order so is diagonalisable.

If $ρ$ is a complex representation, then so is its conjugate $\bar ρ$, its determinant $\det ρ$, and $ερ$ for any linear representation $ε$.

Definition (G-homomorphism) open_in_new

Let $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ and $ρ': G\to \GL(V')$ of $G$ be two representations.

A $G$-homomorphism, a $G$-linear map or an intertwiner is a linear map $ϕ: V\to V'$ satisfying $$ \begin{align*} ϕ(ρ(g) v) = ρ'(g) ϕ(v) \qquad (\text{equivalently } ϕgv = gϕv) \end{align*} $$ for all $g\in G, v\in V$.

We write $\Hom_G(V, V')$ for the $k$-vector space of all $G$-homomorphisms.

A $G$-isomorphism is a $G$-homomorphism that is bijective. If such a map exists, we say $ρ$ and $ρ'$ are conjugate, similar or isomorphic.

$k\actson V$ by scalar multiplication, so $\Hom_k(V,V')$ is the $k$-vector space of all $k$-linear maps $V\to V'$.

If $ϕ:V\to V'$ is $G$-linear, then $\ker ϕ\leq V$ and $\im ϕ\leq V'$ are $G$-invariant subspaces.

Definition (subrepresentation, irreducible) open_in_new

Let $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ be a representation.

We say a vector subspace $W$${}\leq{}$$V$ is a subrepresentation of $V$ or a $G$-invariant subspace if $ρ(g)w \in W$ for all $g\in G, w\in W$.

$\set{0}$ and $W$ are always $G$-invariant subspaces. Call the other ones proper.

$ρ$ is irreducible or simple if it has no proper $G$-invariant subspace.

This induces a representation $G \to \GL(V/W)$.

In matrix representation, $W$ is a $G$-invariant subspace iff in a basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_m, v_{m+1}, \ldots, v_n}$ of $V$ that contains a basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_m}$ of $W$, the matrix of $ρ(g)$ for any $g\in G$ has the form $\pmat{A & * \\ 0 & B}$, where $A$ is the matrix for $W$ and $B$ is the matrix for $V/W$.

Theorem (Schur's lemma) open_in_new

Let $V$ and $W$ be representations of $G$ over $k$.

Suppose $V$ and $W$ are irreducible and $ϕ: V\to W$ is $G$-linear.

  1. $ϕ$ is either zero or an isomorphism.

  2. If $W = V$ is finite-dimensional and $k$ is algebraically closed, then $ϕ = λ\,\id_V$ for some $λ\in k$.

Proof

If $k$ is algebraically closed, then $$ \begin{align*} \dim \Hom_G(V, W) = \begin{cases} 1, &\text{if $V,W$ are $G$-isomorphic}, \\ 0, &\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ (Proof)

If $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ is irreducible and $z\in{}$$Z(G)$, then $ρ(z)$ is $G$-linear (because $ρ(z)ρ(g) = ρ(g)ρ(z)$) so $ρ(z) = λ\, \id_V$ for some $λ\in k$.

If $G$ is abelian, then any irreducible representation of $G$ is one-dimensional. (Proof)

Definition (direct sum of representations) open_in_new

Let $ρ_U: G\to \GL(U), ρ_W: G\to \GL(W)$ be subrepresentations of $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$.

Say $ρ$ is an (internal) direct sum of $ρ_U$ and $ρ_W$, written $ρ = ρ_U \oplus ρ_W$, if $V = U$${}\oplus{}$$W$, i.e. $V = U + W$ and $U \cap W = \set{0}$. In this case we say $ρ$ is decomposable.

In matrix representation, $ρ = ρ_U \oplus ρ_W$ iff in a basis $\set{u_1, \ldots, u_k, w_1, \ldots, w_l}$ for $V$ containing a basis $\set{u_1, \ldots, u_k}$ for $U$ and a basis $\set{w_1, \ldots, w_l}$ for $W$, the matrix of $ρ(g)$ for any $g\in G$ has the form $\pmat{A & 0 \\ 0 & B}$, where $A$ is the matrix for $U$ and $B$ is the matrix for $W$.

Theorem (Weyl's unitary trick) open_in_new

Definition (class function) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a group.

A function $f: G\to \C$ is a class function if it is constant on conjugacy classes of $G$, i.e. $f(hgh\inv) = f(g)$ for all $g,h\in G$.

Write $\mathcal{C}_G$ for the complex vector space of all class functions.

If $G$ is finite, equip $\mathcal{C}_G$ with the inner product $$ \begin{align*} \langle f, f'\rangle = \frac{1}{\# G} \sum_{g\in G} \overline{f(g)}\, f'(g) = \sum_{j=1}^{k} \frac{1}{\# Z_G(g_j)} \overline{f(g_j)} \,f'(g_j). \end{align*} $$ where $\prescript{G}{}g_1, \ldots, \prescript{G}{}g_k$ are all the conjugacy classes of $G$ and $Z_G(g_j)$ is the centraliser of $g_j$.

If $G$ is finite, then we have a basis $\set{δ_1, \ldots, δ_k}$ for $\mathcal{C}_G$ where $δ_j(g) = 1$ if $g\in \prescript{G}{}g_j$ and $0$ otherwise.

Definition (character of representation) open_in_new

Its character is the function $$ \begin{align*} χ_ρ: G&\to \C \\ χ_ρ(g) &= \tr ρ(g). \end{align*} $$

The trivial character $\mathbb{1}: g \mapsto 1$ is the character of the trivial representation.

$χ_ρ(1) = \tr\id_V = \dim V$.

$χ_ρ(g\inv) = \overline{χ_ρ(g)}$ if $G$ is finite. (Proof)

$χ_{ρ\oplus ρ'} = χ_ρ + χ_{ρ'}$.

A linear representation is a character.

    (Proof)

Definition (permutation representation) open_in_new

Suppose a finite group $G$ acts on a finite set $X$. Let $k[X] = \sb{\sum_{x\in X} λ_x e_x}{λ_x\in k}$ be the $\# X$-dimensional $k$-vector space of formal linear combinations of elements in $X$.

The corresponding permutation representation is $ρ_X: G\to \GL(k[X])$ defined by $$ \begin{align*} ρ_X(g)(e_x) = e_{gx} \end{align*} $$ and extending linearly (so $ρ_X(g)(\sum_{x\in X} λ_x e_x) = \sum_{x\in X} λ_x e_{gx}$).

The regular representation $ρ_\text{reg}$ is the permutation representation corresponding to the regular action $G\actson G$ (by left multiplication).

Lemma (character of permutation representation) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group acting on finite sets $X,Y$, and let $ρ_X, ρ_Y$ be the corresponding complex permutation representation.

  1. $π_X(g) :={}$$χ_{ρ_X}$$(g) = \#X^g$ where $X^g = \sb{x\in X}{gx = x}$ is the set of fixed points of $g$. (Proof)

  2. $\langle π_X, \mathbb{1}\rangle = \#G\backslash X$ where $G\backslash X = \sb{Gx}{x\in X}$ is the set of orbits. (Proof)

  3. $\langle π_X, π_Y\rangle = \#G\backslash (X \times Y)$ where $G$ acts on $X\times Y$ by $g(x,y) = (gx,gy)$. (Proof)

  4. $π_X = \mathbb{1} + χ$ where $χ$ is irreducible iff the action $G\actson X$ is 2-transitive, i.e. $G\actson X\times X$ has exactly two orbits $Δ = \sb{(x,x)}{x\in X}$ and $(X\times X) \setminus Δ$. (Proof)

Proposition (decomposition of regular representation) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group, and let $ρ_\text{reg}$ be its regular representation over $k$.

  1. If $\op{char} k = 0$ or $\op{char} k \not\mid \#G$, then any irreducible representation is isomorphic to a subrepresentation of $ρ_\text{reg}$. (Proof)

  2. If $k = \C$ and $χ_1, \ldots, χ_k$ are all the irreducible characters, $ρ_\text{reg}$ has a decomposition $$ \begin{align*} π_\text{reg} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\deg χ_i) χ_i. \end{align*} $$ (Proof)

This shows $\# G = \deg π_\text{reg} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (\deg χ_i)^2$.

Definition (dual representation) open_in_new

Let $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ be a representation over $k$.

Its dual representation is $ρ^\ast: G\to \GL(V^\ast)$ defined by $$ \begin{align*} ρ^\ast(g)(θ)(v) = θ(ρ(g\inv)(v)) \end{align*} $$ where $V^\ast = \Hom_k(V, k)$ is the dual space.

$ρ$ is self-dual if $ρ$ and $ρ^\ast$ are isomorphic.

Check $ρ^\ast$ is a group homomorphism.

For $k = \C$, its character is $χ_{ρ^\ast}(g) = χ_ρ(g\inv)$, which equals $\overline{χ_ρ(g)}$ if $G$ is finite. (Proof)

Therefore $ρ$ is self-dual iff $χ_ρ$ is real.

Definition (tensor product of vector spaces) open_in_new

Let $V, W$ be finite-dimensional vector spaces, with bases $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_m}$ and $\set{w_1, \ldots, w_m}$ respectively.

The tensor product $V\oplus W$ of $V$ and $W$ is the $mn$-dimensional vector space with bases labelled $\sb{v_i \otimes w_j}{i=1,\ldots, m,\; j=1,\ldots, m}$.

Define a bilinear map $\otimes: V \times W \to V \otimes W$ by extending $(v_i, w_j) \mapsto v_i \otimes w_j$ linearly, i.e. $$ \begin{align*} \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{m} λ_i v_i\Bigr) \otimes \Bigl(\sum_{j=1}^{n} μ_j w_j\Bigr) = \sum_{i,j} λ_i μ_j (v_i \otimes w_j). \end{align*} $$

Alternatively define via a universal property: the tensor product $V \otimes W$ of $V$ and $W$ is a vector space, together with a bilinear map $ϕ: V\times W\to V \otimes W$ such that for any bilinear map $h: V \times W\to U$, there exists a unique linear map $\tilde h: V \otimes W\to U$ such that $h = \tilde h \circ ϕ$.

Definition (tensor product of linear maps) open_in_new

Let $f: V \to W$, $f': V'\to W'$ be linear maps between finite-dimensional vector spaces.

The tensor product of $f$ and $f'$ is the linear map $f \otimes f': V$${}\otimes{}$$V' \to W \otimes W'$ defined by $$ \begin{align*} (f\otimes f')(v \otimes w) = f(v) \otimes f'(w) \end{align*} $$ and extending linearly.

If $A$ is the matrix of $f$ in the basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_m}$ for $V$ and $B$ is the matrix of $f'$ in the basis $\set{w_1, \ldots, w_n}$ for $W$, then in the basis $\set{v_1 \otimes w_1, \ldots, v_1 \otimes w_n, v_2 \otimes w_1, \ldots, v_2 \otimes w_n, \ldots, v_m \otimes w_1, \ldots, v_m \otimes w_n}$ of $V \otimes W$, $f \otimes f'$ has a matrix $\pmat{a_{11} B & a_{12} B & \ldots \\$ a{21} B & a{22} B & \ldots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots}$.$> Therefore $\tr(f \otimes f') = \tr f \cdot \tr f'$.

For two representations $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ and $ρ': G\to \GL(V')$, their tensor product $ρ \otimes ρ': g \mapsto ρ(g) \otimes ρ'(g)$ affords a character $χ_{ρ \otimes ρ'} = χ_{ρ} χ_{ρ'}$.

Definition (tensor powers, symmetric power, exterior power) open_in_new

Let $T^n V \equiv V^{\otimes n} = \underbrace{V \otimes V \otimes \ldots \otimes V}_{n\text{ times}}$ be the $n$-th tensor power.

For any representation $G\to \GL(V)$, there is a natural representation $T^n V$ of $G \times S_n$, defined by $$ \begin{align*} (g, σ)(v_1 \otimes v_2 \otimes \ldots \otimes v_n) = (gv_{σ(1)}) \otimes (gv_{σ(2)}) \otimes \ldots \otimes (gv_{σ(n)}). \end{align*} $$

Define the symmetric power as the isotypical component of the trivial representation of $S_n$: $$ \begin{align*} S^nV = \sb{v\in V^{\otimes n}}{σv = v \text{ for all }σ\in S_n} \end{align*} $$ and the exterior power as the isotypical component of the sign representation of $S_n$: $$ \begin{align*} Λ^nV = \sb{v\in V^{\otimes n}}{σv = \sign(σ)\, v \text{ for all }σ\in S_n} \end{align*} $$

$S^2V$ is called the symmetric square and $Λ^2V$ is called the exterior square. We write $vw = v\otimes w + w \otimes v\in S^2V$ and $v\wedge w = v \otimes w - w \otimes v$.

If $V$ is $d$-dimensional and has a basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_d}$, then

  • $S^n V$ is $\pmat{n+d-1 \\ n}$-dimensional and has a basis $\sb{\sum_{σ\in S_n} v_{i_{σ(1)}} \otimes v_{i_{σ(2)}} \otimes \ldots \otimes v_{i_{σ(n)}}}{1 \leq i_1 \leq i_2 \leq \ldots \leq i_n \leq d}$, and

  • $Λ^n V$ is $\pmat{d \\ n}$-dimensional and has a basis $\sb{\sum_{σ\in S_n} \sign(σ) \,v_{i_{σ(1)}} \otimes v_{i_{σ(2)}} \otimes \ldots \otimes v_{i_{σ(n)}}}{1 \leq i_1 < i_2 < \ldots < i_n \leq d}$.

For $n=2$, $V \otimes V = S^2 V \oplus Λ^2 V$, because $x = \frac{1}{2}\underbrace{(x + τx)}_{\in S^2 V} + \frac{1}{2}\underbrace{(x - τx)}_{\in Λ^2V}$ where $τ = (1\,2)\in S_2$.

Definition (tensor algebra) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a $k$-vector space, and write $T^n V = \underbrace{V \oplus V \oplus \ldots \oplus V}_{n\text{ times}}$ be the $n$-th tensor power (with $T^0 V = k$).

The tensor algebra of $V$ is $$ \begin{align*} T(V) = \bigoplus_{n\geq 0} T^n V, \end{align*} $$ which is a $k$-algebra with multiplication $(x,y) \mapsto x\otimes y$.

The symmetric algebra is $S(V) = T(V) / (\text{ideal generated by }u\otimes v - v \otimes u)$, and the exterior algebra is $Λ(V) = T(V) / (\text{ideal generated by }v\otimes v)$.

Definition (restriction of representation) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a group and $H\leq G$ be a subgroup.

The restriction of a representation $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ to $H$ is $\op{Res}^G_H = ρ|_H: H \to \GL(V)$.

Definition (induction of representation) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a group and $H\leq G$ be a subgroup.

The induction of a representation $ρ: H\to \GL(V)$ is $$ \begin{align*} \op{Ind}^G_H ρ: G &\to \GL(\Hom_H(\C[G], V)) \\ g &\mapsto (θ \mapsto (x \mapsto θ(xg))). \end{align*} $$ where $\Hom_H(\C[G], V) = \sb{f: \C[G] \to V \text{ linear}}{f(hx) = ρ(h) f(x)\; \forall h\in H, x\in \C[G]}$.

$\Hom_H(\C[G], V)$ is isomorphic to $\sb{f: G\to V}{f(hg) = ρ(h)f(g)\; \forall h\in H, g\in G}$.

Definition (double coset) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a group and $H,K\leq G$ be subgroups.

A double coset of $H$ and $K$ is a set of the form $HwK = \sb{hwk}{h\in H, k\in K}$.

$H\backslash G/K = \sb{HwK}{w\in G}$ denotes the set of all double cosets of $H$ and $K$.

The double cosets partition $G$.

A double coset is an orbit in any of the following action:

  • $H \actson G/K$ by $h*gK = hgK$;

  • $K \actson H\backslash G$ by $k*Hg = Hgk\inv$;

  • $H \times K\actson G$ by $(h,k)*g = hgk\inv$.

  • $G\actson G/H \times G/K$ by $g*(g_1 H, g_2 K) = (g g_1 H, g g_2 K)$.

Orbit-stabiliser implies $$ \begin{align*} \#HwK = \frac{\# H \,\# K}{\#(H \cap wKw\inv)} = \frac{\#H \, \#K}{\#(K \cap w\inv H w)}. \end{align*} $$

Theorem (Mackey's restriction formula) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a group, $H,K\leq G$ be subgroups, and $V$ be a representation of $H$. Then $$ \begin{align*} \op{Res}^G_K \op{Ind}^G_H V = \bigoplus_{HwK \in H\backslash G/K} \op{Ind}^K_{K \cap w\inv Hw} \prescript{w\inv}{}{\op{Res}^H_{H \cap wKw\inv}} V. \end{align*} $$

Here $H\backslash G/K$ is the set of double cosets, and $\op{Res}$ and $\op{Ind}$ denote the restriction and induction of representations.

$\prescript{x}{}ρ$ denotes the twisted representation: if $ρ: S\to \GL(W)$, $S\leq T$ and $x\in T$, then $\prescript{x}{}ρ: xSx\inv \to \GL(W)$ is defined by $\prescript{x}{}ρ(y) = ρ(x\inv yx)$.

Proof

Together with Frobenius reciprocity, this implies $$ \begin{align*} \langle \op{Ind}^G_H V, \op{Ind}^G_H V\rangle ={}& \langle V, \op{Res}^G_H \op{Ind}^G_H V\rangle \\ ={}& \sum_{HwH\in H\backslash G/H} \langle V, \op{Ind}^H_{H \cap w\inv Hw} \prescript{w\inv}{}{\op{Res}^H_{H \cap wHw\inv}} V\rangle \\ ={}& \sum_{HwH\in H\backslash G/H} \langle \op{Res}^H_{H \cap w\inv H w} V, \prescript{w\inv}{}{\op{Res}^H_{H \cap w H w\inv}}V\rangle \\ ={}& \langle V, V\rangle + \sum_{\substack{HwH\in H\backslash G/H: \\w\not\in H}} \langle \op{Res}^H_{H \cap w\inv H w}, \prescript{w\inv}{}{\op{Res}^H_{H \cap wHw\inv} V}\rangle. \end{align*} $$

Definition (Frobenius group) open_in_new

A finite group $G$ is a Frobenius group if there exists a non-trivial subgroup $1\neq H\leq G$ (called the Frobenius complement) such that $H \cap gHg\inv = \set{1}$ for all $g\in G \setminus H$.

The Frobenius kernel is $$ \begin{align*} K := \set{1} \cup \sb{g\in G}{\prescript{G}{}g \cap H = \emptyset} = \set{1} \cup \sb{g\in G}{gg'H \neq g'H\; \forall g'\in G}. \end{align*} $$

$gHg\inv$ and $g'Hg'\inv$ equals if $g' \in gH$, or intersects trivially otherwise. (Proof)

Under the left multiplication action $G \actson G/H$, each $g\in G\setminus \set{1}$ has at most one fixed point. (Proof)

Equivalently, $G$ is a Frobenius group iff $G$ acts transitively on a finite set $X$ and each $g\in G\setminus \set{1}$ has at most one fixed point: take $H = \op{Stab}_G(x)$ for any $x\in X$ and apply orbit-stabiliser.

If $h\in H \setminus \set{1}$, then $H \cap \prescript{G}{}h = \prescript{H}{}h$ and $Z_G(h) = Z_H(h)$. (Proof)

If $k\in K\setminus \set{1}$, then $\prescript{G}{}k \subseteq K$ and $Z_G(k) \subseteq K$. (Proof)

Theorem (Frobenius) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a Frobenius group. The Frobenius kernel $K$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.

Algebraic integers and character theory open_in_new

Definition (algebraic integer) open_in_new

A complex number $α\in \C$ is called an algebraic integer if it is the root of a monic polynomial in $\Z[X]$.

We write $$ \begin{align*} \Z[α] = \bigcap_{\substack{R\leq \C \\ α\in R}} R, \end{align*} $$ or equivalently, $$ \begin{align*} \Z[α] ={}& \im(e_α), & e_α : \Z[X] &\to \C \\ && f &\mapsto f(α). \end{align*} $$

Proposition (algebraic integer iff eigenvalue of integer matrix) open_in_new

Let $α\in \C$. $α$ is an algebraic integer iff it is an eigenvalue of some square matrix, all of whose entries are integers.

Proof

Theorem (algebraic integers form a ring) open_in_new

The algebraic integers form a subring of $\C$. That is, if $λ,μ$ are algebraic integers, so are $λ+μ$ and $λμ$.

Proof

Proposition (rational algebraic integer is an integer) open_in_new

Let $α\in \C$ be an algebraic integer.

If $α\in \Q$, then $α\in \Z$.

Proposition (subring of C finitely generated as Z-module comprises algebraic integers) open_in_new

Any subring of $\C$ which is finitely generated as a $\Z$-module contains only algebraic integers.

Proposition (character values are algebraic integers) open_in_new

If $χ$ is a character of a finite group $G$, then $χ(g)$ is an algebraic integer for all $g\in G$.

Definition (group algebra) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group.

The group algebra of $G$ is the $\C$-algebra $$ \begin{align*} \C[G] ={}& \sb{\sum_{g\in G} α_g e_g}{α_g \in \C}, \end{align*} $$ of formal $\C$-linear combinations of $G$ (with multiplication rule $e_g e_h = e_{gh}$).

Its centre is $Z(\C[G]) := \sb{x\in \C[G]}{xy = yx\; \forall y\in \C[G]}$, which is a subalgebra.

Lemma (class sums form basis for centre of group algebra) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group. Let $\mathcal{C}_1, \ldots, \mathcal{C}_k$ be all the conjugacy classes of $G$, and define the class sum $C_i = \sum_{g\in \mathcal{C}_i} e_g\in{}$$\C[G]$.

$\set{C_1, \ldots, C_k}$ is a basis for the centre $Z(\C[G])$.

Moreover, $C_i C_j = \sum_{l=1}^{k} a_{ijl} C_k$ for some $a_{ijl}\in \Z_{\geq 0}$ called the structure constants for $\Z(\C[G])$.

Proof

Lemma (central character of group algebra) open_in_new

Let $ρ: G\to \GL(V)$ be a complex representation of a finite group $G$, and extend linearly to $ρ:{}$$\C[G]$${}\to \op{End}(V)$ a representation of algebras.

If $ρ$ is irreducible, then there exists an algebra homomorphism $ω_V: Z(\C[G]) \to \C$ such that $$ \begin{align*} ρ(z) = ω_V(z)\,\id_V \qquad \forall z\in Z(\C[G]). \end{align*} $$

If furthermore $\mathcal{C} = \prescript{G}{}g$ is the conjugacy class of $g\in G$ and $C = \sum_{x\in \mathcal{C}} e_x$ is its class sum, then $$ \begin{align*} ω_V(C) = \frac{\#\mathcal{C}\cdot χ_ρ(g)}{\deg ρ} \end{align*} $$ and is an algebraic integer.

Proof

Theorem (degree of irreducible representation divides group order) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group.

If $ρ$ is an irreducible complex representation of $G$, then $\deg ρ \mid \#G/\#$$Z(G)$.

Proof

Proposition (irreducible character at conjugacy class of size coprime to degree) open_in_new

Let $χ$ be a character of $G$ and $\mathcal{C} = \prescript{G}{}g$ is the conjugacy class of $g\in G$.

If $χ$ is irreducible and $\# \mathcal{C}$ and $\deg χ$ are coprime, then $χ(g) = 0$ or $|χ(g)| = \deg χ$.

Proof

Theorem (finite group with conjugacy class of prime power size is not non-abelian simple) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a finite group. If $G$ has a conjugacy class which is not $\set{1}$ and whose size is a prime power, then $G$ cannot be a non-abelian simple group.

Representation of compact groups open_in_new

Definition (topological group) open_in_new

A topological group is a topological space which is also a group, such that the group operation $\cdot: G\times G\to G$ and the inverse $\inv: G\to G$ are continuous.

Definition (representation of topological group) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a finite-dimensional vector space over $\C$.

A representation of a topological group $G$ on $V$ is a continuous group homomorphism $ρ: G \to{}$$\GL$$(V)$ (where $\GL(V)$ has topology inherited from $\op{End}(V) \isom \C^{n\times n}$).

Theorem (Haar measure) open_in_new

Let $G$ be a compact topological group.

There exists a unique Borel measure $μ$ on $G$ satisfying

  1. translation invariance: $μ(S) = μ(gS)$ for all $S\subseteq G$ Borel, $g\in G$;

  2. $\int_G μ(\d g) = 1$.

Let $G,H$ be compact topological groups. Replacing $\frac{1}{\# G}\sum_{g\in G}$ with the Harr measure $\int \d g$, we prove the following results analogously:

  • Schur's lemma.

  • For irreducible representations $ρ, ρ'$, $\dim \op{Hom}_\C(ρ, ρ') = 1$ if $ρ$ and $ρ'$ are isomorphic and 0 otherwise.

  • Complete reducibility: every (finite-dimensional) representation of $G$ is a direct sum of irreducible representations.

  • Characters are now continuous class functions $G \to \C$.

  • $\langle χ_ρ, χ_ρ\rangle = 1$ iff $ρ$ is irreducible.

  • Every irreducible representation of an abelian group is one-dimensional.

  • All irreducible representations of $G\times H$ are of the form $V \otimes W$, where $V$, $W$ are irreducible representations of $G$, $H$ respectively.

Theorem (representation of S1) open_in_new

The irreducible representations of the compact topological group $S^1 ={}$$U$$(1)$ are $$ \begin{align*} ρ_n: S^1&\to \GL(\C) = \C^\times, &&n\in \Z \\ z &\mapsto z^n. \end{align*} $$ Therefore any character of a (finite-dimensional) representation of $S^1$ is identified with a Laurent polynomial with non-negative integer coefficients $\sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n\in \Z_{\geq 0}[z,z\inv]$ (where $a_n\in \Z_{\geq 0}$).

Theorem (conjugacy of SU(2)) open_in_new

The special unitary group of degree 2, $\op{SU}(2)$, is the group of unitary $2\times 2$ complex matrices of determinant 1.

  1. $\op{SU}(2) = \sb{\pmat{a & b \\ -\bar b & \bar a}}{a,b\in \C, \; |a|^2 + |b|^2 = 1}$, which is homeomorphic to $S^3$. (Proof)

  2. $Z$$(\op{SU}(2)) = \set{\pm I}$. (Proof)

Define the maximal torus $T = \sb{\pmat{a \\&a\inv}}{|a|^2 = 1}\leq \op{SU}(2)$, which is homeomorphic to $S^1$, and $s = \pmat{&1 \\ -1}$.

  1. $s\pmat{a \\ &a\inv}s\inv = \pmat{a\inv \\ &a}$ and $N$$(T) = T \cup sT = \set{\pmat{a \\ &a\inv}, \pmat{& b\\ -b\inv}}$. (Proof)

  2. There is a well-defined bijection $$ \begin{align*} \set{\text{conjugacy classes of $\op{SU}(2)$}} &\leftrightarrow [-1, 1] \\ \prescript{\op{SU}(2)}{}g &\mapsto \frac{1}{2}\tr(g). \end{align*} $$ In particular, every conjugacy class intersects $T$ at $\set{x, x\inv}$. (Proof)

  3. $Z_{\op{SU}(2)}$$(\pmat{λ \\ &λ\inv}) = T$ for $λ\neq \pm 1$. (Proof)

  4. Each conjugacy class, except $\set{I}$ and $\set{-I}$, is homeomorphic to $S^2$. (Proof)

Theorem (representation of SU(2)) open_in_new

Let $V_n$ be the $(n+1)$-dimensional complex vector space of degree $n$ homogeneous polynomials in two variables $x,y$: $$ \begin{align*} V_n = \sb{r_0 x^n + r_1 x^{n-1} y + \ldots + r_{n-1} x y^{n-1} + r_n y^n}{r_i\in \C}, \end{align*} $$ and define a representation of $\op{GL}_2(\C)$ on $V_n$ by $$ \begin{align*} \Bigl(\pmat{a & b \\ c & d} * f\Bigr)(x,y) := f\Bigl((x,y)\pmat{a & b \\ c & d}\Bigr) \equiv f(ax + cy, bx + dy). \end{align*} $$

  1. The restriction of a representation $ρ$ of $\op{SU}(2)$ to $T$ determines $ρ$ uniquely (up to isomorphism). Proof

  2. The character of a representation $ρ$ of $\op{SU}(2)$ correspond to an even Laurent polynomial of non-negative integer coefficients: $$ \begin{align*} χ_ρ(z) \equiv χ_ρ\left(\pmat{z \\ &z\inv}\right) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n z^n, \qquad a_n\in \Z_{\geq 0}, \; a_n = a_{-n}. \end{align*} $$ (Proof)

  3. The character of $V_n$ is $$ \begin{align*} χ_{V_n}(z) = z^n + z^{n-2} + \ldots + z^{2-n} + z^{-n} = \frac{z^{n+1} - z^{-(n+1)}}{z - z\inv}. \end{align*} $$ (Proof)

  4. $V_0, V_1, V_2, \ldots$ are all the irreducible representations of $\op{SU}(2)$. (Proof)

Lemma (Clebsch-Gordan rule for SU(2)) open_in_new

Let $V_n$ be the unique irreducible representation of $\op{SU}(2)$ of dimension $(n+1)$. Then $$ \begin{align*} V_n \otimes V_m \isom V_{n+m} \oplus V_{n+m-2} \oplus \ldots \oplus V_{|n-m|} \end{align*} $$ where $\otimes$ is the tensor product. This is known as the Clebsch-Gordan rule.
