Since $V$ is finite-dimensional, pick any inner product $(,)$ on $V$ by choosing a basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_n}$ for $V$, setting $(v_i, v_j) = δ_{ij}$ and extending linearly.

Define an another inner product $\langle ,\rangle$ on $V$ by $$ \begin{align*} \langle v, w\rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g\in G} (gv, gw). \end{align*} $$ Check this is a $G$-invariant inner product:

  • $\langle v, v\rangle = |G|\inv \sum_{g\in G} (gv,gv) \geq 0$ with equality iff $gv = 0$ for all $g\in G$, iff $v = 0$ by taking $g = 1$.

  • $(,)$ is sesquilinear and Hermitian, and thus so is $\langle ,\rangle$.

  • For any $h\in G$, changing variable to $g' = gh$ yields $$ \begin{align*} \langle hv, hw\rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g\in G} (ghv, ghw) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g'\in G} (g'v,g'w) = \langle v,w\rangle \end{align*} $$ so $\langle ,\rangle$ is $G$-invariant.

For the second claim, suppose $U(V)$ is the unitary group with respect to the inner product $(,)$ with an orthonormal basis $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_n}$ for $V$. Let $\set{w_1, \ldots, w_n}$ be an orthonormal basis for $V$ with respect to $\langle ,\rangle$, and take $X$ to be the change of basis matrix from $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_n}$ to $\set{w_1, \ldots, w_n}$. This is so that $\langle v,w\rangle = (Xv, Xw)$.

Then $(,)$ is invariant under $X\, ρ(G)\, X\inv$: for any $g\in G$, $$ \begin{align*} (XgX\inv v, XgX\inv w) = \langle gX \inv v, gX\inv w\rangle = \langle X\inv v, X\inv w\rangle = (v, w) \end{align*} $$ so $X\, ρ(G)\, X\inv \leq U(V)$.
