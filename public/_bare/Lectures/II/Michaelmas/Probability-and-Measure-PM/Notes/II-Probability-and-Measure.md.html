Definition (sigma-algebra, measurable space) open_in_new

Let $E$ be a set.

A set $\mathcal A\subseteq\mathcal P(E)$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra or tribe on $E$ if it satisfies

  • $E \in \mathcal A$;

  • stability by complementation: $A^\complement\in\mathcal F$ for all $A\in\mathcal F$; and

  • stability by countable unions: if $(A_n)_{n\in\N}$ is a countable sequence in $\mathcal A$, then $\bigcup_{n\in\N} A_n \in \mathcal A$.

Equivalently:

  • $\mathcal F$ is non-empty;

  • $\mathcal F$ is closed under countable set operations (complement, union, intersections). (Derivation of closure under countable intersections)

Elements of $\mathcal{A}$ are called measurable sets, and $(E,\mathcal{A})$ is called a measurable space.

  • Every set $E$ admits the full tribe $\mathcal{P}(E)$ and the trivial tribe $\set{\emptyset, E}$.

  • If $(\mathcal{A}_i)_{i\in I}$ is a collection of $σ$-algebras on $E$, then $\bigcap_{i\in I} \mathcal{A}_i$ is also a $σ$-algebra on $E$.

Definition (sigma-algebra generated by set) open_in_new

Let $E$ be a set.

For any subset $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(E)$, the $σ$-algebra generated by $C$ is the smallest $σ$-algebra on $E$ containing $\mathcal{C}$: $$ \begin{align*} σ(\mathcal{C}) = \bigcup_{\substack{\mathcal{A} \text{ $σ$-algebra}\\ \mathcal{C}\subseteq \mathcal{A}}} \mathcal{A}. \end{align*} $$

Definition (Borel sigma-algebra) open_in_new

Let $(E, \mathcal{O})$ be a topological space.

The Borel $σ$-algebra of $E$ is $\mathcal{B}(E) = σ(\mathcal{O})$ the $σ$-algebra generated by $\mathcal{O}$.

Definition (product sigma-algebra) open_in_new

Let $(E_1, \mathcal{A}_1)$ and $(E_2, \mathcal{A}_2)$ be measurable spaces.

The product $σ$-algebra $\mathcal{A}_1 \otimes \mathcal{A}_2$ on $E_1 \times E_2$ is $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{A}_1 \otimes \mathcal{A}_2 = σ\{ A_1 \times A_2 \ |\ A_1 \in \mathcal{A}_1, A_2 \in \mathcal{A}_2\}. \end{align*} $$

Definition (measure space) open_in_new

Let $(E, \mathcal{A})$ be a measurable space.

A (positive) measure on $E$ is a map $μ: \mathcal{A}\to [0,\infty]$ such that

  1. $μ(\emptyset) = 0$; and

  2. $σ$-additivity: for any countable family $(A_n)_{n\in \N}$ in $\mathcal{A}$ that is pairwise disjoint, $$ \begin{align*} μ\left(\bigsqcup_{n\in \N} A_n\right) = \sum_{n\in \N} μ(A_n) \equiv \lim_{N\to \infty} \sum_{n=1}^{N} μ(A_n). \end{align*} $$

We call $(E, \mathcal{A}, μ)$ a measure space.


  • If $μ(E) = 1$, then $μ$ is a probability measure.

  • If $A\subseteq B$ are measurable, then $μ(B) = μ(A) + μ(B\setminus A) \leq μ(A)$. In particular if $μ(A)$ is finite, then $μ(B\setminus A) = μ(B) - μ(A)$.

  • $μ(A) + μ(B) = μ(A\cup B) + μ(A\cap B)$.

Proposition (continuity of measure) open_in_new

Let $(E, \mathcal{A}, μ)$ be a measure space, and $(A_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a sequence in $\mathcal{A}$.

  1. If $(A_n)$ is increasing (i.e. $A_0 \subseteq A_1 \subseteq A_2 \subseteq \ldots$), then $$ \begin{align*} μ\left(\bigcup_{n\geq 0} A_n\right) = \lim_{n\to \infty} μ(A_n). \end{align*} $$

  2. If $μ(A_0)<\infty$ and $(A_n)$ is decreasing (i.e. $A_0 \supseteq A_1 \supseteq A_2 \supseteq \ldots$), then $$ \begin{align*} μ\left(\bigcap_{n\geq 0} A_n\right) = \lim_{n\to \infty} μ(A_n). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (sigma-subadditivity of measure) open_in_new

Let $(E, \mathcal{A}, μ)$ be a measure space,

For a countable family $(A_n)_{n\in \N}$ in $\mathcal{A}$, $$ \begin{align*} μ\biggl(\bigcup_{n\in \N} A_n\biggr) \leq \sum_{n\in \N} μ(A_n). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (atom, diffuse measure, negligible set, complete measure) open_in_new

Let $(E, \mathcal{A}, μ)$ be a measure space.

  • A point $x\in E$ is called an atom if $\set{x}\in \mathcal{A}$ and $μ(\set{x}) >0$.

  • $μ$ is called diffuse if it has no atom.

  • A subset $B\subseteq E$ is negligible if there exists some $A\in \mathcal{A}$ with $μ(A) = 0$ such that $B\subseteq A$.

  • Say a property holds almost everywhere, almost surely etc if it holds outside a negligible set.

  • $μ$ is called complete if every negligible set is measurable.

Definition (monotone class) open_in_new

Let $E$ be a set.

A family $\mathcal{M}\subseteq \mathcal{P}(E)$ of subsets of $E$ is called a monotone class if

  1. $E\in \mathcal{M}$;

  2. if $A,B\in \mathcal{M}$ and $A\subseteq B$, then $B\setminus A\in \mathcal{M}$;

  3. if $(A_n)_{n\in \N}$ is an increasing sequence in $\mathcal{M}$, then $\bigcup_{n\in \N} A_n\in \mathcal{M}$.

Taking $B=E$ in property 2 means $\mathcal{M}$ is stable by complementation.

If $(\mathcal{M}_i)_{i\in I}$ is a collection of monotone classes on $E$, then $\bigcap_{i\in I} \mathcal{M}_i$ is also a monotone class on $E$.

Definition (monotone class generated by set) open_in_new

Let $E$ be a set.

For any subset $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(E)$, the monotone class generated by $C$ is the smallest monotone class on $E$ containing $\mathcal{C}$: $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{M}(\mathcal{C}) = \bigcup_{\substack{\mathcal{M} \text{ monotone class}\\ \mathcal{C}\subseteq \mathcal{M}}} \mathcal{M}. \end{align*} $$

Lemma (monotone class stable by finite intersections is a sigma-algebra) open_in_new

Let $E$ be a set and $\mathcal{M}\subseteq \mathcal{P}(E)$.

$\mathcal{M}$ is a monotone class that is stable by finite intersections iff $\mathcal{M}$ is a $σ$-algebra.

Proof

Corollary (uniqueness of measures) open_in_new

Let $μ, ν$ be two measures on a measurable space $(E, \mathcal{A})$. Suppose $\mathcal{A}={}$ $σ$$(\mathcal{C})$ with some $\mathcal{C}\subseteq \mathcal{A}$ that is stable by finite intersections, and $μ(A) = ν(A)$ for all $A\in \mathcal{C}$.

Then $μ\equiv ν$ if one of the following conditions is satisfied:

  1. $μ(E) = ν(E) < \infty$;

  2. there exists an increasing sequence $(E_n)_{n\in \N}$ in $\mathcal{C}$ such that $E = \bigcup_{n\in \N} E_n$ and $μ(E_n) = ν(E_n) < \infty$ for all $n\in \N$.

Proof

Definition (measurable function) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A})$ and $(F,\mathcal{B})$ be two measurable spaces.

A function $f: E\to F$ is measurable if $f\inv(B) \in \mathcal{A}$ for all $B\in \mathcal{B}$.

Composition of measurable functions is measurable.

Proposition (function measurable iff preimages of generating sets are measurable) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A})$ and $(F,\mathcal{B})$ be measurable spaces.

Suppose $\mathcal{B} ={}$$σ$$(\mathcal{C})$ for some $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(F)$. Then $f: E\to F$ is measurable iff $f\inv(C)\in \mathcal{A}$ for all $C\in \mathcal{C}$.

Proof

Continuous functions are measurable with respect to Borel $σ$-algebras.

Definition (extended real number line) open_in_new

The extended real number line is $$ \begin{align*} \overline{\R} = \R \cup \set{-\infty, +\infty}. \end{align*} $$

$\overline{\R}$ is homeomorphic to $[-1, 1]$.

Definition (lim inf, lim sup) open_in_new

Let $(a_n)_{n\geq 0}$ be a sequence in $\overline{\R}$. Define $$ \begin{align*} \limsup_{n\to \infty} a_n &:= \limdown_{n\to \infty} \sup_{k\geq n} a_k = \inf_{n\geq 0} \sup_{k\geq n} a_k, \\ \liminf_{n\to \infty} a_n &:= \limup_{n\to \infty} \inf_{k\geq n} a_k = \sup_{n\geq 0} \inf_{k\geq n} a_k. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (measurability in pointwise limits) open_in_new

Let $\{f_n: (E, \mathcal{A})\to (\overline{\R}, \mathcal{B}(\overline{\R}))\}_{n\in \N}$ be a sequence of measurable functions.

  1. The functions $$ \begin{align*} \sup_{n\in \N} f_n, \quad \inf_{n\in \N} f_n, \quad \limsup_{n\to\infty} f_n, \quad \liminf_{n\to \infty} f_n \end{align*} $$ are measurable.

  2. In particular, if $f_n$ converges pointwise to $f: E\to \overline{\R}$, then $f$ is measurable.

  3. For any such $f_n$, the set $$ \begin{align*} \sb{x\in E}{\text{$f_n(x)$ has a limit as $n\to \infty$}} \end{align*} $$ is measurable.

Proof

Definition (image of measure by function) open_in_new

Let $f: (E, \mathcal{A}) \to (F, \mathcal{B})$ be a measurable function, and $μ$ is a non-negative measure on $(E,\mathcal{A})$.

The image of $μ$ by $f$, denoted $f_\astμ$ or $f(μ)$ is the non-negative measure on $(F,\mathcal{B})$ given by $$ \begin{align*} f_\astμ(B) = μ(f\inv(B)), \qquad \forall B\in \mathcal{B}. \end{align*} $$

Definition (simple function) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A})$ be a measurable space.

A function $f: (E,\mathcal{A}) \to \R$ is called simple if it takes a finite number of values $α_1 < α_2 < \ldots < α_n$, and $f\inv(\set{α_j})\in \mathcal{A}$ for all $j=1,\ldots,n$.

Equivalently, there exists a finite partition $\set{A_1, \ldots, A_n}$ of $E$ into $\mathcal{A}$-measurable sets and values $α_1, \ldots, α_n\in \R$ such that $$ \begin{align*} f = \sum_{j=1}^{n} α_j \mathbb{1}_{A_j}, \end{align*} $$ which is called the canonical representation of $f$.

Definition (integral of simple function) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A},μ)$ be a measure space.

For a simple function $f: (E, \mathcal{A}) \to [0,\infty)$ given by $$ \begin{align*} f = \sum_{j=1}^{n} α_j \mathbb{1}_{A_j}, \qquad A_j\in \mathcal{A}, \qquad E = \bigsqcup_{j=1}^{n} A_j, \end{align*} $$ the integral of $f$ with respect to $μ$ is $$ \begin{align*} \int f \d μ = \sum_{j=1}^{n} α_j μ(A_j), \end{align*} $$ where we adopt the convention that if $α_j = 0$ and $μ(A_j) = \infty$, then $α_j μ(A_j) = 0$.

Because of the additivity property of $μ$, the value of the integral is the same even if the $α_j$'s are not distinct.

Linearity: $\int (af + bg) \d μ = a \int f \d μ + b \int g \d μ$ for two simple functions $f,g\geq 0$ and constants $a,b\geq 0$. (Proof)

If $f\leq g$, then $\int f \d μ \leq \int g \d μ$. (Proof)

Definition (integral of non-negative measurable function) open_in_new

Let $(E,A,μ)$ be a measure space.

For a non-negative measurable function $f: (E,\mathcal{A}) \to [0,\infty] \subseteq{}$$\overline{\R}$, the integral of $f$ with respect to $μ$ is $$ \begin{align*} \int f \d μ = \sup_{\substack{h\in \mathcal{E}_+ \\h\leq f}} \int h \d μ \end{align*} $$ where $\mathcal{E}_+$ is the set of non-negative simple functions $(E,\mathcal{A}) \to [0,\infty)$.

This is consistent with the definition of integrals for simple functions because if $f\leq g$ are non-negative simple, then $\int f \d μ \leq \int g \d μ$.

If $f\leq g$ are non-negative measurable, then $\int f \d μ \leq \int g \d μ$ by definition.

Theorem (monotone convergence theorem) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A},μ)$ be a measure space.

If $(f_n: E \to [0,\infty])_{n\in \N}$ is an increasing sequence of non-negative measurable functions, then their integrals are related by $$ \begin{align*} \int \limup_{n\to \infty} f_n \d μ = \limup_{n\to \infty} \int f_n \d μ. \end{align*} $$

Proof

$\int \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n \d μ = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \int f_n \d μ$ for non-negative measurable $(f_n)$ by applying the theorem to $F_n = \sum_{k=1}^{n} f_n$.

Definition (measure modulated by density) open_in_new

Let $μ$ be a measure on $(E,\mathcal{A})$ and $f: E\to [0,\infty]$ be a non-negative measurable function.

The measure modulated by density $f$, denoted $f\cdot μ$, is the measure on $E$ given by $$ \begin{align*} (f\cdot μ)(A) := \int \mathbb{1}_A f \d μ =: \int_{A} f \d μ. \end{align*} $$

Proposition (almost everywhere properties and integrals) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A},μ)$ be a measure space, and $f,g: E \to [0,\infty]$ be non-negative measurable functions.

  1. If $\int f \d μ < \infty$, then $f < \infty$ almost everywhere.

  2. $\int f \d μ = 0$ iff $f = 0$ almost everywhere.

  3. If $f=g$ almost everywhere, then $\int f \d μ = \int g \d μ$.

Proof

Theorem (Fatou's lemma) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A},μ)$ be a measure space,

For any sequence $(f_n: E \to [0,\infty])_{n\in \N}$ of measurable functions, $$ \begin{align*} \int \liminf_{n\to \infty} f_n \d μ \leq \liminf_{n\to \infty} \int f_n \d μ. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Example of strict inequality: take $f_n(x) = \frac{1}{n}\mathbb{1}_{[0,n]}$ on $x\in \R$. Then $f_n \to 0$ (in fact uniformly) but $\int f_n \d μ = 1$.

Definition (integrable, integral of measurable functions) open_in_new

Let $(E,\mathcal{A},μ)$ be a measure space.

We say a measurable function $f: E\to \R$ is integrable if the integral of its absolute value is finite: $$ \begin{align*} \int |f| \d μ < \infty. \end{align*} $$

Write $\mathcal{L}^1(E,A,μ)$ for the space of integrable functions.

If this holds, then define its integral as $$ \begin{align*} \int f \d μ := \int f^+ \d μ - \int f^- \d μ \end{align*} $$ where $f^+ = \max\set{f,0}$ and $f^- = \max\set{-f, 0}$.

Since $\int f^{\pm}\leq \int |f|< \infty$, we don't get $\infty-\infty$ in $\int f^+ - \int f^-$.

This is consistent with our definition for non-negative measurable functions.

We can allow $f$ to take values in $\overline{\R}$, but integrability implies $f\inv(\set{\pm \infty})$ is negligible.
