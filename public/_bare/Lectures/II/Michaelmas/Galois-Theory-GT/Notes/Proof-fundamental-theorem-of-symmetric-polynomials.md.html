Surjectivity

Define the lexicographic ordering on the set of monomials by $$ \begin{align*} X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n} < X_1^{j_1} \ldots X_n^{j_n} :\iff \exists r: i_1 = j_1, \ldots, i_{r-1} = j_{r-1}, i_r < j_r. \end{align*} $$ This is a total ordering.

If $f = 0$ , we are done. Otherwise, induct on the total degree of $f$ and the largest (with respect to $<$) monomial, say $X_1^{i_1}\ldots X_n^{i_n}$, appearing in a symmetric polynomial $f$ (meaning its coefficient in $f$ is non-zero). Because $f$ is symmetric, $X_{σ(1)}^{i_1} \ldots X_{σ(n)}^{i_n}$ also appears in $f$. Because $X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n}$ is largest, we must have $i_1 \geq i_2 \geq \ldots \geq i_n$.

Observing $$ \begin{align*} X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n} = X_1^{i_1 - i_2} (X_1 X_2)^{i_2 - i_3} \ldots (X_1 \ldots X_n)^{i_n}, \end{align*} $$ so define $$ \begin{align*} g := s_1^{i_1 - i_2} s_2^{i_2 - i_3} \ldots s_n^{i_n}. \end{align*} $$

Note that for $h_1,h_2\in R[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$, the largest monomial appearing in $h_1 h_2$ is the product of the one in $h_1$ and the one in $h_2$. Therefore the largest monomial in $g$ is $X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n}$. Letting $c\neq 0$ be the coefficient of $X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n}$ in $f$, we form $F := f - cg$, a symmetric polynomial with total degree $\leq \deg f$ and the largest monomial smaller than $X_1^{i_1} \ldots X_n^{i_n}$.

Since the number of monomials of bounded degree is finite, after a finite number of steps, the process stops, and we obtain $f$ as a polynomial in $s_1, \ldots, s_n$.

Injectivity

Here, write $s_{1,n}, \ldots, s_{n,n}\in R[X_1, \ldots, X_n]$ for the elementary symmetric polynomials in $n$ variables. Induct on $n$ to show the only polynomial $g\in R[Y_1, \ldots, Y_n]$ such that $g(s_{1,n}, \ldots, s_{n,n}) = 0$ is $g = 0$.

For $n=1$, $s_{1,1} = X_1$ so this is trivial.

For $n\geq 2$, suppose $g \neq 0$. Factorise as $$ \begin{align*} g(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n) = Y_n^{m} G(Y_1, \ldots, Y_n) \end{align*} $$ where $m\geq 0$ and $G\in R[Y_1, \ldots, Y_n]^\times$ such that $Y_n\not\mid G$. Because $s_{n,n}\neq 0$, we must have $G(s_{1,n},\ldots, s_{n,n}) = 0$, so by replacing $g$ with $G$, we can WLOG assume $Y_n \not\mid g$.

Note setting $X_n = 0$ gives $$ \begin{align*} s_{r,n}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}, 0) = \begin{cases} s_{r,n-1}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}), &\text{if $r = 1,2,\ldots,n-1$}, \\ 0, &\text{if $r = n$} \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ so $$ \begin{align*} 0 ={}& g(s_{1,n}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1}, 0), \ldots, s_{n,n}(X_1, \ldots, X_{n-1},0)) \\ ={}& g(s_{1,n-1}, \ldots, s_{n-1,n-1}, 0). \end{align*} $$ By induction hypothesis, since $\tilde g(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n-1}) := g(Y_1, \ldots, Y_{n-1}, 0)$ is a polynomial such that $\tilde g(s_{1,n-1}, \ldots, s_{n-1, n-1}) = 0$, we have $\tilde g = 0$. Therefore $Y_n \mid g$, which is a contradiction.
