In this course, all vector spaces are over $\mathbb{F} = \R$ or $\C$.

Definition (norm, normed space) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$.

A norm on $X$ is a function $\|\cdot \|\to \R$ satisfying

  1. positivity: $\|x\| \geq 0$ for all $x\in X$, and $\|x\| = 0$ iff $x = 0$;

  2. homogeneity: $\|λx\| = |λ| \, \|x\|$ for all $λ\in \mathbb{F}$, $x\in X$; and

  3. triangle inequality: $\|x + y\| \leq \|x\| + \|y\|$ for all $x,y\in X$.

A normed space is a pair $(X, \|\cdot \|)$ where $X$ is a vector space and $\|\cdot \|$ is a norm on $X$.

  • The norm induces a metric $d(x,y) = \|x - y\|$ and hence a norm topology on $X$.

  • $x\mapsto \|x\|$ is a 1-Lipschitz function, thus continuous.

Definition (Banach space) open_in_new

A Banach space is a complete normed space.

Proposition (unit ball) open_in_new

The unit ball of a normed space $X$ is $$ \begin{align*} B_X = \sb{x\in X}{\|x\|\leq 1}, \end{align*} $$ which is

  1. symmetric: $x\in B_X \iff -x\in B_X$;

  2. convex: if $x,y\in B_X$ and $λ\in [0,1]$, then $(1-t)x + ty\in B_X$; and

  3. closed.

Conversely, for a metric space $X$, if $B\subseteq X$ is a symmetric, convex, closed and bounded neighbourhood of $0$, then there is a norm on $X$ given by $$ \begin{align*} \|x\| = \inf\sb{t\geq 0}{x\in tB} \end{align*} $$ such that $B$ is the unit ball of $(X, \|\cdot\|)$.

Definition (dense set, separable space) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a topological space.

A subset $A \subseteq X$ is said to be dense if equivalently

  1. $\overline{A}$${}= X$;

  2. for any open set $U\subseteq X$, we have $A \cap U\neq \emptyset$;

  3. for any open set $U\subseteq X$, we have $U \subseteq \overline{A \cap U}$.

$X$ is said to be separable if it has a countable dense set.

Definition (bounded linear maps) open_in_new

Let $X, Y$ be normed spaces, and $T: X\to Y$ be a linear map.

$T$ is said to be bounded if there exists $C\geq 0$ such that $$\|Tx\| \leq C \|x\| \qquad \text{for all }x\in X.$$

Its operator norm is $$ \begin{align*} \|T\| = \sup \sb{\|Tx\|}{x\in B_X}. \end{align*} $$

Write $\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ for the set of bounded linear maps $X\to Y$.

  • $(\mathcal{B}(X,Y), \|\cdot\|)$ is a normed space.

  • If $X \neq \{0\}$, then $(\mathcal{B}(X), \|\cdot\|)$ is a (non-commutative) unital normed algebra with multiplication being composition.

Theorem (bounded linear maps and continuity) open_in_new

Let $X, Y$ be normed spaces, and $T: X\to Y$ be a linear map. The following are equivalent:

  1. $T$ is bounded;

  2. $T$ is Lipschitz;

  3. $T$ is continuous;

  4. $T$ is continuous at 0.

Proof

Definition (isomorphism of normed spaces) open_in_new

Let $X,Y$ be normed spaces.

  • An isomorphism is a bijective linear map $T: X\to Y$ such that $T$ and $T\inv$ are bounded.

    Equivalently, $T$ is a surjective linear map satisfying that $$ \begin{align*} a\|x\|\leq \|Tx\|\leq b\|x\| \qquad \text{for all }x\in X \end{align*} $$ for some $a,b>0$.

    If such $T$ exists, we say $X,Y$ are isomorphic, and we write $X\sim Y$.

  • An isometric isomorphism is a linear bijection $T: X\to Y$ such that for every $x\in X$, $\|Tx\| = \|x\|$ (so $a=b=1$).

    If such $T$ exists, then say that $X,Y$ are isometrically isomorphic and write $X\cong Y$.

  • An isomorphic embedding $X\to Y$ is a linear map $T: X\to Y$ such that $T: X\to TX = \im T$ is an isomorphism.

    If such $T$ exists, we say $X$ (isomorphically) embeds into $Y$ and write $X\hookrightarrow Y$.


  • If $X\sim Y$, then $X$ is complete iff $Y$ is complete.

Definition (equivalent norms) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a vector space.

Two norms $\|\cdot \|$, $\|\cdot \|'$ on $X$ are said to be equivalent if $\id: (X, \|\cdot \|)\to (X, \|\cdot \|')$ is an isomorphism of normed spaces.

Equivalently, there exists $a,b>0$ such that $$ \begin{align*} a\|x\| \leq \|x\|' \leq b \|x\| \qquad \text{for all }x\in X. \end{align*} $$

Definition (direct sum of normed spaces) open_in_new

Let $X,Y,Z$ be normed spaces.

The (external) direct sum $Y\oplus Z$ of $Y$ and $Z$ is the algebraic direct sum of the vector spaces $Y$ and $Z$ equipped with the norm $$ \begin{align*} \|(y,z)\| = \|y\| + \|z\|. \end{align*} $$

Suppose $Y$ and $Z$ are subspaces of $X$. We say $X$ is the (topological) direct sum of $Y$ and $Z$, written $X = Y \oplus Z$, if $X = Y + Z$, $Y \cap Z = \set{0}$, and $$ \begin{align*} T: Y \oplus Z &\to X \\ (y, z) &\mapsto y + z \end{align*} $$ is an isomorphism of normed spaces, i.e. there exists $C>0$ such that $\|y\| + \|z\| \leq C\|y + z\|$.

  • The topology on $Y \oplus Z$ is the product of the topologies on $Y$ and $Z$.

Definition (dual normed space) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a normed space.

The dual space $X^\ast$ of $X$ is $\mathcal{B}(X, \mathbb{F})$, the space of all bounded linear functionals on $X$.

We write $\langle x,f\rangle = f(x)$ for $f\in X^\ast$, $x\in X$.

  • Since $\mathbb{F}$ is complete, $X^\ast$ is always complete.

Definition (nowhere dense set) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a topological space.

A subset $A\subseteq X$ is said to be nowhere dense if equivalently

  1. $\op{int} \overline{A} = \emptyset$;

  2. for any non-empty open set $U\subseteq X$, we have $U \not\subseteq \overline{U \cap A}$.

Definition (meagre, first category, second category) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a topological space.

A subset $A\subseteq X$ is said to be meagre in $X$ or is of first category in $X$ if $A = \bigcup_{n\in \N} A_n$ where $A_n$ is nowhere dense for all $n\in \N$.

Say $A$ is of second category in $X$ if $A$ is not of first category of $X$.

Theorem (Baire category) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a metric space.

If $X$ is complete, then equivalently

  1. if $(U_n)_{n\in \N}$ is a countable family of open dense subsets of $X$, then the intersection $\bigcap_{n} U_n$ is dense (in particular non-empty);

  2. if $(A_n)_{n\in \N}$ is a countable family of nowhere dense subsets of $X$, then the union $\bigcup_{n} A_n$ has empty interior (in particular cannot be $X$ if $X$ is non-empty).

Proof

Theorem (principle of uniform boundedness) open_in_new

Let $X,Y$ be normed spaces, and $\mathcal{T}\subseteq{}$$\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$.

Assume $X$ is complete. If $\mathcal{T}$ is pointwise bounded (i.e. for all $x\in X$, $\sup_{T\in \mathcal{T}} \|Tx\| < \infty$), then $\mathcal{T}$ is uniformly bounded (i.e. $\sup_{T\in \mathcal{T}} \|T\| < \infty$).

Proof

Corollary (pointwise limit of bounded linear maps) open_in_new

Let $X,Y$ be a normed space, and $(T_n)_{n\in \N}$ is a sequence in $\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$.

If $X$ is a Banach space and there is a map $T: X\to Y$ such that $T_n x \to Tx$ for all $x\in X$, then $\sup_{n\in \N} \|T_n\| < \infty$, $T\in \mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ and $\|T\| \leq \liminf_{n\to \infty} \|T_n\|$.

Theorem (open mapping theorem for bounded linear maps) open_in_new

Let $X,Y$ be Banach spaces.

If $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X,Y)$ is surjective, then $T$ is an open map.

Let $X,Y$ be Banach spaces. If $T: X\to Y$ is a continuous linear bijection, then $T\inv$ is also continuous, so $T$ is an isomorphism.

Definition (normal topological space) open_in_new

A topological space $X$ is said to be normal if whenever $A, B$ are disjoint closed sets in $X$, there exists disjoint open sets $U, V$ such that $A \subseteq U$ and $B \subseteq V$.

Equivalently, whenever $A$ is a closed set and $U$ is an open set such that $A \subseteq U$, there exists a closed set $B$ and an open set $V$ such that $A \subseteq V \subseteq B \subseteq U$.

  • Any compact Hausdorff space is normal.

Proof

Definition (algebra) open_in_new

An algebra over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is a vector space $A$ over $\mathbb{F}$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot: A \times A \to A$ satisfying, for all $x,y,z\in A$, $λ\in F$,

  1. associativity: $(xy)z = x(yz)$;

  2. left distributivity: $x(y+z) = xy + xz$;

  3. right distributivity: $(x+y)z = xz + yz$;

  4. compatibility with scalars: $x(λy) = (λx)y = λ(xy)$.

$A$ is a unital algebra if there exists an element $1\in A$ such that $1\cdot x = x = x \cdot 1$ for all $x\in A$. In which case, an element $x\in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists $y\in A$ such that $xy = yx = 1$.

$A$ is a commutative algebra if $xy = yx$ for all $x,y\in A$.

Definition (subalgebra) open_in_new

Let $A$ be an algebra.

A subset $B\subseteq A$ is a subalgebra of $A$ if it is a vector subspace of $A$ and $xy\in B$ for all $x,y\in B$.

$B$ is a unital subalgebra if in addition, $1\in B$.

Definition (normed algebra, Banach algebra) open_in_new

A normed algebra $A$ is a normed vector space that is also an algebra and satisfies $$\begin{align*} \|xy\| \leq \|x\| \|y\| \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in A. \end{align*} $$

$A$ is unital if moreover $A$ is a unital algebra with $\|1\| = 1$.

$A$ is said to be a Banach algebra if $A$ is complete.

Definition (separates points, strongly separates points) open_in_new

Let $S$ be a set of functions $K \to \C$.

$S$ is said to separate points of $K$ if for all $x,y\in K$ distinct, there exists $f\in S$ such that $f(x) \neq f(y)$.

$S$ is said to strongly separate points of $K$ if in addition, for all $x\in K$, there exists $f\in S$ such that $f(x) \neq 0$.

Theorem (Stone-Weierstrass) open_in_new

Let $K$ be a compact topological space.

  1. If $A$ is a subalgebra of $C^\R(K)$ that strongly separates points of $K$, then $A$ is dense in $C^\R(K)$.

  2. If $A$ is a subalgebra of $C^\C(K)$ that strongly separates points of $K$ and is closed under complex conjugation (i.e. if $f\in A$, then $\bar f\in A$), then $A$ is dense in $C^\C(K)$.

Proof

Theorem (Arzelà-Ascoli) open_in_new

Let $K$ be a compact topological space.

A subset $\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(K)$ is relatively compact iff

  1. $\mathcal{F}$ is uniformly bounded: $\sup_{f\in \mathcal{F}} \|f\|_\infty < \infty$; and

Definition (compact operator) open_in_new

Let $X,Y$ be Banach spaces.

A linear map $T: X\to Y$ is said to be compact if equivalently

  1. $\overline{T(B_X)}$ is compact;

  2. $T(B_X)$ is totally bounded (by Heine-Borel);

Definition (inner product space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a real (resp. complex) vector space.

An inner product on $V$ is a bilinear form (resp. sesquilinear form) $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on $V$ that is

  • symmetric (resp. Hermitian); and

  • positive definite.

$(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is called an inner product space.

Definition (norm in inner product space) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space.

Define the norm of a vector $v\in V$ as $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle} \geq 0$.

  • $\|v\| = 0$ iff $v=\v 0$.

  • $\|\cdot\|: V\to\R$ uniquely determines $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ by the polarisation identity.

Lemma (Cauchy-Schwarz) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} |\langle u,v\rangle| \leq \|u\| \,\|v\|. \end{align*} $$ Equality occurs iff $\set{u,v}$ spans a subspace of dimensions $\leq 1$.

Proof

  • This shows $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle: V\times V \to V$ is continuous with respect to the norm topology.

Corollary (triangle inequality) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} \|u+v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • Therefore any inner product space $V$ is a normed space.

Proposition (polarisation identity) open_in_new

  • Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $Q: V\to \mathbb{F}$ be a quadratic form. Suppose $1+1\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{F}$.

    The function $\psi: V\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ given by the polarisation identity $$ \begin{align*} \psi(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}\big(Q(u+v) - Q(u) - Q(v)\big) \end{align*} $$ is the unique symmetric bilinear form such that $Q(v) = \psi(v,v)$ for all $v\in V$.

  • Let $V$ be a $\C$-vector space and $\phi$ be a Hermitian sesquilinear form on $V$.

    $\phi$ is uniquely determined by $Q: V\to\R$ given by $Q(v) = \phi(v,v)\in \R$ via $$ \begin{align*} \phi(u,v) = \frac{1}{4}\big(Q(u+v) - Q(u-v) + iQ(u+iv) - iQ(u-iv)\big). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (parallelogram law) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space. Then $$ \begin{align*} \|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2 \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in X. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • Conversely, any normed space that satisfies the parallelogram law is an inner product space. (Sheet 3 Q8)

  • More generally for $n$ points $x_1, \ldots, x_n\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{ε_1, \ldots, ε_n \in \set{\pm 1}} \left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n} ε_i x_i\right\|^2 =\sum_{i=1}^{n} \|x_i\|^2 \end{align*} $$

Definition (orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is an orthonormal sequence if $\langle x_n, x_m\rangle = δ_{nm}$ for all $n,m\in \N$.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is an orthonormal basis if $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is orthonormal and $\overline{\op{span}}\sb{x_n}{n\in \N} = X$.

Theorem (Gram-Schmidt process) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an inner product space.

For any linearly independent (finite or infinite) sequence $v_1, v_2, \ldots\in V$, there exists an orthonormal sequence $e_1, e_2, \ldots \in V$ such that $$ \begin{align*} \langle e_1, \ldots, e_k\rangle = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_k\rangle \qquad \text{for all } k. \end{align*} $$ Moreover if $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_k}$ for some $k$ is already orthonormal, then $e_i = v_i$ for $i\leq k$.

Proof

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then performing Gram-Schmidt on a basis produces an orthonormal basis.

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $E\subseteq V$ is an orthonormal set, then we can extend to a basis for $V$ and perform Gram-Schmidt to obtain an orthonormal basis for $V$ containing $E$.

Corollary (inner product space is separable iff has orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space.

$X$ is separable iff $X$ has an orthonormal basis.

Proof

Theorem (Fourier series in inner product space) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $(e_n)_{n\in \N}$. For all $x\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle e_n. \end{align*} $$

We call $\langle x,e_n\rangle$ the $n$-th Fourier coefficient of $x$ with respect to $(e_n)$, and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$ the Fourier series.

Proof

Theorem (Parseval's identities for inner product spaces) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $(e_n)_{n\in \N}$. For $x,y\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} \|x\|^2 ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\langle x, e_n\rangle|^2, \\ \langle x,y\rangle ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle \, \overline{\langle y,e_n\rangle}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Theorem (classification of separable Hilbert spaces) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a separable inner product space.

  1. If $\dim X = n < \infty$, then $X \isom l_2^n$. Any orthonormal basis $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ gives an isometric isomorphism $$ \begin{align*} T: X &\to l_2^n \\ x &\mapsto (\langle x, e_1\rangle, \ldots, \langle x, e_n\rangle). \end{align*} $$

  2. If $\dim X = \infty$ and moreover $X$ is a Hilbert space, then $X \isom l_2$. Any orthonormal basis $e_1, e_2, \ldots$ gives an isometric isomorphism $$ \begin{align*} T: l_2 &\to X \\ x &\mapsto (\langle x, e_1\rangle, \langle x, e_2\rangle, \ldots). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (matrix with respect to orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $e_1, e_2,\ldots$. Let $T \in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

The matrix $A = (a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^\infty$ of $T$ with respect to $(e_n)$ is defined by $$ \begin{align*} a_{ij} = \langle Te_j, e_i\rangle. \end{align*} $$

  • Not every matrix is the matrix of a bounded linear operator.

  • However the matrix of $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ uniquely determines $X$: $$ \begin{align*} Tx = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} a_{ij} \langle x, e_j\rangle. \end{align*} $$

Theorem (adjoint map in Hilbert spaces) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

For any $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(H)$, there exists a unique function $T^\ast: H\to H$ satisfying $$ \begin{align*} \langle Tx, y\rangle = \langle x, T^\ast y\rangle \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in H. \end{align*} $$ Moreover $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(H)$.

$T^\ast$ is called the adjoint of $T$.

Proof

  • Under the identification $H = H^\ast$ via the Riesz representation, the adjoint and the dual are the same. (Commutative diagram)

    Therefore $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is compact iff its adjoint $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is compact. (Proof)

  • If $A$ is the matrix of $T$ with respect to some orthonormal basis, then the matrix of $T^\ast$ is $\overline{A^T}$.

  • $(λT + μS)^\ast = \overline{λ} T^\ast + \overline{μ} S^\ast$.

  • $(ST)^\ast = T^\ast S^\ast$.

  • $T^{\ast\ast} = T$.

  • $\|T^\ast\| = \|T\|$. (Proof)

  • $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is invertible iff $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is invertible, and $(T^\ast)\inv = (T\inv)^\ast$. (Proof)

Definition (Hermitian, self-adjoint operator) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an inner product space.

$\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$ is said to be Hermitian or self-adjoint if $\alpha$ is an adjoint map to itself.

Examples of Hermitian operators open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

  • Orthogonal projection from $H$ to any closed subspace is Hermitian. (Proof)

  • For any $T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$, $T^\ast T$ is Hermitian. Moreover $\|T^\ast T\| = \|T\|^2$. (Proof)

Proposition (decomposing complex bounded linear map into Hermitian maps) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

For any $T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$, there exists unique Hermitian operators $T_1, T_2$ such that $T = T_1 + iT_2$, which are given by $$ \begin{align*} T_1 = \frac{1}{2}(T + T^\ast), \qquad T_2 = \frac{1}{2i}(T - T^\ast). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (unitary operator) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

$T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$ is a unitary operator if equivalently

  1. $T^\ast$$T = T T^\ast = \id_H$;

  2. $T$ is invertible and $T\inv = T^\ast$.


  • $T^\ast T = \id_H$ is not sufficient, e.g. the adjoint of the right shift $R: l_2 \to l_2: e_i \mapsto e_{i+1}$ is the left shift $R^\ast: e_1 \mapsto 0, e_{i+1} \mapsto i_{i}$ with $R^\ast R = \id_{l_2}$, but $R$ is not invertible.

Definition (invertible bounded linear map) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a normed space.

$T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$ is said to be invertible if equivalently

  1. $T$ is invertible in the unital algebra $\mathcal{B}(X)$;

  2. there exists $S\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ such that $TS = ST = \id_X$;

  3. $T$ is a linear bijection and $T\inv$ is bounded, i.e. $T$ is an isomorphism of normed spaces.

Write $\mathcal{G}(X)$ for the group of invertible bounded linear operators $X\to X$.

  • If $X$ is a Banach space, then the inversion theorem says $T$ is invertible iff $T$ is a linear bijection.

  • Interaction with compact and finite-rank operators:

    • If $\dim X < \infty$, then $\mathcal{G}(X) = \mathcal{F}(K) = \mathcal{K}(K)$.

    • If $\dim X = \infty$, then $\mathcal{G}(X) \cap \mathcal{K}(K) = \emptyset$. (Proof)

Theorem (invertible operators as open subset and topological group) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a Banach space.

  1. Let $T\in \mathcal{G}(X)$ and $S\in \mathcal{B}(X)$. If $\|S\|<1/\|T\inv\|$, then $S - T\in \mathcal{G}(X)$.

    Therefore $\mathcal{G}(X)$ is an open subset of $\mathcal{B}(X)$.

    In particular, if $\|T\|<1$, then $$ \begin{align*} (\id - T)\inv = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} T^n, \qquad \|(\id - T)\inv\| \leq \frac{1}{1 - \|T\|}. \end{align*} $$

  2. $\mathcal{G}(X)$ is a topological group, i.e. composition and inverse are continuous.

  3. If $(T_n)_{n\in \N}$ is a sequence in $\mathcal{G}(X)$ such that $T_n \to T\in \mathcal{B}(X)\setminus \mathcal{G}(X)$, then $\|T_n\inv\|\to \infty$ as $n\to \infty$.

Proof

Definition (spectrum, resolvent set, resolvent function) open_in_new

Let $X\neq \set{0}$ be a complex Banach space and $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

The spectrum of $T$ is $$ \begin{align*} σ(T) = \sb{λ\in \C}{λ \id - T\not\in \mathcal{G}(X)}. \end{align*} $$

The resolvent set is $ρ(T) = \C \setminus σ(T)$. The resolvent function is $$ \begin{align*} R_T: ρ(T) &\to \mathcal{B}(X) \\ λ &\mapsto (λ \id - T)\inv. \end{align*} $$

  • $σ(T)$ is a closed subset of $\sb{z\in \C}{|z|\leq \|T\|}$, hence compact. (Proof)

  • If $|λ| > \|T\|$, then we saw $$ \begin{align*} R_T(λ) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{T^n}{λ^{n+1}}. \end{align*} $$

  • $R_T$ is a holomorphic function, in particular $\lim_{μ\to λ} \frac{R_T(μ) - R_T(λ)}{μ - λ} = -R_T(λ)^2$. (Proof)

Definition (point spectrum, approximate point spectrum) open_in_new

Let $X\neq \set{0}$ be a complex Banach space and $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

The point spectrum of $T$ is $$ \begin{align*} σ_\text{p}(T) = \set{\text{eigenvaues of $T$}} = \sb{λ\in \C}{λ\id - T \text{ is not injective}}. \end{align*} $$

$λ\in \C$ is an approximate eigenvalue of $T$ if there exists a sequence $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ in $X$ (an approximate eigenvector) such that $\|x_n\| = 1$ and $(λ\id - T) x_n\to 0$ as $n\to \infty$.

The approximate point spectrum of $T$ is $$ \begin{align*} σ_\text{ap}(T) = \set{\text{approximate eigenvalues of $T$}}. \end{align*} $$

  • $σ_\text{p}(T) \subseteq σ_\text{ap}(T) \subseteq{}$$σ(T)$. (Proof)

  • If $\dim X < \infty$, then $σ_\text{p}(T) = σ_\text{ap}(T) = σ(T)$.

  • $\partial$$σ(T) \subseteq σ_\text{ap}(T)$. In particular $σ_{\text{ap}}(T)$. (Proof)

Theorem (spectral mapping theorem) open_in_new

Let $X\neq \set{0}$ be a complex Banach space and $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

For any complex polynomial $p(z) = a_0 + a_1 z + \ldots + a_d z^d$, the spectrum of $p(T) \equiv a_0 \id + a_1 T + \ldots + a_d T^d$ is the image of the spectrum of $T$ under $p$: $$ \begin{align*} σ(p(T)) = p(σ(T)). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Theorem (spectral radius formula) open_in_new

Let $X\neq \set{0}$ be a complex Banach space and $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

Define the spectral radius of $T$ as $r(T) = \sup{} \{|λ|\ |\ λ\in{}$$σ(T)$$\}$. Then $$ \begin{align*} r(T) = \lim_{n\to \infty} \|T^n\|^{1/n}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Theorem (non-zero approximate eigenvalue of compact operator is eigenvalue) open_in_new

Let $X\neq \set{0}$ be a complex Banach space and $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

Suppose $T$ is compact. If $λ$ is a non-zero approximate eigenvalue of $T$, then $λ$ is an eigenvalue of $T$, i.e. $σ_\text{ap}(λ) \subseteq σ_\text{p}(λ) \cup \set{0}$.

Proof

  • In fact $σ_\text{ap}(λ) \subseteq σ_\text{p}(λ) \cup \set{0}$.

Definition (invariant subspace) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a vector space, $Y$ be a subspace and $T: X \to X$ be a linear map.

$Y$ is an invariant subspace of $T$ if $T(Y) \subseteq Y$.

  • $Y = \set{0}$ and $X$ are the trivial invariant subspaces.

  • If $λ$ is an eigenvalue of $T$, then the eigenspace $E_T(λ) = \ker(λ\id - T)$ is an invariant subspace of $T$. If $X$ is normed, then $E_T(λ)$ is closed.
