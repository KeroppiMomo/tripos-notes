Any separable inner product space has an orthonormal basis.

$\dim X = n < \infty$

$T$ is an inverse $$ \begin{align*} T\inv: a \mapsto \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i e_i \end{align*} $$ so is a bijection.

It is isometric because by Pythagoras $$ \begin{align*} \|T\inv a\|^2 = \left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_ie_i\right\|^2 ={}& \sum_{i=1}^{n} |a_i|^2 = \|a\|_2^2 \end{align*} $$ for all $a\in l_2^n$.

$\dim X = \infty$ and $X$ is complete

Parseval's identity says for all $x\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} \|x\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} |\langle x,e_i\rangle|^2 = \|Tx\|_2^2 \end{align*} $$ so $T$ is a well-defined isometric linear map.

To show surjectivity, fix $a\in l_2$. Let, for each $n\in \N$, $$ \begin{align*} s_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i e_i. \end{align*} $$

Then by Pythagoras, for $n\geq m$, $$ \begin{align*} \|s_n - s_m\|^2 = \left\|\sum_{i=m+1}^{n} a_ie_i\right\|^2 = \sum_{i=m+1}^{n} |a_i|^2 \leq \sum_{i=m+1}^{\infty} |a_i|^2 \to 0 \end{align*} $$ as $m\to \infty$. Hence $(s_n)_{n\in \N}$ is a Cauchy sequence. Since $X$ is complete, $s_n$ converges to say $s \equiv \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} a_i e_i$.

For each $i\in \N$, $\langle s_n, e_i\rangle = a_i$ for all $n\geq i$, so by the continuity of $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle$, we have $\langle s,e_i\rangle = a_i$. Hence $Ts = a$.
