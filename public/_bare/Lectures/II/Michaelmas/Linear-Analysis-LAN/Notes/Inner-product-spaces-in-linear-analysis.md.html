Definition (inner product space) open_in_new

Let $V$ be a real (resp. complex) vector space.

An inner product on $V$ is a bilinear form (resp. sesquilinear form) $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ on $V$ that is

  • symmetric (resp. Hermitian); and

  • positive definite.

$(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ is called an inner product space.

Definition (norm in inner product space) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space.

Define the norm of a vector $v\in V$ as $\|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle} \geq 0$.

  • $\|v\| = 0$ iff $v=\v 0$.

  • $\|\cdot\|: V\to\R$ uniquely determines $\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle$ by the polarisation identity.

Lemma (Cauchy-Schwarz) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} |\langle u,v\rangle| \leq \|u\| \,\|v\|. \end{align*} $$ Equality occurs iff $\set{u,v}$ spans a subspace of dimensions $\leq 1$.

Proof

This shows $\langle \cdot ,\cdot \rangle: V\times V \to V$ is continuous with respect to the norm topology.

Corollary (triangle inequality) open_in_new

Let $(V,\langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle)$ be an inner product space. For all $u,v\in V$, $$ \begin{align*} \|u+v\| \leq \|u\| + \|v\|. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Therefore any inner product space $V$ is a normed space.

Proposition (polarisation identity) open_in_new

  • Let $V$ be an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space and $Q: V\to \mathbb{F}$ be a quadratic form. Suppose $1+1\neq 0$ in $\mathbb{F}$.

    The function $\psi: V\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ given by the polarisation identity $$ \begin{align*} \psi(u,v) = \frac{1}{2}\big(Q(u+v) - Q(u) - Q(v)\big) \end{align*} $$ is the unique symmetric bilinear form such that $Q(v) = \psi(v,v)$ for all $v\in V$.

  • Let $V$ be a $\C$-vector space and $\phi$ be a Hermitian sesquilinear form on $V$.

    $\phi$ is uniquely determined by $Q: V\to\R$ given by $Q(v) = \phi(v,v)\in \R$ via $$ \begin{align*} \phi(u,v) = \frac{1}{4}\big(Q(u+v) - Q(u-v) + iQ(u+iv) - iQ(u-iv)\big). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Proposition (parallelogram law) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space. Then $$ \begin{align*} \|x + y\|^2 + \|x - y\|^2 = 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2 \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in X. \end{align*} $$

Proof

  • Conversely, any normed space that satisfies the parallelogram law is an inner product space. (Sheet 3 Q8)

  • More generally for $n$ points $x_1, \ldots, x_n\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2^n} \sum_{ε_1, \ldots, ε_n \in \set{\pm 1}} \left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n} ε_i x_i\right\|^2 =\sum_{i=1}^{n} \|x_i\|^2 \end{align*} $$

Definition (Hilbert space) open_in_new

A Hilbert space is a complete inner product space.

Theorem (closest point theorem) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

If $Y$ is a closed subspace of $H$, then for any $x\in H$, there exists a unique $y\in Y$ such that $\|x - y\| = d(x,Y)$.

Proof

Definition (orthogonal to set) open_in_new

Let $U$ and $V$ be $\mathbb{F}$-vector spaces and $\phi: U\times V\to \mathbb{F}$ be a bilinear form.

For subsets $S\subseteq U$ and $T\subseteq V$, define

  • $S^\perp = \sb{v\in V}{\phi(s,v)=0 \, \forall s\in S} \leq V$, and

  • $^\perp T = \sb{u\in U}{\phi(u, t)=0 \, \forall t\in T} \leq U$.


  • Inclusion reversing: $S_1 \subseteq S_2 \subseteq U \implies S_2^\perp \leq S_1^\perp$. Similarly in $V$.

  • $U^\perp = \ker($$\phi_R$$)$ and $^\perp V = \ker(\phi_L)$.

Theorem (orthogonal complement in Hilbert space) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

For any closed subspace $Y$ of $H$, we have $H = Y$${}\oplus{}$$Y^\perp$.

Moreover the projection $$ \begin{align*} P: H &\to H \\ y + z &\mapsto y, &&y\in Y, z\in Y^\perp \end{align*} $$ sends $x$ to the closest point in $Y$ to $x$.

We say $H$ is the orthogonal direct sum of $Y$ and $Y^\perp$, $Y^\perp$ is the orthogonal complement of $Y$, and $P$ is the orthogonal projection onto $Y$.

Proof

Theorem (Riesz representation for Hilbert spaces) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

There is an isometric conjugate-linear isomorphism $$ \begin{align*} ϕ_H: H &\to H^\ast \\ x &\mapsto (θ_x: y \mapsto \langle y,x\rangle). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is an orthonormal sequence if $\langle x_n, x_m\rangle = δ_{nm}$ for all $n,m\in \N$.

A sequence $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is an orthonormal basis if $(x_n)_{n\in \N}$ is orthonormal and $\overline{\op{span}}\sb{x_n}{n\in \N} = X$.

Theorem (Gram-Schmidt process) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an inner product space.

For any linearly independent (finite or infinite) sequence $v_1, v_2, \ldots\in V$, there exists an orthonormal sequence $e_1, e_2, \ldots \in V$ such that $$ \begin{align*} \langle e_1, \ldots, e_k\rangle = \langle v_1, \ldots, v_k\rangle \qquad \text{for all } k. \end{align*} $$ Moreover if $\set{v_1, \ldots, v_k}$ for some $k$ is already orthonormal, then $e_i = v_i$ for $i\leq k$.

Proof

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional, then performing Gram-Schmidt on a basis produces an orthonormal basis.

  • If $V$ is finite-dimensional and $E\subseteq V$ is an orthonormal set, then we can extend to a basis for $V$ and perform Gram-Schmidt to obtain an orthonormal basis for $V$ containing $E$.

Corollary (inner product space is separable iff has orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space.

$X$ is separable iff $X$ has an orthonormal basis.

Proof

Theorem (Fourier series in inner product space) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $(e_n)_{n\in \N}$. For all $x\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle e_n. \end{align*} $$

We call $\langle x,e_n\rangle$ the $n$-th Fourier coefficient of $x$ with respect to $(e_n)$, and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle e_n$ the Fourier series.

Proof

Theorem (Parseval's identities for inner product spaces) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $(e_n)_{n\in \N}$. For $x,y\in X$, $$ \begin{align*} \|x\|^2 ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} |\langle x, e_n\rangle|^2, \\ \langle x,y\rangle ={}& \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \langle x,e_n\rangle \, \overline{\langle y,e_n\rangle}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Theorem (classification of separable Hilbert spaces) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a separable inner product space.

  1. If $\dim X = n < \infty$, then $X \isom l_2^n$. Any orthonormal basis $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ gives an isometric isomorphism $$ \begin{align*} T: X &\to l_2^n \\ x &\mapsto (\langle x, e_1\rangle, \ldots, \langle x, e_n\rangle). \end{align*} $$

  2. If $\dim X = \infty$ and moreover $X$ is a Hilbert space, then $X \isom l_2$. Any orthonormal basis $e_1, e_2, \ldots$ gives an isometric isomorphism $$ \begin{align*} T: X &\to l_2 \\ x &\mapsto (\langle x, e_1\rangle, \langle x, e_2\rangle, \ldots). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (matrix with respect to orthonormal basis) open_in_new

Let $X$ be an inner product space with an orthonormal basis $e_1, e_2,\ldots$. Let $T \in{}$$\mathcal{B}(X)$.

The matrix $A = (a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^\infty$ of $T$ with respect to $(e_n)$ is defined by $$ \begin{align*} a_{ij} = \langle Te_j, e_i\rangle. \end{align*} $$

Not every matrix is the matrix of a bounded linear operator.

However the matrix of $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ uniquely determines $X$: $$ \begin{align*} Tx = \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} a_{ij} \langle x, e_j\rangle. \end{align*} $$

Theorem (adjoint map in Hilbert spaces) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

For any $T\in{}$$\mathcal{B}(H)$, there exists a unique function $T^\ast: H\to H$ satisfying $$ \begin{align*} \langle Tx, y\rangle = \langle x, T^\ast y\rangle \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in H. \end{align*} $$ Moreover $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(H)$.

$T^\ast$ is called the adjoint of $T$.

Proof

  • Under the identification $H = H^\ast$ via the Riesz representation, the adjoint and the dual are the same. (Commutative diagram)

    Therefore $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is compact iff its adjoint $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is compact. (Proof)

  • If $A$ is the matrix of $T$ with respect to some orthonormal basis, then the matrix of $T^\ast$ is $\overline{A^T}$.

  • $(λT + μS)^\ast = \overline{λ} T^\ast + \overline{μ} S^\ast$.

  • $(ST)^\ast = T^\ast S^\ast$.

  • $T^{\ast\ast} = T$.

  • $\|T^\ast\| = \|T\|$. (Proof)

  • $T\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is invertible iff $T^\ast\in \mathcal{B}(X)$ is invertible, and $(T^\ast)\inv = (T\inv)^\ast$. (Proof)

Definition (Hermitian, self-adjoint operator) open_in_new

Let $V$ be an inner product space.

$\alpha\in \mathcal{L}(V,V)$ is said to be Hermitian or self-adjoint if $\alpha$ is an adjoint map to itself.

Examples of Hermitian operators open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

  • Orthogonal projection from $H$ to any closed subspace is Hermitian. (Proof)

  • For any $T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$, $T^\ast T$ is Hermitian. Moreover $\|T^\ast T\| = \|T\|^2$. (Proof)

Proposition (decomposing complex bounded linear map into Hermitian maps) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

For any $T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$, there exists unique Hermitian operators $T_1, T_2$ such that $T = T_1 + iT_2$, which are given by $$ \begin{align*} T_1 = \frac{1}{2}(T + T^\ast), \qquad T_2 = \frac{1}{2i}(T - T^\ast). \end{align*} $$

Proof

Definition (unitary operator) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

$T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$ is a unitary operator if equivalently

  1. $T^\ast$$T = T T^\ast = \id_H$;

  2. $T$ is invertible and $T\inv = T^\ast$.

$T^\ast T = \id_H$ is not sufficient, e.g. the adjoint of the right shift $R: l_2 \to l_2: e_i \mapsto e_{i+1}$ is the left shift $R^\ast: e_1 \mapsto 0, e_{i+1} \mapsto i_{i}$ with $R^\ast R = \id_{l_2}$, but $R$ is not invertible.

Proposition (unitary iff surjective and isometric) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

$T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$ is unitary iff $T$ is surjective and $T$ is isometric (i.e. $\|Tx\| = \|x\|$ for all $x\in H$).

Proof

Definition (normal operator) open_in_new

Let $H$ be a Hilbert space.

$T\in \mathcal{B}(H)$ is said to be normal if $T^\ast$$T = TT^\ast$.
