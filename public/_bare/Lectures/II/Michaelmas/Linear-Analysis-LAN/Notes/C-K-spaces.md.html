Definition (normal topological space) open_in_new

A topological space $X$ is said to be normal if whenever $A, B$ are disjoint closed sets in $X$, there exists disjoint open sets $U, V$ such that $A \subseteq U$ and $B \subseteq V$.

Equivalently, whenever $A$ is a closed set and $U$ is an open set such that $A \subseteq U$, there exists a closed set $B$ and an open set $V$ such that $A \subseteq V \subseteq B \subseteq U$.

Proposition (examples of normal spaces) open_in_new

  • Any metric space is normal.

  • Any compact Hausdorff space is normal.

Proof

Theorem (Urysohn's lemma) open_in_new

Let $X$ be a normal topological space.

For any disjoint closed sets $A,B\subseteq X$, there exists a continuous function $f: X \to [0,1]$ such that $A \subseteq f\inv(0)$ and $B \subseteq f\inv(1)$.

Proof

Theorem (Tietze's extension theorem) open_in_new

Let $K$ be a normal topological space.

If $L$ is a closed subspace of $K$, then for any $g\in C_b(L)$, there is $f\in C_b(K)$ such that $f|_L = g$ and $\|f\|_\infty = \|g\|_\infty$.

Proof

Definition (algebra) open_in_new

An algebra over a field $\mathbb{F}$ is a vector space $A$ over $\mathbb{F}$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot: A \times A \to A$ satisfying, for all $x,y,z\in A$, $λ\in F$,

  1. associativity: $(xy)z = x(yz)$;

  2. left distributivity: $x(y+z) = xy + xz$;

  3. right distributivity: $(x+y)z = xz + yz$;

  4. compatibility with scalars: $x(λy) = (λx)y = λ(xy)$.

$A$ is a unital algebra if there exists an element $1\in A$ such that $1\cdot x = x = x \cdot 1$ for all $x\in A$. In which case, an element $x\in A$ is said to be invertible if there exists $y\in A$ such that $xy = yx = 1$.

$A$ is a commutative algebra if $xy = yx$ for all $x,y\in A$.

Definition (subalgebra) open_in_new

Let $A$ be an algebra.

A subset $B\subseteq A$ is a subalgebra of $A$ if it is a vector subspace of $A$ and $xy\in B$ for all $x,y\in B$.

$B$ is a unital subalgebra if in addition, $1\in B$.

Definition (normed algebra, Banach algebra) open_in_new

A normed algebra $A$ is a normed vector space that is also an algebra and satisfies $$\begin{align*} \|xy\| \leq \|x\| \|y\| \qquad \text{for all }x,y\in A. \end{align*} $$

$A$ is unital if moreover $A$ is a unital algebra with $\|1\| = 1$.

$A$ is said to be a Banach algebra if $A$ is complete.

Definition (separates points, strongly separates points) open_in_new

Let $S$ be a set of functions $K \to \C$.

$S$ is said to separate points of $K$ if for all $x,y\in K$ distinct, there exists $f\in S$ such that $f(x) \neq f(y)$.

$S$ is said to strongly separate points of $K$ if in addition, for all $x\in K$, there exists $f\in S$ such that $f(x) \neq 0$.

Theorem (Stone-Weierstrass) open_in_new

Let $K$ be a compact topological space.

  1. If $A$ is a subalgebra of $C^\R(K)$ that strongly separates points of $K$, then $A$ is dense in $C^\R(K)$.

  2. If $A$ is a subalgebra of $C^\C(K)$ that strongly separates points of $K$ and is closed under complex conjugation (i.e. if $f\in A$, then $\bar f\in A$), then $A$ is dense in $C^\C(K)$.

Definition (complex Fourier series) open_in_new

For an $L$-periodic function $f: \R\to\C$, define the complex Fourier series $$ \begin{align*} \sum_{n\in\Z} \hat f_n e^{2\pi in\theta/L} \end{align*} $$ where $$ \begin{align*} \hat f_n = \frac{1}{L} \int_{0}^{L} f(\theta) e^{-2\pi in\theta/L} \d \theta \end{align*} $$ and we call $\set{\hat f_n}$ the complex Fourier coefficients of $f$.

We write $$ \begin{align*} f(\theta) \sim \sum_{n\in\Z} \hat f_n e^{2\pi in\theta/L} \end{align*} $$ to mean the series on the right is the complex Fourier series of the $L$-periodic function on the left.

Proposition (Dirichlet kernel) open_in_new

Define the Dirichlet kernel $$ \begin{align*} D_N(t) := \sum_{n=-N}^{N} e^{int} = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{\sin(N + \frac{1}{2})t}{\sin \frac{1}{2}t}, &t\not\in 2π\Z, \\ 2N+1, &t\in 2π\Z \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ so that writing $\hat f_n = \frac{1}{2π} \int_{-π}^{π} f(t)e^{-int} \d t$ for the Fourier coefficients, $$ \begin{align*} S_N(f) := \sum_{n=-N}^{N} \hat f_n e^{int} = \frac{1}{2π}\int_{-π}^{π} f(s) D_N(t-s) \d s \equiv f * D_N \end{align*} $$ where $f\in C(\mathbb{T})$ and $\mathbb{T} = \R/\Z \isom \sb{z\in \C}{|z|=1}$.

Then $$ \begin{align*} T_N: C(\mathbb{T}) &\to \C \\ f & \mapsto S_N(f)(0) = \frac{1}{2π} \int_{-π}^{π} f(s) D_N(-s) \d s \end{align*} $$ is a bounded linear functional, and $$ \begin{align*} \|T_N\| = \frac{1}{2π}\int_{-π}^{π} |D_N(s)| \d s \to \infty. \end{align*} $$

Proof

Therefore by the principle of uniform boundedness, there exists $f\in C(\mathbb{T})$ such that $(T_N(f))_{N\geq 1}$ does not converge.

Proposition (Fejér kernel) open_in_new

Define the Fejér kernel $$ \begin{align*} K_N(t) := \frac{1}{N+1} \sum_{n=0}^{N} D_n(t) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\frac{1}{N+1} \left(\frac{\sin \frac{N+1}{2}t}{\sin \frac{1}{2}t}\right)^2, &t\not\in 2π\Z, \\ N+1, &t\in 2π\Z \end{cases} \end{align*} $$ where $D_n$ is the $n$-th Dirichlet kernel, and write for $f\in C(\mathbb{T})$ $$ \begin{align*} σ_N(f) := f * K_N = \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N} S_n(f) = \frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{N} \sum_{k=-n}^{n} \hat f_k e^{ikt}. \end{align*} $$

Then $K_N$ is an approximate identity, so $$ \begin{align*} σ_N(f) \tto f \qquad \text{in $C(\mathbb{T})$}. \end{align*} $$

Proof

This demonstrates the Stone-Weierstrass theorem: the trigonometric polynomials $\set{\sum_{n=-N}^{N} a_n e^{int}}$ is dense in $C(\mathbb{T})$.

Proposition (Fourier transform of periodic functions) open_in_new

Taking the Fourier coefficients gives rise to a well-defined bounded linear map $$ \begin{align*} \mathcal{F}: C(\mathbb{T}) &\to c_0(\Z) := \sb{\text{sequence $(a_n)_{n\in \Z}$ in $\C$}}{a_n\to 0 \text{ as $|n|\to \infty$}} \\ f &\mapsto (\hat f_n)_{n\in \Z}, \end{align*} $$ known as the Fourier transform. The fact $\mathcal{F}(f) \in c_0(\Z)$ is known as the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma.

It has norm $\|\mathcal{F}\| = 1$. $\mathcal{F}$ is injective but not surjective.

Proof

Theorem (Arzelà-Ascoli) open_in_new

Let $K$ be a compact topological space.

A subset $\mathcal{F} \subseteq C(K)$ is relatively compact iff

  1. $\mathcal{F}$ is uniformly bounded: $\sup_{f\in \mathcal{F}} \|f\|_\infty < \infty$; and

  2. $\mathcal{F}$ is equicontinuous: for all $ε>0$, $x\in K$, there exists a neighbourhood $U$ of $x$ such that for all $f\in \mathcal{F}, y\in U$, we have $|f(y) - f(x)| < ε$.
